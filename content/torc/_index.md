Truth or Consequences sits where the Rio Grande widens toward Caballo
Lake, on I-25 between Socorro and Las Cruces — an unlikely name for an
unlikely little climbing county, and the ground we call home. The rock
around it falls into two families, in two directions. East of the
river and across the reservoir, the **Caballo Mountains** carry
limestone: Mud Mountain rising directly over the city, and the taller,
wilder cliffs above Caballo Lake around the Bat Cave. West and north
the rock turns volcanic — the Eocene andesite of Percha Creek in the
foothills of the **Black Range** near Hillsboro, and the Oligocene
rhyolite of Luna Park and Red Rock Arroyo, which turn out to sit not in
the Black Range at all but on the southern flank of the **San Mateo
Mountains**, in Cibola National Forest.

## The ground itself

That two-family split is not a coincidence of where people happened to
bolt. It is the map of a collision between an old sea floor and a young
rift, and it is worth carrying in your head as you drive the county.

Truth or Consequences sits in the **Palomas Basin of the Rio Grande
rift**, on Pliocene–Pleistocene basin fill of the **Palomas Formation** —
the same aquifer the hot-springs wells are drilled into, where
intersecting faults let water up from depth fast enough to arrive at
98–115 °F. The rift began pulling this country apart around 36 Ma in the
south, peaked between 16 and 10 Ma, and has not stopped; every fault
block you can see from town is part of that machinery.

To the **east**, the **Caballo Mountains** are the rift's flank uplift:
a block tilted east, its broken western edge exposing about three and a
half miles of section — every geologic period but the Triassic and
Jurassic — from Proterozoic granite at the bottom through Cambrian
sandstone to grey Paleozoic carbonate cliffs on the crest. That is where
the county's limestone comes from, and where the Bat Cave is. To the
**northwest**, the **Mud Springs Mountains** are a smaller, northeast-
tilted block of the same drowned platform, missing its Silurian and
Mississippian; Mud Mountain is the summit at its southern end. To the
**west and north**, everything is Cenozoic volcanic rock: **Eocene
Rubio Peak andesite** at Hillsboro, under the **Kneeling Nun Tuff** that
the Emory caldera blew across the Black Range at about 35 Ma; and, up
past Monticello, **Oligocene rhyolite ash-flow tuff** off the **Nogal
Canyon caldera** at Luna Park and Red Rock Arroyo.

So the rule of thumb — limestone east of the river, volcanic west and
north — is geologically exact. The rift tore open along the western edge
of an old Paleozoic carbonate platform, tilted its blocks up on the east
side, and buried everything to the west under ash. Each crag page below
carries the detail: what unit it is, how sure we are, and what that
means for what your hands find.

This is winter and shoulder-season country. TorC sits low and hot in
summer, and the limestone crags are built for it: Mud Mountain's best
wall faces due south, Caballo Lake's cliffs face west into the
afternoon sun, and the desert cools them from October through April
while the high country above Hillsboro and Kingston presumably takes
the summer overflow — though that is inference more than record.
Sierra County's own tourism page puts it plainly: a few different
crags, all within an hour of town.

Mountain Project carries a Truth or Consequences Area with five named
sub-areas and a scatter of route pages beneath them, but the
documentation is thin — grades, lengths, star ratings and first-ascent
credit are missing for nearly every route we have found so far. What
follows in this part is being rebuilt from that scaffold and from our
own fieldwork, not copied from a finished guide, and we say so plainly
wherever a gap remains rather than guessing to fill it.

One caution belongs up front. The approach to the Bat Cave, above
Caballo Lake, passes active or historic mine claims, and Mountain
Project itself warns visitors to stay on public land. Mining claims on
BLM ground are a real legal interest here, not a formality — respect
the boundary even where it is not obviously marked, and expect us to
keep tightening this guidance as the access picture around every crag
in the county gets confirmed.
