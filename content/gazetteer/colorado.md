Colorado is one of the two or three densest climbing states in the
country — one widely cited count puts it at roughly 39,000 documented
routes — and it earns that density from geological range as much as
volume. The Front Range stacks Boulder's gritty sandstone slabs,
Eldorado Canyon's runout trad classics, and the Flatirons' alpine-scale
slabs within a half-hour of a million people, backed by the granite
domes of the South Platte and the volcanic porphyry of the Golden
mesas. West over the Divide the rock turns to limestone at Rifle and
Shelf Road, and to sandstone towers at Colorado National Monument and
the West End. The San Juans mix alpine granite, volcanic tuff, and
pocketed sport rock with a developing scene around Durango. The San
Luis Valley anchors the south with welded-tuff sport at Penitente
Canyon and the state's only Grade VI big-wall terrain at the Black
Canyon. Season and elevation do the organizing: low-desert crags run
best fall through spring, while the high granite of Independence Pass
and Mount Evans gets a short subalpine summer squeezed between snow.

Access is a live, moving target almost everywhere. Front Range crags
carry seasonal raptor-nesting closures (typically February–July) that
shift year to year with where the birds actually nest. Garden of the
Gods requires free climber registration and bans chalk outright. Land
managers worth tracking by area: Colorado State Parks, Boulder OSMP
and Jefferson County Open Space, USFS Arapaho/Roosevelt and Pike/San
Isabel National Forests, BLM, and the NPS units (Rocky Mountain, Black
Canyon of the Gunnison, Colorado National Monument).

## Royal Gorge — do not climb without authorization

Royal Gorge, historically home to lines up to 900 feet including the
Tombstone Wall classics, is **currently closed to unauthorized
climbing** under Cañon City municipal code. Access exists only with
the express permission of the Royal Gorge Company. Treat this as a
closed area, not a discreet objective.
