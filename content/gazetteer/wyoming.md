Wyoming is a state of extremes. Around Lander and in the Bighorn
Mountains, bone-white Madison limestone and Bighorn dolomite give up
pocketed, steep sport climbing that draws destination traffic every
summer. In the southeast corner, Sherman Granite erupts into the
wind-scoured domes of Vedauwoo, a wide-crack and offwidth proving
ground unlike almost anywhere else in the country. Devils Tower rises
alone out of the Black Hills grassland, a laccolithic plug fluted into
parallel cracks that has drawn crack climbers since 1937. To the west,
the Wind River Range and the Tetons carry some of the best alpine
granite in the Lower 48. Casper and Cody add canyon and gorge climbing
on granite and limestone respectively, and Cody's South Fork of the
Shoshone is one of the most storied ice climbing testing grounds in
North America. Public land is the rule almost everywhere, but enclaves
of private land are scattered through it, especially around the
Sweetwater Rocks, and access depends on respecting the boundary
between the two.

The season is short at altitude and long at the canyons: Fremont
Canyon and Guernsey's south-facing limestone climb through much of the
winter, while the Wind Rivers and the Snowy Range hold snow into July.
Raptor nesting closures, grazing-season road closures, and one
long-standing voluntary June closure at Devils Tower all shape when a
given wall is fair game.

## The rock of Wyoming

New Mexico's geologic story is chemistry — what erupted, what
precipitated. Wyoming's is **architecture**. A piece of some of the
oldest continental crust on Earth was buried under a mile and a half of
shallow-sea limestone and desert sandstone, then shoved bodily upward in
discrete blocks with the sedimentary cover peeling off their flanks,
then buried again in its own debris, then dug back out. Almost every
Wyoming crag is explained by where it sits in that sequence: **on the
exhumed Archean core of an uplift, on the tilted Paleozoic carbonate
wrapped around that core, or on something young punched through the pile
afterwards.**

The basement is the **Wyoming Province**, 3.5 to 2.5 billion years old,
exposed wherever a Laramide uplift has stripped its cover — the
Beartooths, the Bighorns, the Wind Rivers, the Tetons, the Laramie
Range, the Granite Mountains. In the southeast corner the state stops
being Archean: the **Cheyenne belt** is the suture where the
Paleoproterozoic **Yavapai** province — the same one that floors New
Mexico — was welded on at 1.78–1.74 Ga. Across that seam, at about
1.43 Ga, the **Sherman batholith** and the Laramie anorthosite complex
intruded, part of the same continent-wide 1.4 Ga pulse that gave New
Mexico the Sandia pluton. That granite is Vedauwoo.

Above the basement is a gap of roughly two billion years. In the
Bighorns the Middle Cambrian **Flathead Sandstone** lies directly on
deeply weathered 2.95 Ga granite, fossil grus and paleosol preserved at
the contact — one of the best places on Earth to put a hand on both
sides of the **Great Unconformity**. Then the platform: Flathead, Gros
Ventre, Gallatin, the thin but real **Bighorn Dolomite**, Darby, the
**Madison Limestone**, the red **Amsden**, and the eolian **Tensleep
Sandstone** at the rim. Two facts from that list run through the whole
chapter. **The Madison is the reason Wyoming has sport climbing** — it
is thick, massive, mechanically strong and it takes solution features.
And **the Bighorn Dolomite is genuinely thin**, 82 to 138 feet in the
Bighorn Mountains, which is a problem for the universal climbers' habit
of calling every carbonate crag in the state Bighorn Dolomite.

The **Laramide orogeny**, 75 to 55 Ma, is what made all of it
climbable — thick-skinned deformation driving basement blocks 3 to 20 km
up along faults dipping 25° to 40°, tilting the carbonate section off
their flanks into cliffs. The uplifts *are* the map of Wyoming climbing:
the Bighorns give Ten Sleep and the eastern canyons, the Wind Rivers
give the alpine granite and the Lander limestone, the Laramie Range
gives Vedauwoo, the Granite Mountains give Fremont Canyon and the
Sweetwater Rocks. Afterwards came Eocene magma in two very different
forms — the **Absaroka volcanic field** (53–43 Ma, 29,000 km³ of mostly
altered andesite) in the northwest, and a swarm of alkaline intrusions
into the flank of the Black Hills at ~49 Ma, one of which is Devils
Tower. The Tetons then got raised a second time by a normal fault that
started moving 13 to 7 Ma and has not stopped. And ice did the
sculpting: the **Pinedale glaciation** is named for the moraines on the
west side of the Wind Rivers.

Two Wyoming-specific things to carry. **The Great Unconformity is
climbable here** — at Shoshone Canyon and in Sinks and Fremont Canyons
you can boulder within a few metres of a two-billion-year gap. And
**freeze–thaw above 7,000 ft is the dominant active process on almost
every wall in the state**, which means alpine rockfall is overwhelmingly
a ledge-and-gully problem rather than a face problem: the clean lower
walls were plucked by ice, and the debris sits on the ledges above them.

## Lander area

Wyoming's sport-climbing capital and home to the International
Climbers' Festival every July, spread across a band of Paleozoic
carbonate in and above Sinks Canyon State Park, with Wild Iris —
higher, cooler, more remote — as the marquee crag.

Lander sits at the southeastern nose of the **Wind River Range**, and
the Laramide uplift of that range **tilted the whole Paleozoic section
off its flank**. So a drive up Sinks Canyon is a drive down the section
and back in time: Tensleep Sandstone at the mouth, then Madison
Limestone and Bighorn Dolomite through the middle, then Archean granite
at the top. Every crag here is a bench on that tilted stack, or the
basement beneath it — which is why one small town offers sandstone
cracks, pocketed carbonate and granite slabs within twenty minutes of
each other. The Sinks themselves are the carbonate's own plumbing: the
Popo Agie vanishes into a cavern in the Madison and takes over two hours
to travel the quarter-mile to the Rise, by dye test. And the Wild Iris
monos, whatever the local story says, are not gas bubbles — they are
dolomitisation porosity, selective dissolution and karst, which is why
pockets come in horizons and clusters rather than at random. Read the
band, not the wall.

## Ten Sleep Canyon

Everyone calls this Bighorn Dolomite, and the canyon walls say
otherwise. Forest Service interpretive signage gives the benches as
Tensleep Sandstone at the rim, the red Amsden recess, the Darwin
Sandstone ledge, then **gray cliffs of Madison Limestone** — with the
Bighorn Dolomite below that, at road level near Leigh Creek, whose upper
member is named for the creek. Both units are genuinely here. But the
Bighorn is 25 to 42 metres thick and the Madison is 490 to 700 feet, and
**a 250-foot crag cannot sit inside a 130-foot formation**. What saves
the climbers' usage is that the Wyoming Madison is itself heavily
dolomitised — so the *lithology* is right even where the formation name
is wrong. See [Part II](/corridor/wyoming/ten-sleep/) for the full
account, including how to read the canyon's gold, white and blue walls
as a rock-quality map.

## Devils Tower

Not basalt, and the distinction is not pedantry: **phonolite porphyry**,
about 49 Ma, one of a swarm of alkaline intrusions punched into the
flank of the rising Black Hills. Phonolite is silica-*under*saturated —
it carries nepheline where a granite would carry quartz — and it takes
its name from the ringing sound its plates make when struck, which any
climber who has tapped a suspect flake up there has heard. The magma
cooled slowly enough to produce the world's finest columnar jointing:
mostly five-sided columns, six to eight feet across at the base tapering
to four at the top, some running 590 feet unbroken. **The routes are the
joints** — a columnar body has no faces, only column flanks and the
partings between them, which is why the Tower is a crack climb from
fingers through offwidth to chimney and why the cracks are so uniformly
parallel-sided that cams work and nuts often don't. The USGS also
explains the standard warning about the last hundred feet: the joints
are smooth in the middle of the Tower but wavy near the top, where
**cross-fractures divide the columns into small irregular blocks**. And
the boulder field is not an ancient landform — as the soft sediments at
the base erode, exterior columns lose their lateral support and fall.

## Vedauwoo

Every complaint climbers make about Vedauwoo is a mineral property of
the **Sherman Granite**, 1.43 Ga, coarse and K-feldspar-porphyritic —
**54% of the rock is feldspar**, in crystals one to three centimetres
across. Feldspar cleaves on two planes at nearly 90°, so as it
disintegrates it breaks into blocky fragments with straight sharp edges
instead of rounding off the way quartz does. A rock that is half
centimetre-scale feldspar, coming apart grain by grain, is a rock
covered in small blades: it shreds skin, and it grips like nothing else.

The offwidths are not a local eccentricity either. A massive, monolithic
granite with a widely spaced east-northeast joint set does not give you
a hundred hairline seams; it gives you a few big splits through a big
block. And the famously flaring, insecure jams are a fossil of how these
tors formed: they weathered **underground**, groundwater rotting the
rock along fractures until the intact corestones were left to be exposed
later. The joint faces are chemically etched and rounded near the
surface, where water sat longest, and stay tight and square deep inside.
Jam deep, expect cams to walk outward in the flare, and tape everything.

## Fremont Canyon / Dome Rock (Casper area)

All three areas here sit on the **Granite Mountains / Sweetwater arch** —
a Laramide uplift that did something unusual: it collapsed, was buried
by its own basin fill, and was then dug back out in what Wyoming
geologists call the Great Exhumation. The rock is Archean, a 2.86 Ga
metamorphic complex intruded by a 2.55 Ga biotite granite. (Some local
material calls Independence Rock 1.5 billion years old; the weight of
the evidence is Archean, and that figure looks like a confusion with the
Sherman Granite two ranges east.)

Fremont Canyon is the North Platte cutting clean through to basement,
exposing the Great Unconformity in a gorge rather than on a mountain
front — and its walls were, until recently in geologic terms, the bed
and banks of that river. "**Water-worn**" is the word every source uses,
and it means abraded, polished and undercut: friction lower than a
normal granite crag, rounded crack lips that want gear placed deep, and
a base that is the river itself, which is why so many routes are
rappelled into from the rim.

## Wind River Range (alpine)

The biggest exposure of Archean crust in the state — roughly **80% of
the range is late Archean granitoid**, dominated by the Bridger, Louis
Lake (~2.63 Ga) and Bears Ears (~2.5 Ga) batholiths. The single most
useful thing to know here is that **the range is two rocks, north and
south**. The southern Winds — Cirque of the Towers, Deep Lake, Haystack,
Big Sandy — are homogeneous granitoid: uniform, predictable,
well-jointed, and the Cirque's shape is simply that joint pattern made
visible by ice plucking along it. The northern Winds — Titcomb, Gannett,
Dinwoody — are **gneiss with granite intruded into it**, heterogeneous
at outcrop scale. The American Alpine Club's own reconnaissance puts it
memorably: if Cirque rock is vanilla ice cream in its uniformity,
Titcomb rock is many-flavoured. Expect the quality to change within a
pitch up there, and expect the named hazard to be the ledges and talus
rather than the routes.

## Tetons (alpine rock)

*Young mountains of very old rock.* The basement is about 2.7 Ga —
complexly deformed layered gneiss, the Webb Canyon Gneiss, and the
non-foliated **Mount Owen Quartz Monzonite** at ~2.55 Ga, all cut by
undeformed **diabase dikes** including the Black Dike that stands 200
feet proud of Mount Moran's face. The range itself is young: a normal
fault on its east side began slipping 13 to 7 Ma, has moved most in the
last 2 Ma, and has put 6 to 9 km of relative displacement between the
range block and Jackson Hole. Glaciers then cut Cascade, Death and
Garnet canyons as troughs, plucking their walls clean along joints.

Which explains the honest local summary — rock quality ranging from
dreamy to nightmare within a single pitch. An interlayered gneiss
complex intruded by a discordant pluton and cut by dikes has four or
five different rocks on one face, and the classic routes wander because
the good rock wanders. **The Mount Owen Quartz Monzonite is the good
rock**: massive, uniform, clean cracks and reliable gear. The layered
gneiss gives the incut holds that make 5.4 to 5.8 possible on a peak
that size — and the detached flakes and loose ledge blocks. One footnote
for the collectors: the **Death Canyon Limestone** is a formally named
member of the Cambrian Gros Ventre Formation, named for the canyon
climbers climb in.

## Jackson area crags

**This is a different tectonic province from everywhere else in this
chapter.** Everything east of Jackson is Laramide, basement-cored,
thick-skinned. The Hoback and Snake River canyons are in the **Sevier
fold-and-thrust belt** — thin-skinned and older, where whole sheets of
Paleozoic rock were shoved eastward over Mesozoic rock. The Absaroka
thrust puts Mississippian Madison Limestone on top of Cretaceous strata,
and these crags ride that upper plate. Rodeo Wall's diagnostic
description — rock that "seems to alternate between either polished or
super-sharp" — is the signature of **thin-bedded** carbonate: hard
fine-grained beds polish, more fossiliferous beds weather to razor
edges. So holds come in horizontal bands because the beds do, and a bolt
is only as good as the bed it is in.

## Cody area (limestone sport + ice heritage)

Shoshone Canyon is the whole of Wyoming geology in three miles. The
North Fork has cut the **Rattlesnake Mountain anticline**, exposing
Archean granite and granitic gneiss more than 2.7 billion years old
beneath 515-million-year-old Flathead Sandstone — the Great Unconformity
on glorious display from the dam — with the tilted Paleozoic section
above. That sorts the climbing: **the limestone sport crags are on the
north side of the road on Rattlesnake Mountain, the granite trad areas
are at the west end near the tunnels and down by the river**, and the
bouldering is on sandstone. Same division as Sinks Canyon and for the
same reason — crystalline rock cracks, carbonate pockets. Expect the
limestone here to have *dipping* bedding, since it sits on a steeply
tilted anticline limb: holds and ledges run at an angle across the wall.

The South Fork's ice is the best "why does this area exist" story in the
state. The Absaroka volcanic pile is thousands of feet of flat-lying,
alternating volcaniclastic units, and it erodes into a stepped cliff
profile — **every resistant band is a lip, every lip is a waterfall, and
every waterfall in a cold dry Wyoming winter is an ice route.** The
densest concentration of frozen waterfalls in the continental US exists
because a volcanic pile is layered. The corollary is the hazard: the
rock behind the ice is breccia that locals call kitty litter, so rock
protection is poor to useless and warm spells bring the cliff down.

## Sheridan / Bighorns (Story area, Eastern Bighorns)

## Sweetwater Rocks / Lankin Dome

Archean granite, 2.6 to 2.8 Ga, standing as **exfoliated domes out of a
plain of young sediment** — which is the Great Exhumation in one image,
since that sediment is the stuff that used to bury them. The climbing
follows from the landform: an exfoliation dome's only fractures are
sheeting joints parallel to its surface, and those are underfoot rather
than in front of you, so there is nothing to put gear in between them.
Hence a seven-pitch 5.5-to-5.9+ classic described as runout slab with
crystal holds. The crystals are the medium — with no cracks and no
pockets, the holds are phenocrysts and the differential weathering
around them. Devils Gate nearby is a **water gap**: the Sweetwater held
its course while the granite ridge rose across it, and sawed through.

## Areas checked but not confirmed as climbing destinations

The geology pass could not turn any of these three into climbing areas.
It could explain two of them.

- **Baggot Rocks** (Carbon County) — still no evidence of an established
  route inventory; every source treats it as a fishing-access and
  geology site. But the rock now has a name and a date, and a nice one:
  the **Baggot Rocks Granite**, 2340 ± 50 Ma, of which this outcrop is
  the **type locality**. Massive to foliated granite cut by diabase
  dikes and pegmatite, forming prominent rounded outcrops — tor-and-dome
  habit, so exfoliation slabs and crystal faces with few continuous
  cracks if anyone ever develops it.
- **Glendo State Park** — the absence is consistent with the rock. This
  is gently dipping, cherty, thin-bedded Mississippian–Pennsylvanian
  carbonate (the Guernsey and Hartville Formations, source of the agate
  and jasper collected around the reservoir) over an Archean core: a
  **ledge-and-slope landscape, not a cliff landscape**. Nothing here is
  the massive 500-foot cliff-former the Madison is on the Bighorn rim.
- **Flaming Gorge / Green River (WY side)** — here the absence of
  climbing is a rock-quality fact rather than a research gap. Firehole
  Canyon's cliffs and spires are **Mancos Shale capped by Mesaverde
  sandstone**, with bits of the lacustrine Green River Formation: soft
  shale under thin ledge-forming sandstone erodes into hoodoos and
  badlands. Spectacular, structurally rotten. **Hoodoo country, not crag
  country.**
