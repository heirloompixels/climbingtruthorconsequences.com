Lander is a small city of under 8,000 wedged against the southern
Wind River Range, bordering the Wind River Indian Reservation, and it
is the closest thing this corridor has to a purpose-built climbing
town. Two areas anchor it: **Sinks Canyon**, which starts at the edge
of town and runs southwest through three distinct rock zones —
desert sandstone low down, limestone/dolomite through the middle,
granite up top — and **Wild Iris**, a high, sun-baked dolomite
complex about thirty minutes further southwest near South Pass,
sitting at roughly 9,000 feet. Newer satellite crags — Fossil Hill,
the Sweat Lodge, remote Wolf Point — round out the current guidebook.

The town's climbing identity is really one man's legacy. Todd Skinner
moved to Lander in 1989–90 after hearing about the dolomite nearby,
began developing what became Wild Iris, and opened Wild Iris Mountain
Sports with Amy Skinner in 1990 to support the community growing
around it. In the early 1990s Skinner and friends founded the
International Climbers' Festival, still running today and billed as
the longest-continuously-running climbers' festival in the country.
Skinner died in a rappelling accident on Yosemite's Leaning Tower in
2006; the shop and the festival he started both carry on. Lander is
also international headquarters for NOLS, giving the town a second,
older outdoor-education identity that runs alongside its climbing
one.

## The rock

Lander sits at the southeastern nose of the **Wind River Range**, a
Laramide basement-cored uplift whose Archean core was driven northeast
over the Wind River Basin — cooling underway by 75 Ma, fastest between
60 and 57. Everything about the local climbing follows from that one
fact, because it **tilted the whole Paleozoic section off the flank of
the range**. Drive up Sinks Canyon and you drive down the section and
back in time: within a few miles the rock goes from sandstone to
carbonate to granite. Every crag around town is a bench on that tilted
stack, or the basement beneath it.

At Sinks the order, top of the section to bottom, is **Tensleep
Sandstone** (exposed from the canyon mouth), **Amsden**, **Madison
Limestone**, **Bighorn Dolomite**, **Gallatin Limestone**, **Gros Ventre
Shale**, **Flathead Sandstone**, and then Precambrian granite and banded
iron formation at the head of the canyon. One number to distrust: the
local interpretive material calls that granite "over 4 billion years
old." It is not. The oldest dated rock in the Wyoming Province is 3.5
Ga, the Wind River batholiths are 2.63 Ga (Louis Lake) and about 2.5 Ga
(Bears Ears), and nothing anywhere on Earth is reliably older than about
4.03 Ga.

### Three rocks in one canyon

**The sandstone** low down is the Pennsylvanian **Tensleep** — eolian
dune sand, cross-bedded, giving crack systems along joints and flakes
parallel to the cross-beds. "Sandy sandstone" is the climbers' word for
it, and it is a warning: eolian sandstone is quartz grains in a small
volume of cement, so give it a day or more after rain before you load a
hold or a piece of gear.

**The carbonate** through the middle is the Madison and the Bighorn, and
it is where Killer Cave, Scud Wall, The Wilds and the rest are. It gives
the pockets, the caves, the steep bulges — and the seepage, because
carbonate drains *through* itself and a limestone crag weeps for days
after the sagebrush is dry. Killer Cave, incidentally, is a genuine
solution feature rather than a metaphor, and its shelter is a real
advantage in a canyon that collects afternoon thunderstorms. The Sinks
themselves are the same plumbing at full scale: the Middle Fork of the
Popo Agie vanishes into a cavern in the Madison and reappears at the
Rise a quarter-mile down-canyon — taking **over two hours** to travel
it, by dye test.

**The granite** at the top is Archean granitoid of the Wind River
batholith complex, most plausibly the 2.63 Ga Louis Lake batholith,
though no source names the pluton at the crag. Rock that has been
weathering for two and a half billion years gives rounded crystals,
gritty friction, grus, and flakes that ring hollow.

### Wild Iris, and where the monos come from

Wild Iris is **Bighorn Dolomite** — bone-white, massive, siliceous,
Middle-to-Late Ordovician, the "miles of bone-white Bighorn Dolomite"
that line the southern Wind Rivers west of town. The formation's middle
massive member here is under 150 feet thick, which is exactly the right
scale for these walls. (Our own earlier notes filed Wild Iris under the
Madison Group; no source we could reach supports that, and the local
sources say Bighorn.)

Then the pockets. Stand at the base and you can find 150 monos on any
section of cliff, and the local story is that they are gas bubbles.
**They are not** — this is sedimentary rock, and the real mechanism
explains how the crag behaves. Dolomitization shrinks the rock's bulk
volume by about 13% as small magnesium ions replace larger calcium ones,
opening intercrystalline porosity and giving crystalline dolomite its
sugary texture; fossils, ooids and burrow fills then dissolve
preferentially into vugs; and karst enlarges joints and partings into
cavities. So pockets sit in **horizons and clusters aligned on the
fabric of the rock** — read the band, not the wall. Their rims are
freshly dissolved crystal terminations, which is why new pockets so
often have that razor lip. And a sugary, porous dolomite is strong in
compression and weak in tension at its surface: **pocket rims break,
whole pockets rarely do.** Bolts hold well in the massive siliceous rock
and badly in a vuggy band, which is why glue-ins have become the local
norm.

Aspect explains the calendar. Wild Iris faces south at about 9,000 feet,
so it is the summer crag; Sinks, 1,500 feet lower and minutes from town,
takes the shoulders. Baldwin Creek's dolomite faces due south and bakes
in July but climbs the cold edges of the year — when the road is open,
since it closes in winter for elk habitat. And the Sweat Lodge is worth
a geologist's second look: it is a **massive dolomite flake that toppled
off the cliff and wedged against it**, forming an A-frame you climb
inside. A carbonate cliff fails along its vertical joints, and where the
slab leans instead of shattering you get exactly that. Two cautions the
guidebooks do not print: a leaning flake is held up by friction and
geometry rather than by attachment, and the floor inside it is the
debris cone from its own collapse.

## Season & logistics

Sinks Canyon, lower and closer to town, climbs earlier and later in
the year than Wild Iris; Wild Iris itself runs roughly June through
October at 9,000 feet, with pleasant but changeable summer weather —
plan around chasing shade on its sun-facing walls. Combined, area
marketing claims some 320 climbable days a year. **Lander City Park**
offers free camping in town for up to three nights, 28 tent sites and
7 RV sites, walking distance to downtown. **Wild Iris Campground**
(Shoshone National Forest) sits near the crag and began charging
$10/night in 2024 after years of being free. Fossil Hill carries a
voluntary raptor closure roughly June 9–August 15; check current
dates with Wild Iris Mountain Sports (166 Main Street), the town's
climbing-shop hub for gear and conditions.

## The book to buy

**Steve Bechtel et al., *Lander Rock Climbs 2023*** (Climb Strong,
1st ed.) — the current standard guidebook, 304 pages covering 1,700+
routes from 5.2 to 5.15a across Sinks Canyon, Wild Iris, Fossil Hill,
the Sweat Lodge and more; author credit varies slightly across
retailers and is worth confirming on the copy in hand. Pair it with
Mountain Project's
[Lander Area](https://www.mountainproject.com/area/110136004/lander-area)
pages for current beta, and check
[Wild Iris Mountain Sports](https://www.wildirisclimbing.com/area-conditions)
for seasonal closures and the
[Shoshone National Forest](https://www.fs.usda.gov/r02/shoshone/recreation/wild-iris-campground)
for campground fees before you go.
