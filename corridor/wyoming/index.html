Wyoming

Dolomite and limestone heartland — Lander and Ten Sleep.

Wyoming is the corridor’s dolomite and limestone heartland, and its best-documented ground: Lander built a town’s identity around Todd Skinner’s Wild Iris, and Ten Sleep Canyon grew from a local secret into an international destination — and into one of American sport climbing’s hardest ethics fights. Come for the pocketed stone; read the access history before you clip a bolt.

Both areas sit on the same arrangement of rock: a Laramide uplift that raised an Archean core and tilted the Paleozoic carbonate section off its flanks into cliffs. Both are climbed on Madison Limestone and Bighorn Dolomite — and at both, climbers call all of it dolomite, which is right about the rock and wrong about the formation. The pages explain the difference, and where those famous pockets actually come from.

  1. Ten Sleep Canyon 5.6–5.14, with named moderate zones at Metropolis and Leigh Creek Bighorn dolomite pockets and edges above a Wyoming ranch town — one of American sport climbing's fastest rises, and one of its hardest ethics fights.
  2. Lander 5.4–5.15a across the area; moderates concentrated 5.8–5.10d Wyoming's climbing town — sandstone-to-granite Sinks Canyon at the doorstep, bulletproof Wild Iris dolomite thirty minutes up the road.
