Tres Piedras

Precambrian granite domes above the Rio Grande gorge country — slabs, splitter cracks and a serious bouldering scene, 30 minutes from Taos.

Rock
Granite — properly an orthogneiss: squeezed alkali feldspar granite
Formation
Tres Piedras Orthogneiss
Age
Paleoproterozoic, 1693 Ma (U-Pb)
Style
Trad — friction slabs, splitter cracks; some bolted faces
Grades
5.4–5.13
Season
Fall through spring; summer runs hot on exposed faces
Approach
Half-mile dirt road from US 64, then a short walk
Land
USFS — Carson National Forest, Tres Piedras Ranger District

Tres Piedras — “Three Rocks” — is a scatter of granite domes on the southeastern shoulder of the Tusas Mountains, where that range gives out into the Rio Grande valley thirty miles west-northwest of Taos. The stone is old even by granite standards: the Tres Piedras Orthogneiss, a Precambrian pluton dated to roughly 1.7 billion years, weathered into six named formations — North, Middle, South, West, Far and Mosaic Rock — climbing to about 200 feet at Mosaic’s south face, the tallest and most popular of the group. It is a trad climber’s crag first: friction slabs, flakes and splitter cracks, with some bolted mixed lines on the steeper faces, and a bouldering scene developed enough that a dedicated Mountain Project sub-area exists for it alone — regulars joke that on a good weekend there are as many crashpads scattered below the domes as ropes hung above them.

Land here is mostly Carson National Forest, with one small private corner in the southwest that the landowner has historically tolerated climbing on, waiver requested — worth confirming before you rely on it. A raptor closure runs roughly mid-February through late May.

The rock

Climbers call Tres Piedras granite. A geologist calls it orthogneiss, and both are being fair: it is granite that has been squeezed. The Tres Piedras Orthogneiss is dated by U-Pb to 1693 Ma — a pluton intruded, on the best reading, as the Yavapai arc docked against the growing southwestern edge of the continent, and then deformed and foliated some quarter of a billion years later, probably during the 1.45 Ga granite event that also built the Sandias. It is pink to reddish-orange, faintly to obviously foliated, grain size half a millimetre to five, and modally about 42% quartz, 40% microcline and 12% albite — the composition of an alkali feldspar granite. That high quartz content, set in coarse feldspar, is where the friction comes from, and the friction is why this is a slab area.

The foliation is the thing to watch, because a foliated rock carries a built-in plane of weakness. Sheets and flakes part along it, which is generous — it is what gives Tres Piedras its flake cracks alongside the splitters — and it is also where the loose blocks come from. The other jointing that matters on a 200-foot dome is sheeting: unloading joints that open parallel to the dome’s surface as erosion takes the weight of the overburden off. That is the classic dome architecture, slabs and detached flakes rather than the columns and corners a lava crag gives you, and it is the same process that shaped Sugarloaf in the Organs six hours south.

The setting is the western shoulder of the Rio Grande rift: the Tusas Mountains give out here into the gorge country, and the rift’s flank uplift is what lifted this basement to 8,000 feet and stripped the cover off it. Drive twenty minutes east and you drop off Proterozoic granite onto the Servilleta Basalt of the Taos Plateau — 240 metres of Pliocene flood basalt that the Rio Grande has trenched. Two of New Mexico’s great climbing rocks, 1.7 billion years apart, inside a single tank of gas.

Season & logistics

High desert at roughly 8,000 feet: fall through spring is the window, with cool mornings and a chance of afternoon monsoon storms in summer. Dispersed camping is free on the surrounding Carson National Forest land; the nearest developed campground is at Hopewell Lake, about 20 miles further west on US 64. The Tres Piedras Ranger Station sits essentially at the turnoff for current conditions and fire restrictions.

The book to buy

Jay Foley, Taos Rock: Climbs & Boulders of Northern New Mexico (Sharp End Publishing, 2005) and Dennis Jackson, Rock Climbing New Mexico (FalconGuides, State Rock Climbing Series) are both described as covering Tres Piedras with essentially the same routes and photos — either will serve. Pair either with Mountain Project’s Tres Piedras area pages for current beta, and check with the Carson National Forest, Tres Piedras Ranger District for fire restrictions and road conditions before you go.
