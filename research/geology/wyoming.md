# The Geology of Wyoming's Climbing Areas

Research notes for *Climbing Truth or Consequences*. The Wyoming volume of
the same reference work as `new-mexico.md` and `montana.md`, and it exists
to answer the same single question for every crag in the book: **why is the
rock the way it is, and what does that mean under your hands and feet?**

---

## Research method and limitation (read before using this file)

This research was compiled almost entirely from **WebSearch result
snippets**. `WebFetch` was tested once at the start of this run — against
`www.wsgs.wyo.gov`, the Wyoming State Geological Survey — and was
**refused by the outbound network proxy** with `EGRESS_BLOCKED`. The two
sibling agents who wrote the New Mexico and Montana volumes tested five
further hosts between them (`en.wikipedia.org`, `geoinfo.nmt.edu`,
`pubs.usgs.gov`, `mbmg.mtech.edu`, `mrdata.usgs.gov`) with the same
result. **The block is total for practical purposes; one test was
enough.** That means:

- Every WSGS report, every USGS Professional Paper and Bulletin, every
  NPS geology page and every peer-reviewed paper cited below is cited
  **from the search engine's summary of it**, not from the document
  itself. Geology summarises unusually well — the snippets quote unit
  names, thicknesses and radiometric ages verbatim — but they are
  summaries.
- **No geologic map was read.** Every statement of the form "this crag is
  on unit X" that is not directly quoted from a source is marked
  `[inferred from regional geology — unverified]`, with the inference
  named.
- Mountain Project and theCrag remain unfetchable (the standing condition
  of this project); climbers' descriptions of rock below come from search
  snippets that quote those pages, from guide services, and from
  destination articles.
- No formation name, age, or stratigraphic assignment in this file was
  invented. Where sources disagree — and on Ten Sleep they disagree
  sharply — both are given and the disagreement is flagged rather than
  averaged.

Roughly 150 web searches went into this file. The single most valuable
follow-up, if direct fetching is ever restored, would be:

1. **WSGS geologic map of the Bighorn Basin** and the USGS Tensleep /
   Leigh Creek 7.5′ quadrangles — to settle, once and for all, whether
   the Ten Sleep Canyon crags are cut in **Madison Limestone** or in
   **Bighorn Dolomite**. The climbing literature says one thing and the
   canyon's own stratigraphic column says another (§3.2). This is the
   single most consequential unresolved question in the file.
2. **USGS Bulletin 1021-I**, Robinson, *Geology of Devils Tower National
   Monument* — the primary description of the columns, their taper and
   their cross-fractures, which is exactly what a crack climber needs.
3. The **WSGS 1:500,000 Geologic Map of Wyoming** (Love & Christiansen)
   for the Lander, Sinks Canyon and Wild Iris ground — to pin which
   Madison member each Lander crag sits in.
4. **USGS Professional Paper 1201-A** and the Absaroka Volcanic
   Supergroup mapping, for the Cody crags.

## Part 1 — The statewide framework

Wyoming is the best state in the union for a climber who wants to see
*structure*. New Mexico's story is chemistry — what erupted, what
precipitated. Wyoming's story is architecture: a piece of some of the
oldest continental crust on Earth, buried under a mile and a half of
shallow-sea limestone and desert sandstone, then shoved bodily upward in
discrete blocks with the sedimentary cover peeling off their flanks, then
buried again in its own debris, then dug back out. Almost every Wyoming
crag is explained by where it sits in that sequence: **on the exhumed
Archean core of an uplift, on the tilted Paleozoic carbonate wrapped
around the core, or on something young that was punched through the pile
afterwards.**

### 1.1 The Wyoming Province: 3.5–2.5 Ga

The basement of the state is the **Wyoming Province** (or Wyoming
craton), one of the ancient nuclei of North America — "a stable Archean
craton … characterized by ancient continental crust formed primarily
between **3.5 and 2.5 billion years ago**," underlying most of Wyoming
and reaching into Montana, Idaho and South Dakota. It is exposed where
Laramide uplifts have stripped their cover: the **Beartooths, the
Bighorns, the Wind Rivers, the Tetons, the Laramie Range, the Granite
Mountains/Sweetwater Rocks**.

The province divides into three subprovinces — oldest to youngest, the
**Montana metasedimentary province**, the **Beartooth–Bighorn magmatic
zone**, and the **Southern accreted terranes**. Rocks of the first two
run to **3.5 Ga**, with detrital zircons to **4.0 Ga** and Nd model ages
beyond 4.0 Ga; the oldest dated rock in the province is a trondhjemitic
gneiss with concordant U-Pb zircon ages of **3.5 Ga**. Cratonization of
the northern subprovinces was complete by about **3.0–2.8 Ga**; crustal
growth continued on the southern margin by continental-arc magmatism and
terrane accretion at **2.68 Ga and younger**.

Two numbers to carry into the crag sections: the **Bighorn batholith**
granite that the Cambrian sea washed over is dated **2950 ± 5 Ma**, and
the layered gneiss of the Teton Range is commonly given as **~2.7 Ga**.

**What a climber gets from this:** the Wind River alpine granite, the
Teton gneiss and granite, the domes of the Sweetwater Rocks, the upper
Sinks Canyon granite, Fremont Canyon's gorge walls, and — indirectly —
every carbonate crag in the state, because it was these blocks rising
that tilted the limestone into cliffs.

### 1.2 The Cheyenne belt: where Wyoming stops being Archean

Southeastern Wyoming is not Wyoming Province. The **Cheyenne belt** is
"the tectonic suture zone between the Archean-age Wyoming craton to the
north and the Paleoproterozoic-age **Yavapai province** to the south," a
major shear zone separating Archean rocks from **1800–1600 Ma**
gneisses, formed during the **Medicine Bow orogeny, 1.78–1.74 Ga**, when
island arcs collided with the craton. Gravity profiles across it in the
Sierra Madre, Park Range and Laramie Range show a 50–100 mgal south-side
drop, meaning the Proterozoic crust south of the belt is more felsic
and/or thicker than the Archean crust north of it.

This is the same Yavapai province that floors New Mexico (see
`new-mexico.md` §1.1) — the two volumes of this reference work meet at
the Cheyenne belt. It matters for Vedauwoo and the Snowy Range, which sit
on or near it.

### 1.3 The 1.4 Ga event: Sherman Granite and the Laramie anorthosite

Around **1.44–1.43 Ga**, a distinct magmatic pulse — the same
continent-wide "anorogenic" 1.4 Ga event that gave New Mexico the Sandia
pluton — intruded southeastern Wyoming as two coeval bodies:

- the **Sherman batholith**, dated **1438–1431 Ma**, a coarse
  K-feldspar-porphyritic granite; and
- the **Laramie anorthosite complex (LAC)**, **1436–1431 Ma**, with five
  monzonitic-to-granitic A-type plutons intruded at 1.43 Ga, the youngest
  being the Red Mountain pluton.

The Sherman Granite intruded across older crust — sources describe it
cutting "a series of older (1700 Ma) metamorphic rocks" and intruding
"Paleoproterozoic crust of the Yavapai Province," while other work
records Archean rocks in the southern Laramie Range intruded at ~1.43 Ga
by both the LAC and the Sherman. In other words the Sherman batholith
stitches across the Cheyenne belt region. **Type I Sherman granite is
coarse granite with large porphyroblasts of K-feldspar (microcline) at
about 54 % of the rock**, and some phases show a flow texture with
aligned feldspar phenocrysts **up to 3 cm long** and cognate xenoliths
that can exceed 50 cm.

**What a climber gets:** Vedauwoo — and the reason Vedauwoo eats skin
(§3.4).

### 1.4 The Great Unconformity and the Paleozoic platform

Above the basement is a gap of roughly **two billion years**. In the
Bighorns, **Middle Cambrian Flathead Sandstone unconformably overlies a
deeply weathered outcrop of 2950 ± 5 Ma Archean granite**, with a fossil
grus and a paleosol developed on the granite at the contact — the **Great
Unconformity**, and one of the best places on Earth to put a hand on both
sides of it. Detrital zircons in the Flathead peak at ~1.8 Ga with a
minor ~2.7 Ga population, with far more Archean grains right at the
unconformity: the sea was reworking the hill it was drowning.

Then the platform. In north and central Wyoming the section, bottom to
top, runs:

| Unit | Age | Character | Cliffs? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Flathead Sandstone** | Middle Cambrian | dull-red quartzitic sandstone; brown-gray medium-grained cross-bedded glauconitic quartz arenite; often <25 m | a hard ledge/step, not a big wall |
| **Gros Ventre Formation** | Middle–Upper Cambrian | Wolsey Shale Member (soft green micaceous shale), **Death Canyon Limestone Member** (blue-gray/yellow mottled hard dense limestone), Park Shale Member | slope-former with one hard band |
| **Gallatin Limestone / Group** | Upper Cambrian | blue-gray and yellow mottled hard dense limestone | ledgy |
| **Bighorn Dolomite** | Ordovician | "**gray massive cliff-forming siliceous dolomite** and locally dolomitic limestone"; in the Bighorn Mountains reported at only ~**25–42 m (82–138 ft)** | yes — but a thin one |
| **Darby Formation** (northwest) / Jefferson | Devonian | dolomite and shale | ledgy |
| **Madison Limestone / Group** | Early Mississippian, ~350 Ma | **Mission Canyon Limestone** (blue-gray massive limestone and dolomite) over **Lodgepole Limestone** (gray cherty limestone and dolomite); ~**700 ft in the Bighorn Mountains**, ~150 m (490 ft) reported in Ten Sleep Canyon | **yes — the great cliff-former of Wyoming climbing** |
| **Amsden Formation** | Late Mississippian–Pennsylvanian | 150–350 ft; **Darwin Sandstone Member** at base (0–80 ft brown-gray fine cross-bedded sandstone), **Horseshoe Shale Member** (0–50 ft red slope-forming mudstone), **Ranchester Limestone Member** at top (100–200 ft) | the red recess between two cliff bands |
| **Tensleep Sandstone** | Pennsylvanian(–Early Permian) | **eolian** cross-bedded quartz sandstone; part of an erg that prograded south out of north-central Wyoming, interrupted repeatedly by marine transgressions; grainfall and wind-ripple foresets, avalanche strata rare | yes — a buff rimrock, alcoves and slots |
| **Phosphoria / Goose Egg / Park City** | Permian | phosphatic shale, carbonate, red beds | ledgy |

Two things follow from that table and they run through the rest of this
file. First, **the Madison is the reason Wyoming has sport climbing**: it
is thick, massive, mechanically strong, and it takes solution features.
Second, **the Bighorn Dolomite is genuinely thin here** — 82 to 138 feet
in the Bighorn Mountains — which is a fact that a 250-foot Ten Sleep
crag has to be reconciled with (§3.2).

### 1.5 The Ancestral Rockies and the Mesozoic

Wyoming caught the edge of the **Ancestral Rocky Mountains**: the
Tensleep erg developed "north of the Ancestral Rocky Mountains as Africa
collided with the North American continent," and further south the Front
Range and Uncompahgre highlands shed the arkose that Colorado climbs on.

The Mesozoic section above is mostly soft and mostly not climbed, but it
is the *scenery* of the state and it is what had to be stripped off the
uplifts:

- **Chugwater Formation** (Upper Triassic) — the red one. "Primarily red
  sandstone," a terrestrial sequence; the red band you see wrapping every
  Wyoming range front.
- **Sundance Formation** (Middle–Upper Jurassic) — greenish-gray
  glauconitic sandstone and shale over red and gray non-glauconitic
  sandstone and shale; a shallow marine seaway.
- **Morrison Formation** (Upper Jurassic) — conformably above the
  Sundance; olive-green lenticular calcareous siltstone and mudstone with
  white-to-buff calcareous quartz-arenites, eolian to fluvial. The
  dinosaur unit.
- **Cloverly Formation** (Lower Cretaceous) — rusty sandstone over
  brightly variegated bentonitic claystone, chert-pebble conglomerate at
  the base.
- **Frontier Formation** (Upper Cretaceous) — Western Interior Seaway
  shoreline sandstone with interbedded shale and bentonite. The Mowry,
  Thermopolis and Cody shales enclose it.

The Cloverly's bentonite and the Cretaceous shales are why Wyoming
range-front roads slump, and why the "badlands" apron below every crag
looks the way it does.

### 1.6 The Laramide orogeny: the event that made Wyoming climbable

Late Cretaceous into Eocene, flat-slab subduction of the Farallon plate
drove deformation a thousand miles inland. In Wyoming the **Laramide
orogeny** (broadly **75–55 Ma**, with sources giving 70–35 Ma for the
whole span) produced the classic **"thick-skinned"** style: "large
uplifts of Precambrian basement, commonly flanked by reverse faults,"
basement blocks moved **3–20 km along faults dipping 25°–40°**.

Thermochronology in the Wind Rivers puts cooling underway by **75 Ma**
(possibly 85 Ma), with the Green River Lakes / Fremont Peak sector
cooling by **62 Ma** and most rapidly **60–57 Ma**. The **Bighorn uplift
developed during the 75–55 Ma Laramide**.

The uplifts and the basins between them are the map of Wyoming climbing:

| Uplift | Basement exposed? | Climbing it makes |
|---|---|---|
| **Bighorn Mountains** | yes (2.95 Ga batholith core) | Ten Sleep Canyon, Story/Piney Creek, Tongue River, Crazy Woman — all on the tilted Paleozoic *flanks*, not the core |
| **Wind River Range** | yes, spectacularly | Cirque of the Towers, Deep Lake/Haystack, Gannett, Titcomb; and, on its southeastern flank, the Lander limestone |
| **Laramie Range** | yes, plus the 1.43 Ga plutons | Vedauwoo |
| **Granite Mountains / Sweetwater arch** | yes, then re-buried, then re-exhumed | Sweetwater Rocks, Lankin Dome, Devils Gate, Independence Rock |
| **Owl Creek / Washakie / Rattlesnake Mountain** | locally | Wind River Canyon; Cody's Shoshone Canyon |
| **Teton uplift (Laramide precursor)** | yes | later re-raised by normal faulting — see §1.8 |
| **Black Hills (edge, in NE Wyoming)** | in South Dakota | the frame Devils Tower was intruded into |

And the basins that took the debris: **Bighorn, Wind River, Powder
River, Green River, Laramie, Hanna, Denver-Julesburg edge**. Basin fill
matters to climbers for one reason above all — the Granite Mountains were
buried in their own basin fill and then dug back out (§1.9).

### 1.7 Eocene magmatism: the Absarokas and the Black Hills intrusions

While the Laramide was finishing, Wyoming caught two very different
pulses of magma.

**The Absaroka volcanic field.** The Absaroka Range "is the remnant of a
large volcanic field that was active for 10 million years during the
Middle Eocene, **53–43 Ma**," erupting roughly **29,000 km³** of
andesite, dacite, rhyolite and pyroclastic material and burying much of
northwest Wyoming and south-central Montana under as much as five miles
of volcanic rock. Activity ran along two subparallel trends about 165
miles long, Livingston MT to Dubois WY, and 60 miles wide. The **Absaroka
Volcanic Supergroup** is formally divided into the **Washburn Group**
(Sepulcher, Lamar River and Cathedral Cliffs Formations — andesitic and
dacitic volcaniclastic rocks) and the **Thorofare Creek and Sunlight
Groups** (volcaniclastic strata and andesite lava flows). It is
"**mostly altered**" andesitic material — a phrase to remember before
trusting a hold on it.

**The northern Black Hills intrusions.** At the other corner of the
state, a swarm of alkaline igneous bodies was emplaced into the flank of
the rising Black Hills at about **49 Ma** — phonolite, trachyte and
related alkaline rocks, unusual compositions that occur almost nowhere
else in the region. One of them is **Devils Tower** (§3.3).

### 1.8 Neogene: the Yellowstone hotspot and Teton extension

The **Yellowstone hotspot** track begins about **16.6 Ma** with the
Columbia River Basalt Group and migrates northeast along the Snake River
Plain, rhyolite-dominated, to its present position under the Yellowstone
Plateau. Its consequences for Wyoming climbing are indirect but real: the
hotspot's uplift of the Yellowstone Crescent of High Terrain both cooled
the region and funnelled moisture onto it, which is why the Absarokas,
the Tetons and the Winds were so heavily glaciated.

Extension came with it. The **Teton fault** is a normal fault on the east
side of the range; slip initiated **~13–7 Ma**, concentrated in the
**10–6 Ma** interval, with most movement in the last 2 Ma and a long-term
slip rate near **2 mm/yr**. Total relative displacement between the range
block and the Jackson Hole basin is **6–9 km (20,000–30,000 ft)**. The
Tetons are therefore a *young mountain range of very old rock*: a
Laramide-age uplift re-raised, and its east face freshly cut, by
Basin-and-Range-style extension.

### 1.9 The Great Exhumation, and why the Sweetwater Rocks look buried

Central Wyoming did something unusual. The **Granite Mountains /
Sweetwater arch** rose in the Laramide like the others, then in the late
Cenozoic **collapsed and was buried by its own basin fill and by regional
tuffaceous sediment** — the White River and, above it, the **Moonstone
Formation**, "best known for mantling the Precambrian granites of the
Sweetwater arch in central Wyoming" (K/Ar on tuff in the lower sandstone
sequence ~**17 Ma**; fission-track on the lower conglomerate ~**24 Ma**).
Then came what Wyoming geologists call **the Great Exhumation** — "a time
of widespread erosion, when much of the Tertiary fill was removed,
uncovering the old landscapes," followed by further burial and further
exhumation.

That history is legible in the rock at Lankin Dome: **Archean granite
domes emerging from their own sediment**, rounded by long subsurface
weathering before they were re-exposed (§3.11).

### 1.10 Pleistocene glaciation

The last major glaciation in the Rockies is named for Wyoming: the
**Pinedale glaciation** (MIS 2), "after the terminal moraines near the
town of Pinedale, Wyoming, on the west side of the Wind River Range."
Glaciers carved the Winds into the cirques, arêtes and horns that make
the Cirque of the Towers; the Tetons' canyons (Cascade, Death, Garnet)
are glacial troughs; and "**glaciers carved out the valley of Tensleep
Canyon within the last 250,000 years**," leaving a U-shaped valley floor
and moraine along West Tensleep Creek.

For a climber the consequences are concrete: **glacially polished slabs**
(low friction where the polish survives), **plucked, joint-controlled
back walls** (the steep clean faces), **hanging valleys and headwalls**,
**frost-riven summit blocks** (the loose rock on alpine ridges), and
**freeze–thaw as the dominant active process on every wall above about
7,000 ft** — which is most of the state.

## Part 2 — The one question this file has to settle first

Before the area entries: **Bighorn Dolomite or Madison Limestone?**

Wyoming's two flagship sport areas, Lander and Ten Sleep, are both
described by climbers as "Bighorn Dolomite," and both are described by
geologic sources as sitting in canyons whose principal cliff-former is
the **Madison**. This is not a trivial naming quibble. The two units are
separated by roughly **140 million years**, by a Devonian formation and
by the state's most important unconformity, and they behave differently
under the hand. The book should not print either name casually.

**What the climbing literature says.** Mountain Project's Lander Area
page: "**Most of the climbing in the Lander Area is on Bighorn
Dolomite**, with some excellent granite and sandy sandstone as well. The
dolomite varies in quality from very good to very featured and consists
mainly of crimps and positive pockets, some being as small as one-finger
pockets on the more difficult lines." Sinks Canyon even has a boulder
sub-area formally named **"Bighorn Dolomite Boulders."** For Ten Sleep:
"hundreds of high quality rock climbs on some of the best limestone in
the world: **the Bighorn Dolomite formation of the Ordovician Era**" —
repeated by nearly every guide service and destination article in the
canyon.

**What the geologic literature says.** A geologist's field description of
the same canyon: "Ten Sleep Canyon on the SW side of the Bighorn
Mountains has visually breath-taking and geologically thought-provoking
cliff exposures, with the high, yellowish cliff-former being the
**Tensleep Sandstone**, below which are shaley intervals and **gray
cliffs of the Amsden Formation and Madison Limestone**. The lower slopes
include the **Cambrian Gros Ventre Formation**, while **Archean rocks
form outcrops in the bottom of the canyon**, with the Great Unconformity
at their top." The Forest Service's own Tensleep Canyon Interpretive Site
gives the benches as, top to bottom, **Tensleep Sandstone → Amsden
(the recessed red band) → Darwin Sandstone → Madison Formation at the
base of the wall**. And the Wyoming State Geological Survey's Sinks
Canyon pamphlet puts the Sinks cavern squarely in the **Madison
Limestone**.

**Both are exposed. Both are real.** The Forest Service's own **Leigh
Creek / Bighorn Interpretive Site**, "at the bottom of Tensleep Canyon
near the western edge of the Bighorn Mountains," is named for the
**Bighorn Dolomite along the road** there — the Bighorn's upper **Leigh
Member** takes its name from Leigh Creek itself. So the Ordovician
dolomite genuinely is at road level at the canyon's lower end, and the
Madison genuinely is the great gray cliff-former above it.

**The reconciliation this file proposes**, marked as inference:

`[Inferred from regional stratigraphy — unverified]` **Ten Sleep Canyon
and the Lander crags expose both units, and the climbers' blanket name
"Bighorn Dolomite" is right for some crags and wrong for others.** The
inference rests on four published facts: (a) the full Paleozoic section,
Archean basement to Tensleep Sandstone, is exposed in Ten Sleep Canyon;
(b) the Bighorn Dolomite in the Bighorn Mountains is only **25–42 m
(82–138 ft)** thick, and in the southeastern Wind Rivers its massive
middle member is **under 150 ft**, while the Madison is **~490–700 ft**;
(c) the interpretive signage puts Madison, not Bighorn, at the base of
the big wall sections; and (d) the Forest Service names Bighorn Dolomite
specifically at the *canyon bottom* at Leigh Creek. A crag 250 ft tall
cannot be entirely in a 130-ft formation. **A crag 80 ft tall at the foot
of the canyon can be entirely in the Bighorn.**

**Why the mistake is so easy to make, and so nearly harmless
lithologically.** The Madison in Wyoming is *itself* substantially
dolomitized: the Mission Canyon Limestone is "blue-gray massive limestone
**and dolomite**" and the Lodgepole Limestone is "gray cherty limestone
**and dolomite**"; "in broad areas of the eastern Rocky Mountains and
western Great Plains, dolomite is a dominant constituent and **in places
the Madison is almost entirely dolomite**." So a climber standing on a
Madison cliff, calling the rock dolomite, is describing the *rock* 
correctly while naming the *formation* wrongly. The lithology is what the
fingers feel; the formation name is what the map says.

**Recommendation for the book.** Say **"Mississippian Madison Limestone
and Ordovician Bighorn Dolomite — both are exposed, and climbers call all
of it dolomite"** rather than picking one. Where a single name is needed,
the honest one is **"Paleozoic carbonate of the Bighorn/Wind River
platform — dolomitized limestone."** And do not repeat the claim in
`research/featured/lander.md` that Wild Iris is "part of the broader
Madison Group": no source found in this pass supports it, and the local
sources say Bighorn Dolomite (§3.1).

## Part 3 — Area by area

### 3.1 Lander — Wild Iris, Sinks Canyon, Fossil Hill, Baldwin Creek, the Sweat Lodge, Wolf Point

**Structural setting.** Lander sits at the southeastern nose of the
**Wind River Range**, a Laramide basement-cored uplift whose Archean core
was thrust northeastward over the Wind River Basin. Thermochronology puts
cooling of the range underway by **75 Ma** and most rapid at **60–57 Ma**.
The consequence for climbers is the entire local geometry: **the
Paleozoic carbonate section is tilted off the flank of the range**, so a
drive up Sinks Canyon walks you *down* the section and back in time,
"within a few miles the rock changes from sandstone to limestone, and
then to granite as you get closer to the Wind River Range." Every crag in
the Lander area is a bench on that tilted section, or the granite core
beneath it.

**The section, at Sinks Canyon, top to bottom** (per the Sinks Canyon
Natural Resource Council and the WSGS): **Tensleep Sandstone** (exposed
from the canyon mouth), **Amsden**, **Madison Limestone/Dolomite**,
**Bighorn Dolomite**, **Gallatin Limestone**, **Gros Ventre Shale**,
**Flathead Sandstone**, and **Precambrian granite and banded iron
formation** at the top of the canyon. One caution: the Sinks Canyon
Natural Resource Council's public pages state the Precambrian granite is
"over 4 billion years old." **That is not correct** — the oldest dated
rock in the Wyoming Province is 3.5 Ga, the Wind River batholiths are
**2.63 Ga (Louis Lake)** and **~2.5 Ga (Bears Ears)**, and no rock
anywhere on Earth is reliably older than ~4.03 Ga. Do not repeat that
number in the book.

#### Wild Iris

*Unit:* **Bighorn Dolomite (Middle–Late Ordovician)**, on the reported
evidence — Mountain Project's area description, and WyoFile's statement
that "the southern Wind River Mountains west of Lander are lined with
miles of **bone-white Bighorn Dolomite**." `[Contested — see Part 2.]`
The project's existing research file calls it Madison Group; **no source
found in this pass supports that**, and it should be corrected or marked.
The Bighorn on the east flank of the Wind Rivers divides into a basal
**Lander Sandstone Member** (lenticular, <5 ft, named for Lander), a
**middle massive dolomite member** (under 150 ft in the southeastern part
of the range), and the upper thin-bedded **Leigh Dolomite Member** — and
the middle massive member is exactly the right thickness and character
for these walls.

*Lithology:* white to bone-white, massive, **siliceous dolomite**. The
formation regionally is "dominated by highly bioturbated and
fossiliferous open marine shallow subtidal facies," with restricted
shallow subtidal and laminated peritidal facies and thin argillaceous
deep-subtidal limestone.

*Setting:* an Ordovician shallow tropical sea across the drowned Wyoming
craton; the dolomite is a replacement fabric, not an original mud.

*Climbing consequence — the pockets, and where they actually come from.*
Wild Iris is the pocket crag of North America's interior. "If you stand
at the base of the wall and look around, you can probably find **150
monos** on any section of cliff." The routes are short, steep test-pieces
of "big pulls off of often tiny one- and two-finger pockets."

A local explanation circulates that the pockets are **gas bubbles**.
**That is wrong, and worth correcting in print**, because the real
mechanism explains the rock's behaviour. Dolomite pockets in a platform
carbonate come from three processes, none of them volcanic:

1. **Dolomitization porosity.** Replacing calcite with dolomite swaps a
   large calcium ion for a smaller magnesium one; the mole-for-mole
   reaction causes **about 13 % shrinkage of the original bulk volume**,
   generating intercrystalline porosity, and "crystalline dolomites are
   often highly porous, with a friable, **sugary texture**." Maximum
   porosity in the regional carbonates tracks "the coarser crystalline
   dolomite facies."
2. **Selective dissolution.** Fossils, ooids, burrow fills and micritized
   grains dissolve preferentially, leaving vugs. In a bioturbated,
   fossiliferous unit like the Bighorn that is a lot of holes.
3. **Karst.** Long subaerial exposure enlarges joints and bedding
   partings into cavities.

The practical consequences follow directly. Pockets are **solution
features aligned on the fabric of the rock**, so they come in horizons
and clusters rather than at random — read the band, not the wall. Their
rims are freshly dissolved crystal terminations, which is why the local
advice is that "new pockets almost always had that **sharp lip on the
front — razor blade sharp**." (Note that the accepted local practice of
filing a razor lip is *not* the same thing as manufacturing a hold; see
Ten Sleep, §3.2, and §5.3.) A dolomite with sugary intercrystalline
porosity is **strong in compression and weak in tension at the surface**:
pocket rims break, whole pockets rarely do. **Bolts hold well** in
massive siliceous dolomite and badly in a vuggy, sugary band — which is
why glue-ins are increasingly the local norm.

*Aspect and season:* "many Wild Iris walls face south / get significant
sun" at ~9,000 ft, which is why the crag is a summer venue while Sinks,
1,500 ft lower and closer to town, works the shoulders. Between them the
town claims ~320 climbable days.

#### Sinks Canyon

*Units, by sub-zone:*
- **Lower canyon — sandstone.** "The **Tensleep Sandstone** formation is
  exposed from the mouth of the canyon, till replaced by Madison
  Limestone above the Rise of the Popo Agie." The Mountain Project
  sub-areas *The Sandstone* and *Sandstone Buttress* sit here.
  `[Inferred — unverified]` **the Sinks Canyon sandstone climbing is on
  the Pennsylvanian Tensleep Sandstone**, resting on the geowyo
  statement of its exposure from the mouth of the canyon and on the
  climbers' description of "sandy sandstone."
- **Middle canyon — carbonate.** The **Madison Limestone** (Mississippian,
  ~325–345 Ma) and the **Bighorn Dolomite** beneath it. This is where
  Killer Cave, Scud Wall, The Wilds, The Joint, the ZoZone, Hardware Wall
  and the rest are.
- **Upper canyon — granite.** "Granite is found in the southern (upper)
  end of the canyon in the national forest," the Mountain Project sub-area
  *The Granite*. `[Inferred — unverified]` this is Archean granitoid of
  the Wind River batholith complex, most plausibly the **2.63 Ga Louis
  Lake batholith**, which with the Bridger and Bears Ears batholiths makes
  up "the dominant rock of the Wind River Range" (~80 % late Archean
  granitoid). No source names the pluton at the crag.

*The Sinks itself.* The Middle Fork of the Popo Agie disappears into a
cavern in the **Madison Limestone** and reappears at **the Rise** a
quarter-mile down-canyon — **and takes over two hours to do it**, by dye
test. This is textbook karst: a phreatic conduit in carbonate, the same
plumbing that gave the Madison its paleokarst pockets in the first place.
The WSGS has published a park geology pamphlet on exactly this.

*Climbing consequence.* Three rock types in one drainage means three
climbing styles and three failure modes:

- **Tensleep Sandstone** is **eolian dune sand** — cross-bedded, with
  grainfall and wind-ripple foresets. Expect **crack systems along
  joints**, cross-bed-parallel flakes, and a friable weathering surface
  ("sandy sandstone" is the climbers' word and it is a warning). Eolian
  sandstone is quartz grains in a small volume of cement; the wet-rock
  ethic from `new-mexico.md` §4.2 applies — **give it a day or more after
  rain** before loading holds or gear.
- **Madison / Bighorn carbonate** gives the pockets, the caves (Killer
  Cave is a genuine solution feature, not a metaphor), the steep bulges,
  and the seepage. Carbonate drains *through* the rock; a limestone crag
  can weep for days after the sagebrush is dry. Killer Cave's shelter is
  a real advantage in a canyon that gets afternoon thunderstorms.
- **Granite** at the top gives cracks, faces and slabs, and grus. Archean
  granitoid this old has been weathering for a long time; expect rounded
  crystals, gritty friction and flakes that ring hollow.

#### Fossil Hill

*Unit:* `[Inferred — unverified]` **the same Paleozoic carbonate band as
the other Lander crags — Bighorn Dolomite and/or Madison Limestone** —
sitting above Sinks Canyon and west-facing. The inference rests on
position within the Lander limestone belt and on the census's own
"limestone" designation; **no source names a formation at this crag.**
The name is suggestive: crinoid and brachiopod debris is abundant in both
units, and a fossiliferous carbonate weathers to a knobby, gritty texture
where the fossil fragments stand proud of the matrix.

*Climbing consequence:* west-facing (so an evening and shoulder-season
crag, as the census says), with a **voluntary raptor/wildlife closure**
roughly June 9 – Aug 15 in past years. Raptors nest on carbonate cliffs
because carbonate cliffs have ledges and solution alcoves — the closure
is, in a real sense, a consequence of the geology.

#### Baldwin Creek

*Unit:* **dolomite** — "a beautiful **dolomite cliff**, about 15 miles
outside of Lander," managed by the **BLM** (which has its own *Baldwin
Creek Climbing Area* page — a land-manager correction: the census lists
Baldwin Creek as unverified and unattributed). About **50 established
routes, 5.11 to 5.14**; "**the best rock in Lander**" per local
description, "a high concentration of hard clip-ups."

*Setting & climbing consequence:* the cliff **faces due south**, and
"you can't get there in the winter as the road is closed for **elk
habitat management**"; the approach road wants 4WD and there is a hike on
top. South-facing dolomite means a spring/fall/winter-adjacent crag that
bakes in July — the inverse of Wild Iris, 4,000 ft higher. The rock being
called the best in the area is consistent with a well-cemented, massive
band of the same dolomite; grade concentration at 5.11–5.14 suggests
steep, pocketed, featured rock rather than the vertical crimping at
Sinks.

#### The Sweat Lodge

*Unit:* **dolomite**, in the **Little Popo Agie** canyon.

*The feature is pure structural geology:* "It was formed by a **massive
dolomite flake that has fallen off and is wedged against the main cliff
band**," forming an A-frame with climbing on both the sheltered inner
face of the flake and the parent wall behind. That is a **toppled
joint-bounded slab** — carbonate cliffs fail along vertical joints, and
where a slab detaches and leans rather than shattering, you get exactly
this geometry.

*Climbing consequence:* the shade and shelter are the point (~15 routes,
5.12c and up, "pumpy pocket-pulling and crimping"). Two cautions follow
from the geology rather than from any guidebook: a leaning flake is
**held up by friction and geometry, not by attachment**, and the ground
inside an A-frame is the debris cone from the collapse. And a
north-facing, shaded, enclosed carbonate space **stays damp**.

#### Wolf Point

*Unit:* **dolomite** — "a huge **south facing cliff** in the Little Popo
Agie canyon … the best crag in Lander for anyone searching out steep,
continuous routes up **featured dolomite**."

*Climbing consequence:* the approach is the geology's fault. A big
south-facing wall in a deep tributary canyon means a "long bumpy drive"
plus roughly an hour on foot, **1,500 ft down and 1,000 ft back up** —
you descend into the canyon to reach the base of a cliff whose top is
near the plateau surface. Steep and continuously featured is what a
thick, well-dolomitized band gives when the canyon wall is undercut.

### 3.2 Ten Sleep Canyon

**Structural setting.** Ten Sleep Canyon cuts the **southwestern flank of
the Bighorn uplift**, a Laramide basement-cored block that "developed in
the Rocky Mountain foreland during the **75–55 Ma** Laramide orogeny,"
with an Archean core (the Bighorn batholith, **2950 ± 5 Ma** where dated
at the Great Unconformity) and the Paleozoic platform tilted off its
flanks. Structurally the west side of the range is the classic Wyoming
geometry — the **Shell monocline**, "generated by a buried reverse fault,"
was the type example used to build the *block uplift model* of Laramide
folding.

Then the ice: "**glaciers carved out the valley of Tensleep Canyon within
the last 250,000 years**," leaving a U-shaped valley floor and moraine
along West Tensleep Creek. Near Meadowlark Lake, at the top of the drive,
"an apron of glacial debris … conceals the 2-billion-plus-year age gap
between the rocks of the canyon walls and the Precambrian rocks that form
the high peaks."

**Stratigraphy of the canyon walls, top to bottom** (Forest Service
interpretive signage plus a geologist's field description):

| Bench | Unit | Look |
|---|---|---|
| rim | **Tensleep Sandstone** | the high yellowish cliff-former |
| recess | **Amsden Formation** | the red slope — Horseshoe Shale |
| ledge | **Darwin Sandstone Member** (basal Amsden) | thin brown-gray sandstone bench |
| **the walls** | **Madison Limestone** | gray cliffs — the main cliff-former |
| lower wall / road level at Leigh Creek | **Bighorn Dolomite** (incl. the **Leigh Member**, named for Leigh Creek) | thin, hard, cliffy dolomite band |
| lower slopes | **Gallatin / Gros Ventre** | slopes, not cliffs |
| canyon bottom | **Archean basement**, with the **Great Unconformity** at its top | outcrops in the creek bed |

**Rock name — see Part 2.** Climbers call all of it Bighorn Dolomite.
The Bighorn is real and is at road level at the canyon's lower end, but
the Madison is the thick gray cliff-former and cannot be avoided in a
canyon whose crags run from 6,000 to 9,500 ft. **The book should say
"Madison Limestone and Bighorn Dolomite" and note that climbers call all
of it dolomite.** One climbing source is admirably precise about the
lithology even while getting the formation wrong: "while some might say
that Ten Sleep Canyon hosts the best *limestone* sport climbing in the
'States, they are wrong — **Ten Sleep is in fact dolomite**, though the
composition and style of climbing is hard to distinguish from limestone."

**Lithology and colour — and what "blue rock" is.** Climbers prize the
canyon's **"beautiful gold and blue-streaked walls,"** and French Cattle
Ranch in the upper canyon is "celebrated for its beautiful, high-quality
dolomite rock that often displays stunning **gold, white, and blue**
hues." That colour scheme is not decoration; it is a rock-quality map:

- **Blue-gray** is the signature of the **Mission Canyon Limestone**,
  formally described as "**blue-gray massive limestone and dolomite**" —
  unweathered, dense, low-porosity carbonate. This is why climbers'
  "blue rock" is the good rock: they are looking at **fresh rock**,
  unaltered by the surface weathering rind.
- **Gold / tan / orange** is an iron-oxide-stained weathering rind, or
  water-streaked calcite. Often good, sometimes a shell.
- **Bone-white** is the leached, decalcified, chalky surface of an
  exposed carbonate — the least reliable of the three, and the reason
  some Ten Sleep walls that look striking climb badly.

**Climbing consequence.**

- **Pockets are the medium.** "There are **millions of pockets**. Some are
  HUGE, some are very tiny, **most are sharp**." Their origin is the same
  triple mechanism as at Wild Iris (§3.1): dolomitization porosity,
  selective dissolution of grains and fossils, and karst. In the Madison
  specifically, an enormous amount of pore space was made by **evaporite
  paleokarst**: the "lithological trinity of dolostone, limestone and
  sulfates" dissolves the sulfates first, and "**karst deposits and
  solution-breccia beds are widespread in the upper part of the Madison
  Group in Montana and Wyoming**," with "enlarged joints, sinkholes,
  caves and two solution-breccia zones" documented in the upper 400 ft.
  There is a real risk hidden in that sentence: a **solution-breccia
  zone is a band of collapsed, re-cemented rubble**, and a band of
  collapse breccia in a cliff is a band of bad holds and bad bolts.
- **Plates and flakes.** Climbers report pulling "pockets, edges and
  **flakes**," with "between-buttress layback flakes." Those are
  **bedding-parallel partings** and joint-bounded slabs — carbonate
  platform rock is layered, the layers differ in hardness, and the softer
  interbeds erode back to leave the harder ones standing as plates.
  Plates are the holds *and* the hazard: a plate is attached on one side.
- **Angle.** "Much of the rock has a mildly bulgy, slightly overhung
  quality so climbs can be pumpy," commonly "roughly 15 degrees
  overhanging" on the steep routes, with dead-vertical technical faces
  elsewhere. The bulges are dissolution-sculpted bedding, not tectonics.
- **Bolts.** "Kindly spaced bolts throughout most of the canyon" is a
  cultural fact resting on a geological one: massive, siliceous,
  well-cemented dolomite holds a bolt. It is also why the
  route-manufacturing controversy took the form it did.
- **Shade and season.** Aspect drives the day: Downtown gets afternoon
  shade around 2 pm, Leigh Creek is shaded in the morning, Cobra and Full
  Charge stay cool most of the day, and Crag 6 above Meadowlark Lake sits
  near 9,000 ft. That range of elevation and aspect is why the canyon
  climbs from spring into fall.

**The ethics fight is a geology story too.** Ten Sleep's manufactured-hold
controversy — chipping, drilling and gluing, "some of the routes with as
many as 20 drilled holds," the 2019 bolt-chopping, and the Forest
Service's **July 19, 2019 forest-wide halt to new bolting and route
development** — happened *here* rather than on granite for a physical
reason. A vuggy, sugary dolomite with high intercrystalline porosity is
**soft enough to drill a "pocket" into with a hand tool, and pocketed
enough that a manufactured pocket is hard to distinguish from a real
one.** The local convention of **filing a razor-sharp pocket lip** for
safety sits at one end of a continuum whose other end is a fabricated
route, and the rock itself provides no bright line. That is exactly why
the community had to draw one. (§5.3.)

### 3.3 Devils Tower

**Unit and age.** **Phonolite porphyry, Eocene, ~49 Ma.** Devils Tower
is "a large mass of columnar-jointed **porphyritic phonolite** of Eocene
age (49 million years old)" — one of a swarm of alkaline igneous
intrusions emplaced into the flank of the rising Black Hills late in the
Laramide. The census and gazetteer both already say phonolite porphyry;
**that is correct and unusually well sourced, and it should be kept.**

**What phonolite actually is, and why the name matters.** Phonolite is
*not* basalt, and the distinction is not pedantry. Phonolite is a
**silica-undersaturated alkaline rock**: it is trachyte's cousin,
containing **nepheline or leucite instead of quartz**, "dominated by
low-silica feldspathoid minerals … more than feldspar minerals," with
sanidine/anorthoclase, sodalite, hauyne and analcime among its primary
phases and biotite, amphibole, pyroxene and olivine as secondaries. At
Devils Tower it is **porphyritic** — "gray-green igneous rock with
**white feldspar crystals**" set in a fine groundmass. Nepheline's Mohs
hardness is 5.5–6, and the rock overall sits in that range: hard, but
softer than a quartz-bearing granite. Phonolite takes its name from the
sound: "a fine-grained, compact igneous rock that splits into thin, tough
plates which **make a ringing sound when struck**." Any climber who has
tapped a suspect flake on the Tower has heard the etymology.

**Emplacement setting — still genuinely argued.** "The scientific debate
on the origin of Devils Tower has lasted for more than 100 years."
Candidates in the literature include a **magmatic stock**, a
**laccolith**, a **volcanic conduit/neck**, and — in a 2015 *Geosphere*
paper — a **lava coulée emplaced into a maar-diatreme volcano**. What is
*not* in dispute: the magma cooled slowly enough, and in a body of the
right geometry, to produce the world's finest columnar jointing; the
column form we see is close to the shape of the original igneous mass;
and the Tower became a tower only when the soft sedimentary cover was
stripped off it.

**The frame it sits in.** The hills at the base are "red, yellow, green
or gray … sandstone, shale, or gypsum," about **400 ft thick**,
comprising, oldest to youngest, the upper **Spearfish Formation**
(Triassic, dark red sandstone and maroon siltstone with shale — exposed
along the Belle Fourche River), the **Gypsum Spring Formation** (Middle
Jurassic, a thin white gypsum band), and the **Sundance Formation** (Late
Jurassic — Stockade Beaver Shale, Hulett Sandstone, Lak and Redwater
Shale members). The Tower stands because those rocks erode and it does
not, and **"as rain and snow continue to erode the sedimentary rocks
surrounding the Tower's base, more of Devils Tower will be exposed"** —
the monument is still growing.

**Dimensions.** Summit **867 ft above its base**, 5,112 ft above sea
level; the summit surface is roughly **180 × 300 ft**; the base
circumference is about a mile; the monument is 1,347 acres.

**Column geometry — this is the climbing.** From the USGS/NPS
description: most columns are **five-sided**, with four-, six- and
seven-sided columns also present; they are **6 to 8 ft in diameter at the
base, tapering upward to about 4 ft at the top**; some run **over 180 m
(590 ft)** from base to summit. Crucially: "the columns are bounded by
**well-developed smooth joints in the middle part of the Tower**, but as
the columns taper upward, the joints between them, rather than being
smooth, **may be wavy and some of the columns may unite**," and
"**numerous cross-fractures in the upper part** of the Tower divide the
columns into many small irregularly shaped blocks."

**Climbing consequence.** Almost everything climbers say about the Tower
falls out of that paragraph.

- **The routes are the joints.** Devils Tower has essentially no face
  climbing because a columnar-jointed body has essentially no faces —
  only column flanks and the parting planes between them. The
  crack-climbing reputation is not a style choice; it is the only thing
  the rock offers. Reported sizes on the Durrance run **fingers to
  chimney**, with a pitch that takes finger-to-fist plus a large
  Camalot, a double-crack sequence that "eventually leads to
  offwidthing," and a section wanting a **#5 Camalot**. That range —
  fingers to offwidth to chimney — is precisely what a set of parallel
  cooling joints between 4- and 8-ft columns produces as the columns
  separate by varying amounts.
- **The cracks are uniform, and that is a geologic fact.** "Cracks are
  beautiful and **uniformly shaped**." Cooling joints form perpendicular
  to the cooling front and propagate with regular spacing; the resulting
  crack has parallel walls over long distances. Cams are excellent in
  them. What they lack is constrictions — a parallel-sided joint gives
  few natural chockstones or tapers, so nuts are less useful than on a
  weathered granite crack.
- **Rock quality is a function of height on the wall, exactly as the
  geology predicts.** Climbers report "excellent **up until the last 100
  feet of the tower or so**." That matches the USGS description precisely:
  smooth joints in the middle of the Tower, wavy joints and **numerous
  cross-fractures dividing the columns into small irregular blocks** near
  the top. **The summit band is the fractured band.** Take the loose-rock
  warnings on the final pitches literally.
- **The talus is the hazard, and it is active.** "Unsupported by the
  sediments, the **exterior columns occasionally break and fall** around
  the tower, leaving huge sharp-edged boulders at its base." The boulder
  field is not an ancient landform; it is the Tower's ongoing failure
  mechanism. The columns at the outside of the array lose lateral
  support as the soft sediments erode, and go. Approach and descend with
  that in mind, and do not linger under the face.
- **Polish.** The Durrance is "a little polished on the left side and
  will feel harder than 5.7 if you are less experienced in crack
  climbing." Fine-grained phonolite polishes readily under traffic — it
  has no coarse quartz grains to keep renewing the surface. Expect the
  85 %-of-summit-traffic route to be slicker than its grade.
- **Friction.** A fine-grained, compact, feldspathoid-rich rock gives
  moderate friction — nothing like Sherman Granite's crystal grip, and
  nothing like sandstone's. On the Tower, security comes from jamming
  geometry, not from smearing.

**The June voluntary closure — cultural context.** The **1995 Devils
Tower National Monument Climbing Management Plan** established a
voluntary closure of all climbing routes each June out of respect for
Native American ceremonial use; it has been implemented every June since
**1996**, and it was agreed by a work group that included tribal
representatives and climbing organisations. **26 tribes hold the Tower —
*Mato Tipila*, Bear Lodge — as significant in their cultural or religious
beliefs**, and June ceremonies including the **Lakota Sun Dance** are
among their most important spiritual practices. Climber compliance has
been reported at roughly an **85 % reduction** in June climbing, and the
**Access Fund fully supports the closure**. It is voluntary; it is
observed; the book should present it as an obligation of good manners
rather than a regulation, which is exactly how the climbing community
treats it. Note also the name: the book should give **Bear Lodge / Mato
Tipila** alongside the map name.

### 3.4 Vedauwoo

**Unit and age.** **Sherman Granite, ~1.43 Ga (batholith dated
1438–1431 Ma)**, in the **Laramie Range** between Cheyenne and Laramie on
the north side of I-80, at about 8,000 ft. The census and gazetteer both
say Sherman Granite; **that is correct.** The WSGS maintains a
public-facing Vedauwoo geology guide.

**Composition — the reason this crag has a reputation.** The Sherman is a
coarse, **K-feldspar-porphyritic granite**: "**Type I Sherman granite is
coarse granite with large porphyroblasts of K-feldspar (microcline)**"
making up about **54 %** of the rock, with some phases showing a flow
texture of aligned feldspar phenocrysts **up to 3 cm long** and cognate
xenoliths exceeding 50 cm. Field descriptions: "pink, massive,
mid-Proterozoic"; "pink feldspar, white quartz, black specs of hornblende
and other minerals such as mica"; "frequently quite coarse-grained, rough
surfaces displaying **smoky quartz and larger alkali feldspar crystals
1–2 cm wide**." A second granite, the **Lincoln Granite**, is present in
the area and forms towers of its own — worth knowing if a route's rock
looks different from the neighbours'. `[Two-granite distinction reported
but not mapped here — unverified for specific crags.]`

**Structural setting.** The Sherman batholith and the coeval **Laramie
anorthosite complex** intruded older crust — Archean rocks in the
southern Laramie Range and ~1.7 Ga metamorphic rocks — near the
**Cheyenne belt**, the Archean/Proterozoic suture (§1.2). The whole block
was then raised by the **Laramide** uplift of the Laramie Range, "about
70 million years ago."

**Landform — tors, corestones and grus, and why it matters to a
climber.** Vedauwoo's domes and stacks are **tors**: rounded corestones
of intact granite left standing when the rotted rock around them was
stripped away. "Groundwater flowing through fractured granite can break
down narrow zones of rock, creating rounded blocks and **filling the
cracks with grus**"; tors "**develop underground**, where they wait to be
revealed." The granite "weathers to **grus** — accumulation of angular,
coarse-grained fragments (sand and gravel) resulting from the granular
disintegration of crystalline rocks." The mechanism is specific and it is
mineralogical: **biotite weathering to swelling clays** expands and
prises the near-surface rock apart, crystal from crystal. And the pattern
is joint-controlled: "differential weathering by ice, water and wind on
the rocks and **within east-northeast trending fractures and joints** has
created the landscape seen today."

**Climbing consequence.** Vedauwoo is the clearest case in this file of
geology dictating climbing style, and every complaint climbers make about
it is a mineral property.

- **The cracks are joints, and the joints are widely spaced.** A coarse,
  massive, monolithic granite with a **widely spaced ENE joint set** does
  not give you a hundred hairline seams; it gives you a few big splits
  through a big block. Hence "**offwidths … have formed the basis of
  Vedauwoo's widespread reputation**." The offwidth is not an eccentric
  local taste. It is what this rock makes.
- **The cracks flare, and that is corestone weathering.** "In Vedauwoo,
  the cracks tend to **open up (flare) on the outside**, so jams are
  typically deep inside the crack or not possible at all." That is
  exactly the geometry of subsurface corestone rounding: the joint faces
  are chemically etched and rounded near the surface, where water sat
  longest, and stay tight and square deep inside. **Every flared,
  rounded, insecure Vedauwoo jam is a fossil of the rock's underground
  weathering.** Practically: expect to jam deep, expect cams to want to
  walk outward in the flare, and expect the best gear to be well back
  from the lip.
- **It eats skin because 54 % of it is feldspar.** "The Precambrian
  granite is laced with **huge feldspar crystals with scalpel-like
  edges**"; "**granite crystals like Lego pieces rip chunks out of your
  skin**." Feldspar cleaves on two good planes at nearly 90°, so a
  weathered feldspar phenocryst breaks down into blocky fragments with
  straight, sharp edges rather than rounding off the way quartz does.
  A rock that is half centimetre-scale feldspar, disintegrating grain by
  grain, is a rock covered in small blades. It also means the friction is
  outstanding — "sharp, hostile crystals that will shred your skin but
  may just **keep you from slipping out**." **Tape up. All of it.**
- **The sandbag is structural, not cultural.** "Solid 5.11 leaders have
  been sandbagged by many routes of lesser grade." Flaring, grainy,
  wide, poorly protected cracks in a rock with no face holds between them
  produce climbing that resists grade calibration against face-climbing
  areas. It is a fair grade for the medium and a brutal one on arrival.
- **Grus at the base and on the ledges.** Ball-bearing grit on every
  slab and ledge, and rounded, gritty footholds. Brush and expect
  slipping.
- **Blocks.** A tor is a stack of joint-bounded blocks whose separating
  rock has been removed. Some of those blocks are supported only by
  friction. Test everything on the summit blocks and in chimneys.

**Season and aspect.** ~8,000 ft, short approaches, and famously
**windy** — the tors are wind-scoured for a reason. Summer and shoulder
seasons; south faces climb surprisingly late in the year.

### 3.5 The Laramie-area crags beyond Vedauwoo — the Snowy Range

**Unit and age — and a correction.** The gazetteer's Rock column for the
Snowy Range reads "**Alpine granite/quartzite**." The geology says
**quartzite, decisively**, and the granite half of that entry should go.

**Medicine Bow Peak and the peaks of the Medicine Bow Mountains are
composed of the Medicine Peak Quartzite**, a Paleoproterozoic
metasedimentary unit **2.4–2.0 Ga** (deposited within the **Snowy Pass
Supergroup** between about **2300 and 2000 Ma**), "**extraordinarily
thick** — on the order of **5,600 feet**," and Medicine Bow Peak itself
is described as "a mass of **pure white quartzite**, rising nearly 2,000
feet above the surrounding country." It was originally shallow-marine
sand, buried, heated and recrystallised.

Related units in the same supergroup, useful for orientation: the **Nash
Fork Formation**, a 6,500-ft tan siliceous metadolomite with black
phyllite lenses, carrying **2-billion-year-old stromatolites** in
spectacular outcrops at the eastern base of the range; and the **Libby
Creek Group**, intruded by mafic dikes through faults in the underlying
Archean rocks. The whole package was deposited in "a widening,
rift-formed **passive-margin basin**" on the southern edge of the Wyoming
craton — i.e. the shelf that the Cheyenne belt (§1.2) later closed.

**Climbing consequence.** Quartzite is not granite and does not behave
like it:

- **It is very hard and nearly non-porous.** Quartz grains recrystallised
  and welded together; the rock breaks *through* grains rather than
  around them. Friction on clean quartzite is superb; friction on
  lichened or wet quartzite is poor, and it stays wet longer than its
  hardness suggests because water sits on it rather than in it.
- **Holds are edges and bedding breaks, not pockets.** No solution
  features, no crystals to pinch. The "alpine slab and face" character
  the census reports is exactly right for a massive metaquartzite.
- **Protection is the problem.** Quartzite joints tend to be **shallow,
  flaring, and blind**. Metamorphic foliation and relict bedding give
  parting planes that make flakes and thin cracks rather than parallel
  splitters. That, plus the sub-5.10 grades reported, reads as slab
  climbing with sparse gear — the same profile as the Organ Mountains
  slabs in `new-mexico.md`.
- **Altitude and season.** Climbing "starts around 10,500 ft"; the census
  gives mid-June to mid-September with crampons sometimes useful into
  July. That is a glaciated Pleistocene cirque landscape — the walls
  south of Medicine Bow Peak are plucked glacial headwalls, which is why
  they are steep and clean, and why the talus below them is fresh.

`[Inferred — unverified]` **The climbing at the Medicine Bow Peak crags
is on Medicine Peak Quartzite.** The inference rests on the published
statement that the peaks of the range are built of it, on Medicine Bow
Peak's description as a mass of pure white quartzite, and on the
climbing sources' own use of the word quartzite. No source names the unit
at a specific crag.

### 3.6 Fremont Canyon, Dome Rock and Independence Rock — the Granite Mountains

These three sit on the same basement massif: the **Granite Mountains /
Sweetwater arch** of central Wyoming, the Laramide uplift that collapsed,
was buried in its own basin fill, and was dug back out (§1.9).

**The rock.** USGS reconnaissance geochronology of the Granite Mountains:
the western part contains "a **metamorphic complex of gneisses, schists
and amphibolites**" derived by amphibolite-grade metamorphism from a
sedimentary-volcanic sequence, with whole-rock Rb-Sr metamorphism at
**2,860 ± 80 Ma**; this was intruded by "a **batholith and satellite
bodies of medium- to coarse-grained, generally massive biotite granite**
and related **pegmatite and aplite**," the main granite body dated
**2,550 ± 60 Ma**. That is the granite of all three areas.

**A discrepancy to carry, not to resolve.** WyoHistory states
Independence Rock is "Precambrian granite approximately **1.5 billion
years old**"; Wikipedia calls it "a large rounded monolith of **Archean
granite** typical of the surrounding region … at the southeast end of the
Granite Mountains"; the BLM's Sweetwater Rocks page describes "massive
blocks of **2.6 to 2.8 billion-year-old Archean granite**"; and the USGS
dates the Granite Mountains batholith at 2.55 Ga. **The weight of the
evidence is Archean (2.5–2.8 Ga), and the 1.5 Ga figure looks like an
error** — possibly a confusion with the 1.4 Ga Sherman Granite two
ranges east. Do not print 1.5 Ga.

#### Fremont Canyon

*Setting.* "The North Platte has cut through uplifted strata **all the
way down to basement rocks**, revealing an intriguing gap in the rock
record of some two billion years" — the Great Unconformity again, this
time exposed by a river rather than a mountain front. The canyon is about
four miles southwest of Pathfinder Dam; the rim country above carries the
**Goose Egg Formation** (Permian) and its associated sandstone and
limestone.

*Lithology and climbing consequence.* Two words in the sources do all the
work: **"water-worn."** "Hundreds of rock climbing routes on **water worn
granite**"; "these steep **water-worn walls**." A gorge cut by a large
river through hard granite produces walls that were, until recently in
geologic terms, the *bed and banks of that river* — abraded, polished,
scalloped and undercut, with potholes and flutes. Consequences:

- **Friction is lower than a normal granite crag.** Polished granite
  gives less than fresh granite. The canyon's reputation as "a hard
  training ground" and the grade concentration at **5.9–5.12** are partly
  a friction story.
- **Cracks are the protection**, and the best of them are "**some of the
  best single-pitch cracks in the canyon**" right under the bridge.
  Water-worn joint faces are rounded at the lip, so, as at Vedauwoo,
  place deep.
- **Rap-in access is a landform consequence.** Many routes are reached by
  rapping from the rim to a ledge or hanging belay above the water,
  because the base of the wall is the river. Retreat, rope management and
  rising water are all part of the geology of a dam-controlled gorge.
- **The Blade** (5.10d, a detached 95-ft tower) is a **joint-bounded slab
  separated from the wall** — the same failure geometry as the Sweat
  Lodge flake at Lander, one step further along. The West Chimney (5.4)
  behind it is explicitly "**easy but loose**," which is what the gap
  behind a detaching tower always is.
- **Aspect.** Power Tower Wall is "a striking **South/Southeast facing**
  granite wall" with multipitch lines. South-facing granite over a
  reservoir at 6,000-odd feet is why the census can call this a
  much-of-the-winter crag.

#### Dome Rock

*Unit:* `[Inferred — unverified]` **Archean granite of the Granite
Mountains / Pedro Mountains basement block.** Mountain Project files it
as "**Dome Rock / Pedro Mountains**," and the BLM's Dome Rock page places
it on the **Seminoe–Alcova Backcountry Byway**, elevation **7,116 ft**,
with the main climbing on the **west side**. The Pedro Mountains are a
Precambrian-cored block of the same central Wyoming basement; the USGS
Granite Mountains geochronology (2.86 Ga metamorphic complex intruded by
2.55 Ga biotite granite plus pegmatite and aplite) is the best available
description. **No source read in this pass names the pluton at the
crag.**

*Climbing consequence.* Described as "**perfect for beginning rock
climbers with its gentle inclines and forgiving terrain**" and "a staple
area for climbers from Casper." Gentle inclines on a granite dome means
**exfoliation slabs** — sheeting joints parallel to the surface, the
classic dome architecture (§1.9, and compare Lankin Dome below). Friction
climbing on knobs and crystals, thin gear, and — the recurring central
Wyoming caveat — **a mix of BLM and private land, with the rancher owning
several acres around the dome itself; ask.**

*Correction to the gazetteer:* Dome Rock is currently listed as
"unverified" with no style, size or grades. It can now be given a land
manager (**BLM**, Seminoe–Alcova Backcountry Byway), an elevation
(**7,116 ft**), a west-side climbing aspect, a beginner-terrain
character, and the **private-inholding access caveat**.

#### Independence Rock

*Unit:* **Archean granite** of the Granite Mountains, an isolated
monolith at the southeast end of the range beside the Sweetwater River.

*Landform:* a textbook **exfoliation dome**. "As Independence Rock was
slowly uncovered by erosion, the immense pressures of the weight of
overlying rock were gradually lessened. The rock then **expanded
outwardly, shedding its surface layers like an onion**." Local accounts
add that "about 15 million years ago, **wind-blown sand smoothed and
rounded** their summits" — the Granite Mountains' burial-and-exhumation
history written on a single rock.

*Climbing consequence:* a low-angle, shelly, sheeted granite whaleback.
Scrambling terrain, as the census correctly says. What a climber should
note is the **sheeting** — the shells are the rock's structure, and
detached shells are the loose rock. Note also that it is a **National
Historic Landmark covered in emigrant inscriptions**; the ethical status
is closer to a rock-art site than to a crag.

### 3.7 Sweetwater Rocks — Lankin Dome and Devils Gate

*Unit:* **Archean granite**, "massive blocks of **2.6 to 2.8
billion-year-old Archean granite** … pushed southwestward along the
**Emigrant Trail thrust fault**" (BLM). The Lankin Dome WSA covers
**6,316 acres** of BLM land (plus 360 acres of split-estate surface) in
Fremont County, about 10 miles east of Jeffrey City.

*Landform:* "two landforms: the **uplifted mountains of reddish granite
rocks, slabs and exfoliated domes**, and the flats of Nolen Pocket north
and west of the rocks." That is the Great Exhumation in one sentence
(§1.9): domes of ancient granite standing out of a plain of young
sediment, the sediment being the stuff that used to bury them.

*Climbing consequence.* The Sky Route on the Direct South Buttress —
"**the classic of its length and grade in Wyoming**," 7 pitches, 5.5 to
5.9+ — is described as "varied **slab and face climbing** … **run out
slab, crystal holds**, and face climbing on slabs," on granite whose
"rock quality is incredible."

Every phrase there is diagnostic of a **massive, poorly jointed,
coarse-grained granite dome**:

- **Runout is inevitable.** An exfoliation dome's only fractures are the
  sheeting joints parallel to its surface — which are underfoot, not in
  front of you. There is nothing to put gear in between them. Compare
  Questa Dome in `new-mexico.md`.
- **Crystal holds are the medium.** With no cracks and no pockets, the
  holds are the phenocrysts and the differential weathering around them.
  A coarse biotite granite that has been weathering since the Eocene has
  quartz and feldspar standing proud of the rotted mica — good friction,
  small positive edges, and a surface that sheds grus.
- **"Reddish"** means iron oxidation of the biotite and hornblende — a
  weathering rind. Rinds on granite are usually sound; rinds on a sheeted
  dome can be the outer shell of an exfoliation slab. Listen for hollow.
- **South-facing and sunny**, so late spring/early summer and fall, and
  hot in midsummer with no shade for an hour in any direction.

*Access is a geologic-landuse hybrid.* The **Lankin Gap approach closes
roughly March/April–June for livestock grazing**; the Agate Flat Road
approach from the west is open year-round except when snow-drifted; the
land is a checkerboard of BLM and private and "closures are adjustable
and are done via ranchers and BLM officials." Respect fences and gates.

*Devils Gate* — the Sweetwater River's notch through a granite ridge, a
few miles from Independence Rock and a famous emigrant landmark. `[Rock
type inferred — unverified]` **the same Archean Granite Mountains
basement**; the census's "granite" is right on regional grounds but no
climbing-specific geologic source was found. The gate itself is a
**water gap**: the river held its course while the granite ridge rose
across it, and sawed through. That is worth a sentence in the book
regardless of route count.

### 3.8 The Wind River Range alpine — Cirque of the Towers, Deep Lake/Haystack, Gannett and Titcomb

**Unit and age.** The Wind Rivers are the biggest exposure of Archean
crust in the state. "The basement rocks of the Wind River Range consist
of **ancient Archean granites, gneisses, and metasedimentary units**," and
"approximately **80 % of the Wind River Range is composed of late Archean
granitoids**," dominated by three batholiths: **Bridger**, **Louis Lake
(emplaced ~2630 Ma)** and **Bears Ears (~2500 Ma)**. The Louis Lake is a
calc-alkalic I-type pluton "of minor diorite, quartz diorite,
granodiorite and granite," formed by partial melting of an Early Archean
trondhjemitic-to-tonalitic protolith. "At shallow structural levels the
batholith is pyroxene free, but at deeper levels all units contain
pyroxenes."

**Structural and glacial setting.** Laramide uplift raised the Wind River
block along a crustal-scale thrust — the range is the type example
imaged by the COCORP deep seismic profiles — with cooling underway by
**75 Ma** and most rapid at **60–57 Ma**. Then the ice did the sculpting:
"later glacial erosion shaped the **cirques, basins, ridges, lake chains
and steep headwalls** that define the range today," and the last major
Rocky Mountain glaciation is named **Pinedale** for the moraines on this
range's west side. **Gannett Peak is flanked by five glaciers**, "the
largest single concentration of glacial ice in the American Rockies,"
with **Gannett Glacier** the largest single glacier in the US Rockies and
the **Dinwoody** among the most studied.

**The north/south rock split — the most useful thing in this entry.**
The American Alpine Club's own reconnaissance of Titcomb Basin puts it
plainly: "**Most of the northern Wind Rivers is gneiss** — metamorphic
rock … formed when rock below the surface was subjected to such high
temperatures and pressures that it softened and flowed like chocolate in
a warm pack." And: "While Cirque rock is famously good granite,
**Titcomb's is more enigmatic. If Cirque rock is vanilla ice cream in its
uniformity, Titcomb rock is many flavored**, including various swirly
concoctions. Titcomb's gneiss was there when granite intruded, but the
emplacement of granite didn't occur as cleanly as a geologic map
suggests."

That single paragraph explains most of what climbers report about the
range, and it belongs in the book:

- **Southern Winds (Cirque of the Towers, Deep Lake, Haystack, Big
  Sandy)** — homogeneous late-Archean granitoid. Uniform, predictable,
  well-jointed. "The rock quality on most of the mountain is **high
  quality granite**."
- **Northern Winds (Titcomb, Gannett, Dinwoody)** — gneiss with granite
  intruded into it, heterogeneous at outcrop scale. Foliation, contacts,
  screens and inclusions all give planes of weakness that a uniform
  pluton does not. **Expect variable rock, and expect it to vary within a
  pitch.**

**Climbing consequence, southern Winds.**

- **Coarse grain.** "The rock was coarse, with **large inch-sized
  crystals that painfully press into your hands** as you tightened them
  into jams." That is a coarse Archean granitoid weathering by grain
  loss — Vedauwoo's problem at altitude, if less extreme.
- **Jointing.** Pingora and Wolf's Head give continuous crack systems and
  clean faces because a massive granitoid pluton cracks along a few
  well-organised joint sets; glaciers then plucked along those joints to
  produce the towers themselves. **The Cirque's shape is the joint
  pattern, made visible by ice.**
- **The named hazard is talus and ledges, not the routes.** "Watch for
  **loose talus near trail junctions and ledges**." Above the plucked,
  clean lower walls sit frost-shattered summit ridges and ledge systems
  full of freeze–thaw debris. Rockfall on alpine granite in the Rockies
  is overwhelmingly a *ledge and gully* problem, not a face problem.
- **Weather.** Afternoon thunderstorms are routine, and a granite tower
  at 12,000 ft is a lightning rod. This is not geology, but the exposure
  that makes the climbing is the exposure that makes the risk.

**Climbing consequence, northern Winds (Titcomb, Gannett).**
`[Inferred from the reported gneiss/granite mix — unverified at
individual walls]` Gneissic terrain gives: **foliation-parallel flakes
and exfoliating sheets**, cracks that follow contacts rather than clean
joints and therefore taper and blank out, **micaceous bands that are
slick when wet and rotten when weathered**, and quality that changes with
lithologic layering. Gannett is a snow-and-ice objective more than a rock
objective for good reason. Add active glaciers — moving ice, crevasses,
a retreating margin exposing fresh unstable moraine, and bergschrunds
that grow through the season.

### 3.9 The Tetons — Grand Teton, Death Canyon, Cascade Canyon, Blacktail Butte

**The range in one sentence:** *young mountains of very old rock.*
Roughly **2.7 Ga** basement raised on a **~13–7 Ma and still active**
normal fault (§1.8), with **6–9 km** of relative displacement and its
canyons cut by Pleistocene glaciers.

**The units, with ages** (USGS geochronology of Teton Precambrian rocks):

| Unit | Age | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Layered gneisses** | metamorphosed **2,875 ± 150 Ma** (Rb-Sr, with Webb Canyon Gneiss and Rendezvous Metagabbro) | "complexly deformed interlayered **biotite gneiss, plagioclase gneiss, amphibole gneiss and amphibolite**," principally quartz, feldspar, biotite and hornblende; distinct alternating dark-gray and white layers, magnetite "eyes" |
| **Webb Canyon Gneiss** | Archean | granitic gneiss, cut by the Mount Owen pluton |
| **Mount Owen Quartz Monzonite** | whole-rock **2,495 ± 75 Ma**; zircon **2547 ± 3 Ma** | "**non-foliated or weakly flow-foliated**," a **discordant pluton** in the central part of the range, cutting the Webb Canyon Gneiss and the layered gneisses — the light rock of the central Tetons |
| **Diabase dikes** ("the Black Dike") | undetermined; biotite in wall rock K-Ar **1,450 Ma**, suggesting emplacement during or before a 1.3–1.5 Ga thermal event | undeformed, slightly metamorphosed **tholeiitic diabase**; the Mount Moran dike is **~150 ft wide**, stands **200 ft proud of the face**, and runs six or seven miles west |
| **Paleozoic cover** | Cambrian–Permian, ~3,000–5,000 ft regionally | Flathead Sandstone, **Gros Ventre Formation (600–800 ft, green flaky shale with limestone and dolomite, including the cliff-forming Death Canyon Limestone Member)**, Gallatin, Bighorn, Darby, Madison, and up |

*A note the gazetteer should absorb:* the Rock cell for the Grand Teton
currently reads "Precambrian gneiss/granite." That is right but can be
sharpened to **"Archean layered gneiss and ~2.55 Ga Mount Owen Quartz
Monzonite, cut by diabase dikes."** And note that **the Death Canyon
Limestone**, a member of the Cambrian Gros Ventre Formation, is *named
for the Teton canyon climbers climb in* — the same kind of type-locality
coincidence that `new-mexico.md` found at Bat Cave.

**Climbing consequence.**

- **"Rock quality can range from dreamy to nightmare, all in one pitch,
  which is typical of the Tetons."** That is the honest local summary and
  it is a direct consequence of the geology: an interlayered gneiss
  complex intruded by a discordant pluton and cut by dikes has *four or
  five different rocks* on one face. The Exum and the Owen-Spalding wander
  because the good rock wanders.
- **Foliation is the enemy and the friend.** Layered gneiss splits along
  its layering. That gives ledges, flakes, and the incut holds that make
  the Grand's moderate routes possible at 5.4–5.8 — and it gives the
  detached flakes and the loose blocks on ledges.
- **The Mount Owen Quartz Monzonite is the good rock.** Non-foliated,
  massive, uniform: where routes are on the pluton, expect clean cracks,
  solid jams and reliable gear. Where they cross into gneiss, expect the
  opposite.
- **Diabase dikes are hard, dark, and structurally distinct.** The Black
  Dike stands *proud* of Mount Moran's face rather than being recessed —
  it is more resistant than the gneiss around it. Dike margins are chilled
  and jointed, which makes dike-margin cracks and chimneys.
- **Fresh fault scarps, fresh talus.** A range whose bounding fault has a
  ~2 mm/yr slip rate and Holocene scarps is a range in active
  disequilibrium. Rockfall and slab failure on the east face are
  ongoing processes, not historical ones.
- **Glacial canyons.** Death Canyon's "sheer cliffs of granite and gneiss,
  rising to more than 2,000 feet" are a **glacial trough's plucked
  walls** — steep because ice removed the joint-bounded blocks, clean for
  the same reason, and undercut at the bottom where the ice was thickest.
  Cascade Canyon is the same landform.

#### Blacktail Butte

*Unit:* limestone. **A correction and a caution:** the summit and flanks
of Blacktail Butte are described as "layered sedimentary rocks from the
**Jurassic Sundance and Gypsum Spring formations**, overlain by softer
**Miocene Teewinot Formation** deposits including white limestone,
claystone and volcanic tuff," while its "**Paleozoic base … comprises
Cambrian to Permian formations**," including the cliff-forming **Death
Canyon Limestone Member** of the Gros Ventre. The butte's beds are
**tilted westward by faulting**, and it rises about **1,000 ft** above the
valley floor to 7,688 ft.

`[Unresolved — the specific unit of the climbing crag is not named by any
source found.]` The three climbing walls (lower, middle, upper — about 20
routes, mostly 5.10–5.12) are on **limestone**; whether that is Paleozoic
(Madison or Death Canyon Member) or the **Miocene Teewinot** lacustrine
limestone is not stated anywhere read in this pass. **The distinction
matters enormously** — a Miocene lake limestone is soft, chalky and
weakly cemented, and a Mississippian platform limestone is not. The
reported climbing character (**"technical, polished, and generally
hard"**) reads more like a hard Paleozoic carbonate. **Do not print a
formation name for Blacktail Butte.**

*Climbing consequence:* whatever the unit, the operative words are
**technical and polished.** Polish on limestone means either heavy
traffic or an originally fine-grained, low-porosity micrite. Either way,
expect footwork to matter more than strength, and expect the crag to be
slick when humid.

### 3.10 The Jackson-area crags — Rodeo Wall and the Hoback corridor

*Unit:* limestone. `[Inferred from regional structure — unverified]`
**most probably Mississippian Madison Limestone carried on the upper
plate of the Absaroka thrust.** The inference rests on published
descriptions of the Snake River / Hoback canyon corridor along US-89,
which exposes **Sevier-age thrust faults — the Absaroka and Darby
thrusts — that place older Paleozoic rocks on top of younger Mesozoic
rocks**, with "the **Absaroka Thrust fault thrust Mississippian Madison
Limestone (upper plate) over Cretaceous strata**." Just west of Hoback,
the Darby fault's upper plate carries the **Permian Phosphoria
Formation** over the **Jurassic Twin Creek Limestone**. The Madison here
is "fossiliferous, thinly bedded **blue-gray limestone**"; the **Darby
Formation** is Devonian "thinly bedded gray and buff dolomite with
interbeds of gray, yellow and black shale"; the **Bighorn Dolomite** is
"thin beds of blue-gray or brown dolomite, **weathering white**, capped
by about 10 m of white dolomite."

**This is a different tectonic province from the rest of this file.**
Everything east of Jackson is Laramide basement-cored uplift; the Hoback
and Snake River canyons are in the **Sevier fold-and-thrust belt** —
thin-skinned, older (Cretaceous), with whole sheets of Paleozoic rock
shoved eastward over Mesozoic rock. The book should say so; it is the
only place in Wyoming where our crags sit on a thrust sheet.

*Climbing consequence.* Rodeo Wall is "a small limestone crag … two miles
south of Hoback Junction," "less than or near vertical," and — the
diagnostic phrase — the rock "**seems to alternate between either
polished or super-sharp**." That is the signature of a **thinly bedded**
carbonate: hard, fine-grained, low-porosity beds polish under traffic
and under water; interbedded coarser or more fossiliferous beds weather
to razor edges. On a thin-bedded limestone, expect:

- **holds that come in horizontal bands**, because the beds do;
- **bedding-plane ledges and small roofs** at bed boundaries;
- **bolts that are only as good as the bed they are in** — a thin bed
  over a shaly parting is a bad host;
- east-facing aspect (morning sun, early-afternoon shade) making it the
  hot-day and evening option the census already describes;
- and a ~10-minute approach because the crag is a road-cut-scale
  exposure on the canyon wall.

*Hoback Shield* and the other Hoback-corridor crags: `[Inferred —
unverified]` **same thrust-sheet Paleozoic carbonate**. No independent
source confirms unit or route count.

### 3.11 Cody — Shoshone Canyon rock, South Fork ice

#### Shoshone Canyon

**Structural setting.** The canyon cuts the **Rattlesnake Mountain
anticline**, a Laramide basement-cored fold whose uplift "began to form
**66 million years ago**"; the present canyon is the product of
"**erosional downcutting occurring over the past three million years**."
The Buffalo Bill Dam sits in the narrows.

**The Great Unconformity, again, and at its best.** "This is where
Precambrian metamorphic rocks that are **more than 2.7 billion years
old** are overlain by the Cambrian (**515 million-year-old**) **Flathead
Sandstone**," with "**Archean-age granite and granitic gneiss** … on
glorious display when facing east from the dam." Above it, "thin- and
thick-bedded, **tilted Paleozoic marine sandstones, shales and
limestones** formed in marine waters of the proto-Pacific from
mid-Cambrian to Pennsylvanian time," with **Madison Limestone** in the
upper canyon. Note the age discrepancy in the sources — one says the
granite at the dam is "two billion years old," another says the
Precambrian rocks are "more than 2.7 billion years old." **The 2.7 Ga
figure is consistent with the Wyoming Province and is the one to use.**

**Which crag is on which rock — and this resolves the gazetteer's vague
"limestone, granite, sandstone" cell.** A climbing source is specific:
"**the limestone crags are on the north side of the road on Rattlesnake
Mountain**, accessed by the only two right-turn options in the canyon.
**The granite areas are at the west end of the canyon near the tunnels
and deep in the canyon near the river.**" And the canyon offers
"**sandstone bouldering**, limestone and granite sport cragging, and a
variety of traditional routes **primarily on the granite**."

So, in the book's terms:
- **Limestone sport crags** — north side, on the tilted Paleozoic flank
  of the anticline; `[inferred — unverified]` **Madison Limestone** is
  the most likely cliff-former, on the same reasoning as everywhere else
  in this file.
- **Granite trad crags** — west end near the tunnels and down by the
  river, in the **Archean core** exposed by the anticline and the river.
- **Sandstone bouldering** — `[inferred — unverified]` most plausibly the
  **Cambrian Flathead Sandstone**, the one hard sandstone immediately
  above the unconformity here, though Tensleep and Mesozoic sandstones
  are also present in the section.

**Climbing consequence.** Shoshone Canyon is the whole of Wyoming
geology in three miles, and climbers can literally boulder on the
unconformity. Practically: **the granite is the trad rock and the
limestone is the sport rock**, which is the same division as Sinks
Canyon and for the same reason — crystalline rock cracks, carbonate
pockets. Expect the granite to be gneissic in places ("granite and
granitic gneiss") and therefore foliated and flaky in places. Expect the
limestone crags, on a steeply tilted anticline limb, to have **dipping
bedding** rather than horizontal — holds and ledges will run at an angle
across the wall.

#### South Fork of the Shoshone — the ice

**Unit:** the **Absaroka Volcanic Supergroup**, middle Eocene **53–43
Ma** (§1.7). "The rock is a mix between layers of **volcanic
conglomerate** … and **sandstone/shale** units"; "the mountains here are
formed from layers of volcanic debris that are **thousands of feet
thick**, and the most common type of rock found in these mountains is
**breccia**, … affectionately known as '**kitty litter**' due to its
crumbly and unstable nature."

**Why the ice is there — the best geological explanation in this file of
why an area exists at all.** "**Bands of harder horizontal stone provide
benches for ice to form on.** These horizontal stone formations act as
natural shelves where water cascading down the creeks can freeze."

That is the whole story of the South Fork. A thick pile of **flat-lying,
alternating volcaniclastic units** — welded and cemented breccias, lahar
deposits, lava flows, softer tuffaceous sandstone and shale — erodes
differentially into a **stepped cliff profile**. Every resistant band is
a lip; every lip is a waterfall; every waterfall in a cold, dry,
high-altitude Wyoming winter is an ice route. **The densest concentration
of frozen waterfalls in the continental US exists because the Absaroka
volcanic pile is layered.**

**Climbing consequence.**

- **The rock behind the ice is bad.** "Kitty litter" breccia is a matrix-
  supported volcaniclastic with weak cement. **Rock protection is poor to
  useless**, mixed climbing is often on rotten rock, and screws in thin
  ice backed by breccia are backed by nothing. This is a large part of
  why the South Fork built the reputation it has (Stan Price, Alex Lowe,
  Mark Twight, Steve House, Aaron Mulkey) — the hazard is real.
- **Ice quality follows aspect and the bench geometry.** Steps mean
  pillars and curtains rather than continuous gullies.
- **Rockfall in thaw.** A crumbly volcaniclastic cliff bound together by
  ice sheds rock as soon as the ice lets go. Warm spells are the danger.
- **"Mostly altered."** The Absaroka Supergroup is formally described as
  "a series of **mostly altered**, andesitic volcanic and volcaniclastic
  rocks." Hydrothermal alteration turns feldspar to clay; clay-altered
  volcanic rock is soft, slick when wet, and holds nothing.

### 3.12 Sheridan, Story and the eastern Bighorns

Same range as Ten Sleep, opposite flank. On the **east side of the
Bighorn uplift** the Paleozoic carbonate section is tilted eastward off
the Archean core, and the creeks draining to the Powder River Basin —
**South Piney, Tongue, Crazy Woman** — have cut canyons across it. Every
crag here is that carbonate.

#### South Piney Creek Canyon (Story Canyon)

*Unit:* **Bighorn Dolomite and Madison Limestone** — and here, unusually,
the climbing sources and the geology agree. The canyon is described as
having "striking **dolomitic limestone** cliffs" and "sweeping bands of
**Bighorn Dolomite**," in a valley of old-growth ponderosa. Bighorn
Dolomite in northern Wyoming: "**gray massive cliff-forming siliceous
dolomite and locally dolomitic limestone.**"

*Climbing consequence:* "a stunning limestone canyon with a wide array of
rock climbing routes … short approach, high quality routes with grades
from **5.3 to 5.13**" — "the preeminent sport climbing destination of the
Eastern Bighorns." A grade range starting at 5.3 means the canyon offers
low-angle as well as steep rock, which on a carbonate canyon means the
**bedding is exposed at a range of angles** — dip slopes and slabs
alongside joint-controlled vertical faces. Creekside crags in a
ponderosa canyon are **shaded, cool and slow to dry**; carbonate in a
wet canyon grows lichen and holds seeps.

#### Tongue River Canyon

*Unit:* named directly, and this is a solid finding: "**Tongue Canyon
Road passes by cliffs of Ordovician Bighorn Dolomite and Mississippian
Madison Limestone**," and "the rocks of the canyon are **dolomitic
limestone of the Madison Formation**." Both units, in one canyon, exactly
as in Ten Sleep.

*The karst is spectacular and the access is regulated by bats.* **Tongue
River Cave is in the Madison Limestone** — 106 ft deep, **1.23 miles of
passages**, with an active stream passage carrying an underground stretch
of the Little Tongue River that resurges further down the canyon. The
cave is **closed October 15 – April 15 every year to protect hibernating
bats**. Two things follow for the book: this is the same
sinking-stream karst plumbing as the Sinks at Lander; and **cave closures
and white-nose-syndrome protocols are a real access issue in Bighorn
carbonate**, distinct from the raptor closures.

*Climbing consequence:* "sport, some trad" per the census, on the same
Madison/Bighorn carbonate — expect pockets, bedding ledges, and seepage
where the karst drains.

#### Crazy Woman Canyon

*Unit:* **limestone** — "a serene **limestone canyon** with excellent
rock climbing readily accessible from the road." `[Inferred —
unverified]` **Madison Limestone and/or Bighorn Dolomite**, the same
east-flank carbonate band as Story and Tongue River. The canyon road runs
about 13 miles from the Powder River Basin at ~5,900 ft to the eastern
edge of the Bighorns at ~7,100 ft, so the road climbs *down* the section
as it goes west toward the range core; nearby **Eocene Wasatch
conglomerates (56–50 Ma)** carry an increasing volume of Precambrian
clasts, recording the unroofing of the range.

*Climbing consequence:* "What Crazy Woman Canyon lacks in size it makes
up for in the uniqueness of its geological features, quality of climbing,
and positioning right above a blue-ribbon trout stream." A short, narrow,
roadside carbonate canyon: single-pitch, quick-drying on the sunny side,
seepy on the shaded side, and with the base of every route within a few
metres of a road or a creek.

### 3.13 The "checked but not confirmed" entries — geology where geology is knowable

The gazetteer closes with three areas it declined to populate: **Baggot
Rocks**, **Glendo State Park** and **Flaming Gorge / Green River (WY
side)**. Their *climbing* status is still unconfirmed and this file does
not change that. Their *geology* is knowable, and in two cases the
geology explains the absence.

A fourth area belongs in this section for the opposite reason: **Guernsey
State Park** is named in the gazetteer's own introduction ("Fremont
Canyon and Guernsey's south-facing limestone climb through much of the
winter") but has **no table row anywhere in Part III**, and it turns out
to have a real, mapped, named route inventory. That is a gap, not a
caveat.

#### Baggot Rocks (Carbon County)

*Unit:* **the Baggot Rocks Granite** — and the crag is its **type
locality**, which is the same kind of find as Bat Cave in
`new-mexico.md`. From the National Geologic Map Database's Geolex entry:
the Baggot Rocks Granite is "a **massive to foliated granite cut by
diabase dikes and pegmatite** that has the form of a **phacolith** and
shows gradational and cross-cutting relationships to gneiss country
rock," of Precambrian age, **2340 ± 50 Ma** on seven whole-rock Rb/Sr and
zircon samples (Sutherland & Hausel 2004 classify it as
Paleoproterozoic). The type locality is "**Baggot Rocks, T. 15 N.,
R. 83 W., Carbon Co., south-central Wyoming**." In the Sierra Madre Range
it occurs as "**mildly foliated to massive quartz monzonite and
granite** along the east flank, with gradational to sharp cross-cutting
contacts with older Archean augen and quartz-biotite gneiss, and forms
**prominent rounded outcrops which contrast with the subdued topography
of the gneiss**."

*Climbing consequence, if anyone ever develops it.* "Prominent rounded
outcrops" is the tor-and-dome habit again (§3.4, §3.7) — exfoliation
slabs, crystal faces, and few continuous cracks. "Massive to foliated"
warns that the fabric changes across the body: where it is foliated it
will flake along the foliation; where it is massive it will be blank.
Diabase dikes and pegmatite give the only reliable crack systems, since
both weather differentially from the host granite. The Wyoming Game and
Fish **Encampment River–Baggot Rocks public access area** is the access
point, managed for fishing.

*Status:* **geology confirmed and well dated; climbing still
unconfirmed.** The gazetteer's caution is correct and should stand — but
the Rock column can now say **"Baggot Rocks Granite, 2.34 Ga — the unit's
type locality."**

#### Glendo State Park

*Unit:* the **Hartville uplift**, "a small mountain range with rocks
showcasing more than **2.5 billion years** of Earth's history," with "a
core of **Archean eugeoclinal metasedimentary and metavolcanic rock**,
with overlying Paleozoic sedimentary rocks that **dip off the flank of
the uplifted core**." The sedimentary section here has its own local
names: the **Guernsey Formation** (Mississippian–Devonian) and the
**Hartville Formation** (Pennsylvanian) above it, both carbonate and both
famously **cherty** — "pink, dark brown, red and gray" cherts, the source
of the agate and jasper collected around Glendo Reservoir. Fine-grained
orthoquartzites occur in the **Morrison–Cloverly** section. The WSGS has
published a Glendo State Park geology pamphlet.

*Why there may be little climbing.* `[Inference, offered as an
explanation rather than a finding]` A reservoir park set in gently
dipping, cherty, thin-bedded Mississippian–Pennsylvanian carbonate and
Cretaceous clastics is a **ledge-and-slope** landscape, not a cliff
landscape. Nothing here is the massive 500-ft cliff-former the Madison is
on the Bighorn rim. The census's finding — real park, no climbing
literature — is consistent with the rock.

*Status:* **climbing unconfirmed; leave as flagged.** The Rock column, if
one is ever wanted, is **Guernsey and Hartville Formations (cherty
Mississippian–Pennsylvanian carbonate) over an Archean core, Hartville
uplift.**

#### Flaming Gorge / Green River (Wyoming side)

*Unit:* the Wyoming side of the reservoir is in **Sweetwater County**, in
the Green River Basin, and the cliffs are **Late Cretaceous**. "The
cliffs and spires of **Firehole Canyon** are made up of **Mancos Shale,
Mesa Verde Sandstone, and bits of the Green River Formation**" — "nearly
flat-lying **shale and ledge-forming sandstones** … Mancos Shale capped
by Mesa Verde Group sandstones," producing "chimneys and pinnacles" and
"red and white cliffs."

*Why there is little climbing.* This is the clearest geological
explanation of a blank spot in the gazetteer. **Mancos Shale does not
hold up a wall**, and a landscape of soft shale capped by thin
ledge-forming sandstone erodes into **hoodoos and badlands** — visually
spectacular, structurally rotten. It is the same lithology that makes
the Bisti and the Mancos badlands of New Mexico unclimbable
(`new-mexico.md` §1.5). Add the **Green River Formation**, an Eocene
**lacustrine** oil-shale-and-marlstone unit that is thin-bedded, fissile
and weak.

*Status:* **the absence of climbing here is a rock-quality fact, not a
research gap.** The gazetteer should say so rather than merely flagging
it, and the sentence is a good one: *the Wyoming side of Flaming Gorge is
Mancos Shale under Mesaverde sandstone caps — hoodoo country, not crag
country.*

#### Guernsey State Park — a gap in Part III, with a named unit

*Unit:* **Madison Limestone** and the **Guernsey / Hartville Formations**
of the **Hartville uplift**. "Most of the rocks in Guernsey State Park
are of the **Paleozoic** group but there is a wisp of **Precambrian
metadolomite** near the very SE corner river bottom exit"; "the most
prominent features of the park are the **bluffs of limestone and
sandstone** that rise above the eastern half of the reservoir and along
the river"; and Mountain Project's *Red Clove Wall* is described as
"**perched above the Madison limestone** in the central part of the long
red wall … east and downstream of the side canyon on the north side of
the **North Platte River**." The WSGS has a Guernsey State Park geology
pamphlet (WSGS 2019-IP-15) and a Hartville Uplift cultural-geology guide.

*Named sectors found:* **The Red Clove Wall**, **Area 4: The Red Cloud
Wall**, **The Center Slab**, **The Rev Wall**; routes include *Fun Hog*
(90 ft), *Valley Forge*, *Augmented Reality*, and a *Red Zone
Project/Toprope*.

*Climbing consequence — and a genuine safety warning.* Two independent
sources say the same thing bluntly. From Mountain Project: "**much of the
climbing at Guernsey State Park is on fractured or otherwise unsound rock
and is protected by home-made anchors and bolts, requiring extreme
caution — holds and bolt placements that appear sturdy may not be.**"
From the geology side: "there are miles of canyon walls here … but **much
of the rock is quite shattered** and it often needs cleaning or your
route will simply not be fit for climbing safely."

That is exactly what a **small, tightly folded Laramide uplift** does to
brittle carbonate: the Hartville uplift is a compact structure with steep
dips, and steeply dipping, folded limestone is **fractured limestone**.
Add a cherty unit (chert shatters where limestone bends) and you get
shattered rock as a *structural* property of the area, not a local
accident.

*Recommendation:* **give Guernsey a table row in the gazetteer**, with
Rock = "**Madison Limestone and Guernsey/Hartville Formation carbonate,
Hartville uplift**," Style = "sport and toprope, home-made fixed gear,"
and a **red hazard note**: fractured rock, non-standard anchors, helmet
and judgement required. It is currently mentioned in the chapter
introduction and nowhere else, which is the worst of both worlds.
