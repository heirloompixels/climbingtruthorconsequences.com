# The Geology of Wyoming's Climbing Areas

Research notes for *Climbing Truth or Consequences*. The Wyoming volume of
the same reference work as `new-mexico.md` and `montana.md`, and it exists
to answer the same single question for every crag in the book: **why is the
rock the way it is, and what does that mean under your hands and feet?**

## Research method and limitation (read before using this file)

This research was compiled almost entirely from **WebSearch result
snippets**. `WebFetch` was tested once at the start of this run — against
`www.wsgs.wyo.gov`, the Wyoming State Geological Survey — and was
**refused by the outbound network proxy** with `EGRESS_BLOCKED`. The two
sibling agents who wrote the New Mexico and Montana volumes tested five
further hosts between them (`en.wikipedia.org`, `geoinfo.nmt.edu`,
`pubs.usgs.gov`, `mbmg.mtech.edu`, `mrdata.usgs.gov`) with the same
result. **The block is total for practical purposes; one test was
enough.** That means:

- Every WSGS report, every USGS Professional Paper and Bulletin, every
  NPS geology page and every peer-reviewed paper cited below is cited
  **from the search engine's summary of it**, not from the document
  itself. Geology summarises unusually well — the snippets quote unit
  names, thicknesses and radiometric ages verbatim — but they are
  summaries.
- **No geologic map was read.** Every statement of the form "this crag is
  on unit X" that is not directly quoted from a source is marked
  `[inferred from regional geology — unverified]`, with the inference
  named.
- Mountain Project and theCrag remain unfetchable (the standing condition
  of this project); climbers' descriptions of rock below come from search
  snippets that quote those pages, from guide services, and from
  destination articles.
- No formation name, age, or stratigraphic assignment in this file was
  invented. Where sources disagree — and on Ten Sleep they disagree
  sharply — both are given and the disagreement is flagged rather than
  averaged.

Roughly 150 web searches went into this file. The single most valuable
follow-up, if direct fetching is ever restored, would be:

1. **WSGS geologic map of the Bighorn Basin** and the USGS Tensleep /
   Leigh Creek 7.5′ quadrangles — to settle, once and for all, whether
   the Ten Sleep Canyon crags are cut in **Madison Limestone** or in
   **Bighorn Dolomite**. The climbing literature says one thing and the
   canyon's own stratigraphic column says another (§3.2). This is the
   single most consequential unresolved question in the file.
2. **USGS Bulletin 1021-I**, Robinson, *Geology of Devils Tower National
   Monument* — the primary description of the columns, their taper and
   their cross-fractures, which is exactly what a crack climber needs.
3. The **WSGS 1:500,000 Geologic Map of Wyoming** (Love & Christiansen)
   for the Lander, Sinks Canyon and Wild Iris ground — to pin which
   Madison member each Lander crag sits in.
4. **USGS Professional Paper 1201-A** and the Absaroka Volcanic
   Supergroup mapping, for the Cody crags.

## Part 1 — The statewide framework

Wyoming is the best state in the union for a climber who wants to see
*structure*. New Mexico's story is chemistry — what erupted, what
precipitated. Wyoming's story is architecture: a piece of some of the
oldest continental crust on Earth, buried under a mile and a half of
shallow-sea limestone and desert sandstone, then shoved bodily upward in
discrete blocks with the sedimentary cover peeling off their flanks, then
buried again in its own debris, then dug back out. Almost every Wyoming
crag is explained by where it sits in that sequence: **on the exhumed
Archean core of an uplift, on the tilted Paleozoic carbonate wrapped
around the core, or on something young that was punched through the pile
afterwards.**

### 1.1 The Wyoming Province: 3.5–2.5 Ga

The basement of the state is the **Wyoming Province** (or Wyoming
craton), one of the ancient nuclei of North America — "a stable Archean
craton … characterized by ancient continental crust formed primarily
between **3.5 and 2.5 billion years ago**," underlying most of Wyoming
and reaching into Montana, Idaho and South Dakota. It is exposed where
Laramide uplifts have stripped their cover: the **Beartooths, the
Bighorns, the Wind Rivers, the Tetons, the Laramie Range, the Granite
Mountains/Sweetwater Rocks**.

The province divides into three subprovinces — oldest to youngest, the
**Montana metasedimentary province**, the **Beartooth–Bighorn magmatic
zone**, and the **Southern accreted terranes**. Rocks of the first two
run to **3.5 Ga**, with detrital zircons to **4.0 Ga** and Nd model ages
beyond 4.0 Ga; the oldest dated rock in the province is a trondhjemitic
gneiss with concordant U-Pb zircon ages of **3.5 Ga**. Cratonization of
the northern subprovinces was complete by about **3.0–2.8 Ga**; crustal
growth continued on the southern margin by continental-arc magmatism and
terrane accretion at **2.68 Ga and younger**.

Two numbers to carry into the crag sections: the **Bighorn batholith**
granite that the Cambrian sea washed over is dated **2950 ± 5 Ma**, and
the layered gneiss of the Teton Range is commonly given as **~2.7 Ga**.

**What a climber gets from this:** the Wind River alpine granite, the
Teton gneiss and granite, the domes of the Sweetwater Rocks, the upper
Sinks Canyon granite, Fremont Canyon's gorge walls, and — indirectly —
every carbonate crag in the state, because it was these blocks rising
that tilted the limestone into cliffs.

### 1.2 The Cheyenne belt: where Wyoming stops being Archean

Southeastern Wyoming is not Wyoming Province. The **Cheyenne belt** is
"the tectonic suture zone between the Archean-age Wyoming craton to the
north and the Paleoproterozoic-age **Yavapai province** to the south," a
major shear zone separating Archean rocks from **1800–1600 Ma**
gneisses, formed during the **Medicine Bow orogeny, 1.78–1.74 Ga**, when
island arcs collided with the craton. Gravity profiles across it in the
Sierra Madre, Park Range and Laramie Range show a 50–100 mgal south-side
drop, meaning the Proterozoic crust south of the belt is more felsic
and/or thicker than the Archean crust north of it.

This is the same Yavapai province that floors New Mexico (see
`new-mexico.md` §1.1) — the two volumes of this reference work meet at
the Cheyenne belt. It matters for Vedauwoo and the Snowy Range, which sit
on or near it.

### 1.3 The 1.4 Ga event: Sherman Granite and the Laramie anorthosite

Around **1.44–1.43 Ga**, a distinct magmatic pulse — the same
continent-wide "anorogenic" 1.4 Ga event that gave New Mexico the Sandia
pluton — intruded southeastern Wyoming as two coeval bodies:

- the **Sherman batholith**, dated **1438–1431 Ma**, a coarse
  K-feldspar-porphyritic granite; and
- the **Laramie anorthosite complex (LAC)**, **1436–1431 Ma**, with five
  monzonitic-to-granitic A-type plutons intruded at 1.43 Ga, the youngest
  being the Red Mountain pluton.

The Sherman Granite intruded across older crust — sources describe it
cutting "a series of older (1700 Ma) metamorphic rocks" and intruding
"Paleoproterozoic crust of the Yavapai Province," while other work
records Archean rocks in the southern Laramie Range intruded at ~1.43 Ga
by both the LAC and the Sherman. In other words the Sherman batholith
stitches across the Cheyenne belt region. **Type I Sherman granite is
coarse granite with large porphyroblasts of K-feldspar (microcline) at
about 54 % of the rock**, and some phases show a flow texture with
aligned feldspar phenocrysts **up to 3 cm long** and cognate xenoliths
that can exceed 50 cm.

**What a climber gets:** Vedauwoo — and the reason Vedauwoo eats skin
(§3.4).

### 1.4 The Great Unconformity and the Paleozoic platform

Above the basement is a gap of roughly **two billion years**. In the
Bighorns, **Middle Cambrian Flathead Sandstone unconformably overlies a
deeply weathered outcrop of 2950 ± 5 Ma Archean granite**, with a fossil
grus and a paleosol developed on the granite at the contact — the **Great
Unconformity**, and one of the best places on Earth to put a hand on both
sides of it. Detrital zircons in the Flathead peak at ~1.8 Ga with a
minor ~2.7 Ga population, with far more Archean grains right at the
unconformity: the sea was reworking the hill it was drowning.

Then the platform. In north and central Wyoming the section, bottom to
top, runs:

| Unit | Age | Character | Cliffs? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Flathead Sandstone** | Middle Cambrian | dull-red quartzitic sandstone; brown-gray medium-grained cross-bedded glauconitic quartz arenite; often <25 m | a hard ledge/step, not a big wall |
| **Gros Ventre Formation** | Middle–Upper Cambrian | Wolsey Shale Member (soft green micaceous shale), **Death Canyon Limestone Member** (blue-gray/yellow mottled hard dense limestone), Park Shale Member | slope-former with one hard band |
| **Gallatin Limestone / Group** | Upper Cambrian | blue-gray and yellow mottled hard dense limestone | ledgy |
| **Bighorn Dolomite** | Ordovician | "**gray massive cliff-forming siliceous dolomite** and locally dolomitic limestone"; in the Bighorn Mountains reported at only ~**25–42 m (82–138 ft)** | yes — but a thin one |
| **Darby Formation** (northwest) / Jefferson | Devonian | dolomite and shale | ledgy |
| **Madison Limestone / Group** | Early Mississippian, ~350 Ma | **Mission Canyon Limestone** (blue-gray massive limestone and dolomite) over **Lodgepole Limestone** (gray cherty limestone and dolomite); ~**700 ft in the Bighorn Mountains**, ~150 m (490 ft) reported in Ten Sleep Canyon | **yes — the great cliff-former of Wyoming climbing** |
| **Amsden Formation** | Late Mississippian–Pennsylvanian | 150–350 ft; **Darwin Sandstone Member** at base (0–80 ft brown-gray fine cross-bedded sandstone), **Horseshoe Shale Member** (0–50 ft red slope-forming mudstone), **Ranchester Limestone Member** at top (100–200 ft) | the red recess between two cliff bands |
| **Tensleep Sandstone** | Pennsylvanian(–Early Permian) | **eolian** cross-bedded quartz sandstone; part of an erg that prograded south out of north-central Wyoming, interrupted repeatedly by marine transgressions; grainfall and wind-ripple foresets, avalanche strata rare | yes — a buff rimrock, alcoves and slots |
| **Phosphoria / Goose Egg / Park City** | Permian | phosphatic shale, carbonate, red beds | ledgy |

Two things follow from that table and they run through the rest of this
file. First, **the Madison is the reason Wyoming has sport climbing**: it
is thick, massive, mechanically strong, and it takes solution features.
Second, **the Bighorn Dolomite is genuinely thin here** — 82 to 138 feet
in the Bighorn Mountains — which is a fact that a 250-foot Ten Sleep
crag has to be reconciled with (§3.2).

### 1.5 The Ancestral Rockies and the Mesozoic

Wyoming caught the edge of the **Ancestral Rocky Mountains**: the
Tensleep erg developed "north of the Ancestral Rocky Mountains as Africa
collided with the North American continent," and further south the Front
Range and Uncompahgre highlands shed the arkose that Colorado climbs on.

The Mesozoic section above is mostly soft and mostly not climbed, but it
is the *scenery* of the state and it is what had to be stripped off the
uplifts:

- **Chugwater Formation** (Upper Triassic) — the red one. "Primarily red
  sandstone," a terrestrial sequence; the red band you see wrapping every
  Wyoming range front.
- **Sundance Formation** (Middle–Upper Jurassic) — greenish-gray
  glauconitic sandstone and shale over red and gray non-glauconitic
  sandstone and shale; a shallow marine seaway.
- **Morrison Formation** (Upper Jurassic) — conformably above the
  Sundance; olive-green lenticular calcareous siltstone and mudstone with
  white-to-buff calcareous quartz-arenites, eolian to fluvial. The
  dinosaur unit.
- **Cloverly Formation** (Lower Cretaceous) — rusty sandstone over
  brightly variegated bentonitic claystone, chert-pebble conglomerate at
  the base.
- **Frontier Formation** (Upper Cretaceous) — Western Interior Seaway
  shoreline sandstone with interbedded shale and bentonite. The Mowry,
  Thermopolis and Cody shales enclose it.

The Cloverly's bentonite and the Cretaceous shales are why Wyoming
range-front roads slump, and why the "badlands" apron below every crag
looks the way it does.

### 1.6 The Laramide orogeny: the event that made Wyoming climbable

Late Cretaceous into Eocene, flat-slab subduction of the Farallon plate
drove deformation a thousand miles inland. In Wyoming the **Laramide
orogeny** (broadly **75–55 Ma**, with sources giving 70–35 Ma for the
whole span) produced the classic **"thick-skinned"** style: "large
uplifts of Precambrian basement, commonly flanked by reverse faults,"
basement blocks moved **3–20 km along faults dipping 25°–40°**.

Thermochronology in the Wind Rivers puts cooling underway by **75 Ma**
(possibly 85 Ma), with the Green River Lakes / Fremont Peak sector
cooling by **62 Ma** and most rapidly **60–57 Ma**. The **Bighorn uplift
developed during the 75–55 Ma Laramide**.

The uplifts and the basins between them are the map of Wyoming climbing:

| Uplift | Basement exposed? | Climbing it makes |
|---|---|---|
| **Bighorn Mountains** | yes (2.95 Ga batholith core) | Ten Sleep Canyon, Story/Piney Creek, Tongue River, Crazy Woman — all on the tilted Paleozoic *flanks*, not the core |
| **Wind River Range** | yes, spectacularly | Cirque of the Towers, Deep Lake/Haystack, Gannett, Titcomb; and, on its southeastern flank, the Lander limestone |
| **Laramie Range** | yes, plus the 1.43 Ga plutons | Vedauwoo |
| **Granite Mountains / Sweetwater arch** | yes, then re-buried, then re-exhumed | Sweetwater Rocks, Lankin Dome, Devils Gate, Independence Rock |
| **Owl Creek / Washakie / Rattlesnake Mountain** | locally | Wind River Canyon; Cody's Shoshone Canyon |
| **Teton uplift (Laramide precursor)** | yes | later re-raised by normal faulting — see §1.8 |
| **Black Hills (edge, in NE Wyoming)** | in South Dakota | the frame Devils Tower was intruded into |

And the basins that took the debris: **Bighorn, Wind River, Powder
River, Green River, Laramie, Hanna, Denver-Julesburg edge**. Basin fill
matters to climbers for one reason above all — the Granite Mountains were
buried in their own basin fill and then dug back out (§1.9).

### 1.7 Eocene magmatism: the Absarokas and the Black Hills intrusions

While the Laramide was finishing, Wyoming caught two very different
pulses of magma.

**The Absaroka volcanic field.** The Absaroka Range "is the remnant of a
large volcanic field that was active for 10 million years during the
Middle Eocene, **53–43 Ma**," erupting roughly **29,000 km³** of
andesite, dacite, rhyolite and pyroclastic material and burying much of
northwest Wyoming and south-central Montana under as much as five miles
of volcanic rock. Activity ran along two subparallel trends about 165
miles long, Livingston MT to Dubois WY, and 60 miles wide. The **Absaroka
Volcanic Supergroup** is formally divided into the **Washburn Group**
(Sepulcher, Lamar River and Cathedral Cliffs Formations — andesitic and
dacitic volcaniclastic rocks) and the **Thorofare Creek and Sunlight
Groups** (volcaniclastic strata and andesite lava flows). It is
"**mostly altered**" andesitic material — a phrase to remember before
trusting a hold on it.

**The northern Black Hills intrusions.** At the other corner of the
state, a swarm of alkaline igneous bodies was emplaced into the flank of
the rising Black Hills at about **49 Ma** — phonolite, trachyte and
related alkaline rocks, unusual compositions that occur almost nowhere
else in the region. One of them is **Devils Tower** (§3.3).

### 1.8 Neogene: the Yellowstone hotspot and Teton extension

The **Yellowstone hotspot** track begins about **16.6 Ma** with the
Columbia River Basalt Group and migrates northeast along the Snake River
Plain, rhyolite-dominated, to its present position under the Yellowstone
Plateau. Its consequences for Wyoming climbing are indirect but real: the
hotspot's uplift of the Yellowstone Crescent of High Terrain both cooled
the region and funnelled moisture onto it, which is why the Absarokas,
the Tetons and the Winds were so heavily glaciated.

Extension came with it. The **Teton fault** is a normal fault on the east
side of the range; slip initiated **~13–7 Ma**, concentrated in the
**10–6 Ma** interval, with most movement in the last 2 Ma and a long-term
slip rate near **2 mm/yr**. Total relative displacement between the range
block and the Jackson Hole basin is **6–9 km (20,000–30,000 ft)**. The
Tetons are therefore a *young mountain range of very old rock*: a
Laramide-age uplift re-raised, and its east face freshly cut, by
Basin-and-Range-style extension.

### 1.9 The Great Exhumation, and why the Sweetwater Rocks look buried

Central Wyoming did something unusual. The **Granite Mountains /
Sweetwater arch** rose in the Laramide like the others, then in the late
Cenozoic **collapsed and was buried by its own basin fill and by regional
tuffaceous sediment** — the White River and, above it, the **Moonstone
Formation**, "best known for mantling the Precambrian granites of the
Sweetwater arch in central Wyoming" (K/Ar on tuff in the lower sandstone
sequence ~**17 Ma**; fission-track on the lower conglomerate ~**24 Ma**).
Then came what Wyoming geologists call **the Great Exhumation** — "a time
of widespread erosion, when much of the Tertiary fill was removed,
uncovering the old landscapes," followed by further burial and further
exhumation.

That history is legible in the rock at Lankin Dome: **Archean granite
domes emerging from their own sediment**, rounded by long subsurface
weathering before they were re-exposed (§3.11).

### 1.10 Pleistocene glaciation

The last major glaciation in the Rockies is named for Wyoming: the
**Pinedale glaciation** (MIS 2), "after the terminal moraines near the
town of Pinedale, Wyoming, on the west side of the Wind River Range."
Glaciers carved the Winds into the cirques, arêtes and horns that make
the Cirque of the Towers; the Tetons' canyons (Cascade, Death, Garnet)
are glacial troughs; and "**glaciers carved out the valley of Tensleep
Canyon within the last 250,000 years**," leaving a U-shaped valley floor
and moraine along West Tensleep Creek.

For a climber the consequences are concrete: **glacially polished slabs**
(low friction where the polish survives), **plucked, joint-controlled
back walls** (the steep clean faces), **hanging valleys and headwalls**,
**frost-riven summit blocks** (the loose rock on alpine ridges), and
**freeze–thaw as the dominant active process on every wall above about
7,000 ft** — which is most of the state.
