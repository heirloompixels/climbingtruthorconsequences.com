# The Geology of New Mexico's Climbing Areas

Research notes for *Climbing Truth or Consequences*. This file exists to
answer one question for every crag in the book: **why is the rock the way
it is, and what does that mean under your hands and feet?**

## Research method and limitation (read before using this file)

This research was compiled almost entirely from **WebSearch result
snippets**. `WebFetch` was tested three times at the start of the run —
against `en.wikipedia.org`, `geoinfo.nmt.edu` (the New Mexico Bureau of
Geology & Mineral Resources), and `pubs.usgs.gov` — and **all three were

was opened and read in full. That means:

- Every NMBGMR Geologic Tour page, every NMGS guidebook PDF, every USGS
  quadrangle pamphlet and every Open-File Geologic Map report cited below
  is cited **from the search engine's summary of it**, not from the
  document itself. The summaries are unusually good for geology — they
  quote unit names, thicknesses and radiometric ages verbatim — but they
  are summaries.
- **No geologic map was read.** Every statement of the form "this crag is
  on unit X" that is not directly quoted from a source is marked
  `[inferred from regional geology — unverified]`, with the inference
  named.
- Mountain Project and theCrag remain unfetchable (the standing condition
  of this project); climber descriptions of rock type below come from
  search snippets that quote those pages.
- No formation name, age, or stratigraphic assignment in this file was
  invented. Where two sources disagree — and several do — both numbers
  are given and the disagreement is flagged rather than averaged.

## Part 1 — The statewide framework

New Mexico is the best state in the union for a climber who wants to
understand rock, because almost every major process that makes rock is
exposed here and the sequence is legible. The Bureau of Geology divides
the state into **six geologic provinces: the Southern Rocky Mountains,
the Colorado Plateau, the Rio Grande Rift, the Basin and Range, the
Mogollon–Datil volcanic field, and the High Plains.** Nearly every
climbing area in the book sits in one of them, and knowing which one
tells you most of what you need to know about the stone before you get
out of the car.

### 1.1 Building the basement: 1.8–1.6 Ga

The floor of New Mexico is Paleoproterozoic crust that arrived in pieces.
Southwestern Laurentia grew by the accretion of two largely juvenile
crustal provinces: the **Yavapai province (1.8–1.7 Ga)** and the
**Mazatzal province (1.7–1.6 Ga)**. Assembly happened during the
**Yavapai orogeny, 1.72–1.68 Ga**, and the **Mazatzal orogeny,
1.65–1.60 Ga**; both provinces were deformed and metamorphosed at middle
crustal depths, which is why so much of New Mexico's "granite" is
properly a *gneiss*. Recent work models the Mazatzal province of New
Mexico as a continental-margin arc built on the already-assembled Yavapai
province — Mazatzal sediments deposited on 1.70 Ga Yavapai basement, then
arc collision with bivergent structures at 1.68–1.65 Ga. The
Yavapai–Mazatzal boundary is inferred to run along the northern edge of
the Jemez lineament and through the Nacimiento uplift.

**What a climber gets from this:** the oldest rock in the book —
Tres Piedras, the Picuris/Tusas quartzites, the Pecos granite, the
Zuni Mountains core, and the granite at the base of the Caballo
escarpment.

### 1.2 The 1.4 Ga granites

A second, distinct pulse of granite intruded across the Southwest around
**1.45–1.4 Ga**. The **Sandia pluton** dates to about **1.42–1.45 Ga**,
with a published radiometric age of **1453 ± 12 Ma**; the nearby **Priest
pluton** is **1456 ± 13 Ma**. Composition ranges monzogranite to
granodiorite. These have traditionally been called "anorogenic" A-type
granites, but structural studies of the Sandia pluton and its aureole
document deformation *synchronous with emplacement*, which calls the
anorogenic label into question — the tectonic discriminants read
volcanic-arc / syn-collisional or late-orogenic instead.

**What a climber gets:** the Sandias — the single biggest body of
climbable crystalline rock in the state — plus the Sandia foothills
boulders.

### 1.3 The Great Unconformity and the Paleozoic sea

Above the basement sits a gap of roughly **1.1 billion years**. In the
Sandias, 1.45 Ga granite is directly overlain by ~320 Ma Pennsylvanian
sediment. Then, between about **510 and 340 Ma**, shallow seas advanced
and retreated repeatedly across what is now southern New Mexico, laying
down a carbonate platform. In the Caballo and Fra Cristobal Mountains the
sequence runs:

| Unit | Age | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Bliss Sandstone | Late Cambrian–Early Ordovician | beach/nearshore sand; a distinctive **dark band** low on the escarpment |
| El Paso Group — Sierrite Limestone, then **Bat Cave Formation** | Early Ordovician | shallow-marine limestone; Bat Cave Fm has a lower light slope-former and an **upper darker, banded, cliff-forming** unit; biostromes and bioherms |
| Montoya Group — Cable Canyon Sandstone, **Upham Dolomite**, **Aleman Formation**, Cutter Formation | Late Ordovician | Upham is a **massive dark-gray cliff-forming dolomite, chert-poor**; Aleman is **cherty** dark-gray dolomite |
| Fusselman Dolomite | Middle Silurian | basal cross-bedded sandy dolomite, gray medium-bedded **cherty dolomite** above; ~250–300 ft; dolomitization has erased primary structures |
| Percha Shale (Percha Formation) | Late Devonian (Famennian) | greenish-gray to black shale, Ready Pay Member below, Box Member above; **named in 1907 by C.H. Gordon for exposures on Percha Creek near Hillsboro** — the type area of our own crag |
| Lake Valley Formation | Mississippian | bluish-gray limestone, dolomite and cherty limestone on a south-dipping marine ramp; Andrecito, Alamogordo, Nunn, Tierra Blanca members; type section at Lake Valley, Sierra County |
| Magdalena Group (Red House, Nakaye, Bar B; or Red House / Gray Mesa / Atrasado in the newer scheme) | Pennsylvanian | the great cliff-forming carbonate pile; in the Mud Springs Mountains ~500 m thick |
| Bursum Formation, Abo Formation | Permian (Wolfcampian) | red beds; the end of the marine record here |

Two nomenclatures are in play for the Pennsylvanian and both appear in
sources: the older **Magdalena Group** (Red House / Nakaye / Bar B), used
on the Cuchillo quadrangle map, and Lucas and coworkers' **Red House
Formation → Gray Mesa Formation (Elephant Butte, Whiskey Canyon, Garcia
Members) → Atrasado Formation**. Do not treat the two as contradictions;
they are different subdivisions of the same rock.

### 1.4 The Ancestral Rocky Mountains

In the **Pennsylvanian**, the collision of North America with
South America–Africa (the Ouachita–Marathon orogeny) reached inland and
broke the craton into intracratonic block uplifts and deep basins — the
**Ancestral Rocky Mountains**. In New Mexico the major positive element
was the **Pedernal uplift** running north–south through the centre of the
state; the **Joyita uplift** was a smaller high to the northwest; the
**Zuni–Defiance uplift** rose in the west; the **Orogrande Basin** sank
west of the Pedernal and took on ~1,600 m of Pennsylvanian and Lower
Permian fill; the Permian Basin lay east. The Fra Cristobal and Caballo
Mountains sat on the **northwestern margin of the Orogrande Basin**.

The **Sandia Formation** records the first stage of marine deposition
(90–320 ft thick, sediment largely off the Pedernal uplift); the
**Madera Group** above it is the main marine sequence. In the Sandias
these two make the **300–315 Ma limestone cap** that climbers see as "a
half-pitch-thick icing of white limestone" on top of a granite face.

### 1.5 Mesozoic: dunes, then the Western Interior Seaway

The **Zuni Sandstone** — eolian dune sand of the **Middle Jurassic,
~165–175 Ma** — forms the great eastern escarpment at El Malpais, cliffs
up to 400 ft, capped by Cretaceous rock.

In the **Late Cretaceous**, the **Western Interior Seaway** ran from the
Gulf of Mexico to the Arctic. Its shoreline sands are the **Dakota
Sandstone** (Cenomanian, ~100–95 Ma; in west-central New Mexico
subdivided into the Encinal Canyon, Oak Canyon, Cubero and Paguate
members with intertonguing Mancos shale tongues). Offshore muds are the
**Mancos Shale** (~95–80 Ma, up to 3,000 ft). Later shoreline sand
progrades back as the **Gallup Sandstone**, which intertongues seaward
with the Mancos and landward with the coastal-plain **Crevasse Canyon
Formation**.

**What a climber gets:** every sandstone climb in the book. Roy and
Philmont on Dakota; Mentmore on Gallup; El Malpais on Zuni.

### 1.6 The Laramide orogeny, ~75–40 Ma

Late Cretaceous into Paleogene, flat-slab subduction of the Farallon
plate drove basement-cored uplifts and intervening basins far inland —
the **Laramide orogeny**, roughly **70–40 Ma** (some sources 75–40 Ma).
In New Mexico this raised much of the **Sangre de Cristo Range**, the
**Nacimiento uplift**, the Archuleta arch, and produced the monoclines
that fold the Colorado Plateau's sedimentary cover. It also stripped a
reported ~20,000 ft of Cretaceous and older cover off the highest Zuni
Mountains, re-exposing Proterozoic basement. Laramide uplift is why the
Cimarron Range and Philmont's high country exist.

### 1.7 The mid-Tertiary ignimbrite flare-up and the Mogollon–Datil field

This is the single most important event for New Mexico climbing.

Removal of the Farallon slab and asthenospheric upwelling drove a
continent-scale **ignimbrite flare-up (~40–18 Ma)** across the Southwest.
Its New Mexico expression is the **Mogollon–Datil volcanic field**, a
**40,000 km²** province of andesitic-to-rhyolitic lavas, tuffs, domes and
calderas active **~40–24 Ma**; the flare-up proper in the eastern and
central field ran **ca. 36.5–24.0 Ma**, with an initial episode of at
least **15 eruptions between 36.5 and 33.8 Ma** totalling a minimum
~**1,350 km³**. Caldera formation started near Socorro about **32 Ma**
and migrated southwest. Today the field is "dominated by massive cliffs
of ash-flow tuffs and exposures of shallow batholiths."

Named calderas and units that matter to this book:

| Caldera / centre | Age | Signature unit | Climbing it makes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Emory caldera**, southern Black Range near Hillsboro Peak | ~34–35 Ma | **Kneeling Nun Tuff**, >900 km³, VEI 8 | **City of Rocks**; the caprock of the Hillsboro/Kingston country |
| **Organ caldera & batholith**, Las Cruces | cauldron fill + batholith **33.7–32.8 Ma**; Organ Needle Quartz Monzonite reported at ~36 Ma; Sugarloaf Peak Quartz Monzonite **34.66–33.46 Ma** | Organ Needle Quartz Monzonite | **Organ Mountains**, Aguirre Springs, Rabbit Ears |
| **Doña Ana caldera**, north of Las Cruces | ~36 Ma | **Doña Ana Rhyolite** ash-flow tuff, beds to 440 m | **Doña Ana Mountains**, Checkerboard Wall, Pizza & Bear Boulders |
| **Socorro caldera** | ~32.5 Ma | **Hells Mesa Tuff** (~33 Ma, crystal-rich, ~42% crystals); **La Jencia Tuff** (~30 Ma, to 1,700 ft thick); **Luis Lopez Formation** andesite porphyry lavas as caldera fill | **The Box** |
| **Nogal Canyon caldera**, southern San Mateo Mtns | **28.4 Ma** | **Vicks Peak Tuff** (28.5 Ma), a **densely welded, crystal-poor, quartz-poor rhyolite ash-flow tuff**, 0–800 ft (locally >490 m); overlain by **Springtime Canyon Formation** rhyolite/quartz latite | **Luna Park, Red Rock Arroyo** |
| **Bear Trap Canyon caldera**, San Mateo Mtns | **24.4 Ma** | later ash flows and rhyolite lavas | — |
| **Datil Group**, Datil Mountains | Oligocene | tuff of Rock House Canyon, tuff of Blue Canyon, Dog Springs volcanic complex | **Enchanted Tower** |

Underlying all of it in the southwest quadrant is the **Rubio Peak
Formation** — a thick middle-to-late **Eocene** sequence of **andesitic
to dacitic lava flows with interbedded volcaniclastic sediment** that
laps onto older rocks and *underlies every younger volcanic unit* in the
Hillsboro country. Above it come the **Sugarlump Tuff**, the **Kneeling
Nun Tuff** and the **Mimbres Peak Rhyolite**. Around Kingston the mapped
succession is exactly that: Sugarlump, Kneeling Nun, Mimbres Peak.

### 1.8 The Rio Grande rift

Beginning roughly when the flare-up was winding down, the crust started
pulling apart. Extension began **earlier in the south (~36 Ma)** than in
the north (**~26 Ma**), peaked **10–16 Ma**, and continues today. In the
southern rift, extension is dated from about **27 Ma** by the eruption of
the voluminous **Uvas basalts**. The mechanism is the Colorado Plateau
rotating away from the High Plains; the geometry is a chain of
**half-grabens**, each tilted toward its master fault, separated by
accommodation zones, and flanked by **rift-flank uplifts** — the Sandias
being the classic example, and the Caballo Mountains ours.

Rift basins filled with the **Santa Fe Group**; in Sierra County the
upper part is the **Palomas Formation** (Pliocene–middle Pleistocene,
bulk **3.5–2 Ma**, base possibly 4.5 Ma), the unit most Truth or
Consequences water wells are drilled into, including the hot-springs
wells. Early unroofing of the **Caballo rift-flank uplift** is recorded
in the **Hayner Ranch Formation, ~25–16 Ma**.

The rift also plumbs the heat: Truth or Consequences' hot springs sit in
the **Palomas Basin** near its boundary with the **Engle Basin**, where
several faults intersect and let deep groundwater rise fast enough to
reach the surface at 98–115 °F carrying ~2,700 ppm dissolved minerals.
Keep that in mind at the Tufa Wall (§2.2).

### 1.9 The Jemez lineament and the Valles caldera

The **Jemez lineament** is a northeast-trending zone of young volcanism
that is the surface expression of a Precambrian basement structure. Where
it crosses the western margin of the Rio Grande rift, it built the Jemez
volcanic field. Two caldera-forming eruptions produced the **Bandelier
Tuff**: the **Otowi Member, 1.61 Ma**, from the Toledo caldera, and the
**Tshirege Member, 1.25 Ma**, from the Valles caldera, separated by 1–2 m
of **Tsankawi Pumice**. The tuff is pumice, ash, quartz and sanidine
crystals and lithic fragments, welded to varying degrees, and it caps the
Pajarito Plateau.

Beneath it, and forming the rim of White Rock Canyon, is the older
**Cerros del Rio volcanic field** — alkali basalt, basaltic andesite and
hawaiite, with maars and scoria cones, principal activity **~2.3–2.8 Ma**
(commonly quoted as ~2.5–2.6 Ma), erupted 3–1 Ma.

### 1.10 Basin and Range and the far south

South of Las Cruces the rift merges into the **Basin and Range**: Neogene
east–west extension, a normal-fault landscape of north–south ranges and
wide basins, some of them synclinal-horst geometry (e.g. the Jornada
horst near Las Cruces, Tertiary volcanics over Permian sediment, buried
by upper Santa Fe Group). The Organ Mountains are the textbook uplifted
fault block on the east side of the Mesilla Basin.

Also in the far south, and unrelated to the ignimbrites: the **Cornudas
Mountains**, a group of **Eocene–Oligocene nepheline syenite and syenite
laccoliths and plugs with phonolite and syenite sills and dikes**,
intruded into Permian and Cretaceous sediment in two pulses,
**37.14–34.5 Ma** and **32.48–26.95 Ma**.

### 1.11 Quaternary volcanism

New Mexico is still erupting on geologic time. The **Raton–Clayton
field** began ca. **9.2 Ma**. The **Zuni–Bandera field** is Quaternary,
with the **Bandera flow at 11.2 ka** and the **McCartys flow at 3.9 ka** —
the youngest volcanism in the state. The **Carrizozo flow field** dates
to **4.8–5.2 ka**, the second youngest. Basalt this fresh is what makes
El Malpais a lava field with sandstone bluffs on its edge rather than a
canyon.

Farther north, the **Taos Plateau volcanic field** is the largest
volcanic field in the rift: >7,000 km², at least 35 vents, ~300 km³
erupted, of which **66% is Servilleta Basalt (olivine tholeiite),
12% olivine andesite, 17% dacite, 3% trachybasalt/trachyandesite and <1%
rhyolite**. The **Servilleta Basalt is 5.3–2.95 Ma** (a tighter
Ar/Ar study gives 5.3–3.3 Ma) and reaches **240 m thick in the Rio
Grande gorge**, where the river has cut through it. That single fact —
stacked sheet flows, three main layers separated by sediment — is the
whole architecture of Taos climbing.

And in the northwest, the **Navajo (Diné) volcanic field**: **minette**
(alkali feldspar + biotite/phlogopite + diopside) diatremes and radiating
dikes, ~30 Ma, of which Shiprock is the type example.

## Part 2 — Truth or Consequences: the home crags

Sierra County is a small stratigraphic miracle. In under an hour's drive
you can touch Lower Ordovician sea-floor limestone, Eocene andesite from
a volcano that no longer exists, and Oligocene ash from a caldera 50
miles away. The county straddles the boundary between the **Rio Grande
rift** (the valley, the Palomas Basin, the hot springs) and the
**Mogollon–Datil volcanic field** (everything west and north). The rule
of thumb the book already uses — *limestone east of the river, volcanic
west and north* — is geologically exact, and the reason is that the rift
tore open along the western edge of an old Paleozoic carbonate platform
and then buried the western half under ash.

### 2.1 Mud Mountain

**Coordinates and setting.** The summit named Mud Mountain in Sierra
County sits at roughly **33.1565 N, −107.3081 W, 5,728 ft** (also
reported 5,749 ft), on the USGS **Cuchillo** 7.5′ quadrangle, about two
miles north of Williamsburg. The climbers' approach matches exactly:
I-25 Exit 75 (Williamsburg), past the Sierra County fairgrounds, onto
Mud Canyon Road, under the interstate, over a dam, 1.4 miles to a cairned
arroyo, then up onto the mesa to a water tank.

**Geologic unit — the correction.** The gazetteer and crag page both say
only "Limestone." That is right as far as it goes, and it can be
sharpened considerably. Mud Mountain lies at the southern end of the
**Mud Springs Mountains**, a small (~8 km N–S × 3 km E–W, under 26 km²,
max relief ~357 m) **northeast-tilted fault block** immediately northwest
of Truth or Consequences — Williamsburg is described in the literature as
"3½ miles south of the Mud Springs Mountains," and Mud Canyon Road is the
range's access road. The block is **Proterozoic basement overlain by a
Paleozoic section almost 1 km (~3,660 ft) thick, mainly limestones,
dolomites and shales**, with reddish fluvial mudstone and sandstone at
the top. Crucially, **the Silurian (Fusselman) and Mississippian (Lake
Valley) are absent here** — unlike the Caballos across the river. The
lower-middle Paleozoic succession is 377 m: **Bliss Formation (54 m)**;
**El Paso Group** — Hitt Canyon Fm (140 m) and McKelligon Fm (36 m);
**Montoya Group** — Cable Canyon Sandstone (6 m), Upham (19 m), Aleman
(46 m), Cutter (39 m). Above that sits the **Devonian Percha Formation**
(the Mud Springs Mountains are among its northernmost outcrops) and then
nearly **500 m of Pennsylvanian**: the **Red House Formation** (~56–59 m,
Atokan, slope-forming) and the **Gray Mesa Formation** — whose
**Whiskey Canyon Member is "mostly cliff-forming, cherty limestone"**
(early Desmoinesian), with the Elephant Butte Member (interbedded
limestone and shale) below and the Garcia Member above. The Cuchillo
quadrangle map (Maxwell & Oakman, USGS GQ-1686, 1990) shows Magdalena
Group units — **Bar B, Nakaye, Bursum Formations** — across this ground.

`[Inferred from regional geology — unverified]` **The four walls at Mud
Mountain are most likely cut in Pennsylvanian cliff-forming limestone of
the Magdalena Group — the Whiskey Canyon Member of the Gray Mesa
Formation is the single best candidate — with Ordovician El Paso and
Montoya carbonates possible lower on the block.** The inference rests on
(a) the crag's position within the Mud Springs Mountains fault block,
(b) that block's published section, (c) the identification of the Whiskey
Canyon Member as the range's principal cliff-former, and (d) the reported
sport-crag height (single-pitch walls, not 300-ft escarpments). It is
**not** confirmed by a map read directly. Do not print a formation name
in the guidebook until GQ-1686 is opened.

**Lithology and climbing consequence.** Climbers describe Mud Mountain as
"**variably sharp limestone**" with **pockets** and, on the route
*Jasper*, "very thin, technical climbing on **orange limestone**." Both
of those read as classic weathered platform carbonate: dissolution
pockets where slightly acidic rainwater has enlarged flaws along bedding
and joints, sharp grey where the surface is freshly exposed, orange where
iron oxide has stained a weathering rind. Chert nodules in the
Pennsylvanian and Ordovician carbonate here will show as harder,
sometimes glassy knobs that stand proud of the limestone as it dissolves
around them — good holds, occasionally razor-edged ones.

Expect: **bedding-plane roofs and ledges**, since carbonate platform rock
is layered and the layers vary in resistance; **solution pockets and
small huecos** rather than continuous cracks — hence a pure sport crag
with bolts; **a south-facing main wall (Winter Wall) that bakes**, which
is the whole reason this is a winter crag; and **loose rock, explicitly
called out**: "since this area is new, some climbs still have loose rock
and care should be taken to protect people at the base of the cliffs." A
freshly developed limestone crag sheds its weathering rind for several
seasons. **Wear a helmet at the base.** Bolts hold well in sound
platform limestone; they hold badly in the soft, punky orange weathering
rind, which is one more reason to trust the developer's plaque-marked
lines rather than improvising.

**Naming ambiguity, resolved as far as the record allows.** The
`further-leads` page asks whether "Mudspring / Mud Springs Mountains" is a
different range from "Mud Mountain." The answer that the geologic
literature supports: **Mud Springs Mountains is the range; Mud Mountain
is a named summit at its southern end.** They are not two names for the
same feature and they are not two separate ranges. `[Inferred —
unverified]`: the inference rests on the published range dimensions and
position relative to Williamsburg versus the summit's mapped coordinates.
A single look at the Cuchillo quad settles it.

### 2.2 Caballo Lake and the Bat Cave — the best find in this file

**This crag is the type-area landmark of a named geologic formation, and
the formation is named after the cave the climbers climb in.**

The **Bat Cave Formation** is the upper formation of the **El Paso
Group**, **Early Ordovician**, raised to that status by **Kelley and
Silver (1952)** in their work on the Caballo Mountains, which
distinguished a lower **Sierrite Limestone** and an upper **Bat Cave**
unit. The formation's type section is **on the north side of Flordillo
Canyon opposite the Sierrite Mine, Caballo Mountains, Sierra County**,
and — quoting the National Geologic Map Database's Geolex entry — the
unit is "named from **Bat Cave whose opening is a prominent landscape
mark on a sheer cliff in the upper part of the unit**, a few hundred
yards northwest of the type section." At the type section the formation
is "a lower light-colored slope-forming unit and an upper somewhat darker
and banded **cliff-forming** unit," the lower carrying biostromes and
bioherms in thin- to medium-bedded limestone. Thickness ranges from a
fraction of a foot to over 1,000 ft. It underlies the Cable Canyon
Sandstone of the Montoya Group and overlies the Sierrite Limestone.

The identification is circumstantial but very strong:

- Both the geologists' Bat Cave and the climbers' Bat Cave are in the
  **Caballo Mountains, Sierra County**, on the **west-facing** side above
  the Rio Grande / Caballo Lake.
- The climbers' approach runs I-25 Exit 59 (Arrey–Derry / Caballo &
  Percha State Parks) → NM 187 → a **single-lane tunnel under the
  highway** → a **fork at the dam** → **about 3.5 miles** further → a
  rough 4×4 road. Caballo Dam sits at roughly 32.90 N; the **Sierrite
  Mine and Cable Canyon** are on the west face a few miles up-range. The
  distance and bearing fit.
- Mountain Project warns climbers about **mine claims** on the approach
  and to stay on public land. The Sierrite Mine is right there, and
  **Bat Cave itself was mined for bat guano**.
- The climbers' description — "a cave starting roughly 30 feet off the
  ground, perhaps 100 feet tall, capped by a triangular roof as much as
  30 feet deep," reached by a **30-foot ladder** — matches "a prominent
  landscape mark on a sheer cliff." A fixed ladder into a guano working
  is exactly what one would expect.

`[Strong identification, not formally confirmed]` **The Bat Cave the
guidebook describes is, on the evidence, the Bat Cave of the Bat Cave
Formation — the cliff of Lower Ordovician El Paso Group limestone from
which the unit takes its name.** Confirming this needs either the
Mountain Project map page's GPS or NMBGMR Memoir 49. If it holds, it is
the best single sentence in Part I of the book.

**The wider stratigraphy of those walls.** The Caballo Mountains are an
**east-dipping fault block**, the rift-flank uplift on the east side of
the southern Rio Grande depression, with **Proterozoic granite,
amphibolite and gneiss exposed low on the rugged western side** and
Paleozoic carbonate forming the crest. The escarpment reads, bottom to
top: reddish **Proterozoic granite** → a dark band of **Cambrian Bliss
Sandstone** → **gray cliffs of Ordovician, Silurian and Mississippian
carbonate** → ledgy **Pennsylvanian** carbonate and shale capping the
range. Local relief averages about 2,000 ft, and the range preserves
**3.5 miles (5.6 km) of section representing every period except the
Triassic and Jurassic** — extraordinary for southern New Mexico. Note
that unlike the Mud Springs Mountains, the Caballos **do** carry the
Silurian **Fusselman Dolomite** and the Mississippian **Lake Valley
Formation**.

The named sectors are strung along that escarpment and are therefore
probably not all in one unit. `[Inferred — unverified]` The Bat Cave and
the walls immediately around it should be **Bat Cave Formation (upper El
Paso Group)**; sectors higher on the hill could be in the **Montoya
Group** — the **Upham Dolomite**, whose type locality is *near the
Sierrite Mine at the head of Cable Canyon*, is "a **massive dark-gray
cliff-forming dolomite**," and the **Aleman Formation**, whose type
section is *on the north side of Cable Canyon opposite the Sierrite
Mine*, is **cherty dark-gray dolomite**. Both type sections are in the
immediate neighbourhood of the climbing.

**Climbing consequence, unit by feature.**

- **"Great to pretty bad onion skin."** Onion skin is the climbers' name
  for a case-hardened weathering rind that has separated from the parent
  rock. It is characteristic of desert carbonate: dissolution and
  reprecipitation of calcite at the surface builds a hard shell, thermal
  cycling and salt weathering pop it loose. It sounds hollow and it takes
  body weight until it doesn't. Test everything; do not trust a bolt
  placed through a rind rather than into fresh rock.
- **Chert.** The Aleman Formation is defined by its chert. Where a cherty
  dolomite weathers, silica bands stand out as knobs and blades. Sharp
  holds, hard on the fingers, and unyielding to a drill bit.
- **Dolomite versus limestone.** Dolomitized rock is generally harder,
  more brittle, and less prone to the deep solution pocketing of pure
  limestone; the Fusselman's dolomitization "has erased many sedimentary
  structures." That is consistent with a crag that gives features and
  small huecos rather than the Mediterranean-style pocket festival.
- **The Villain Wall** (which our pages do not yet list) is described as
  "generally pretty solid, and littered with features and **small
  huecos**," with "some of the **sharp grey limestone** and less of the
  **orange limestone** that some of the other walls have." The route
  *Catwoman's Claw* "climbs beautiful **brown crystal-filled pods**." All
  three textures are diagnostic of carbonate: sharp grey = fresh, orange
  = iron-stained rind, crystal-filled pods = vugs lined with secondary
  calcite.
- **The Tufa Wall.** A real geologic feature, not a borrowed name — "a
  short 70–80 foot yellow/orange wall … with several short 20-foot tall
  **tufas** that give the cliff its namesake." Tufa is calcium carbonate
  precipitated from **groundwater emerging at the cliff face**, the same
  chemistry that builds the travertine at Truth or Consequences' hot
  springs 20 miles north and at Sitting Bull Falls. `[Inferred —
  unverified]` its presence implies a **seep or perched spring** feeding
  the wall, which in turn implies the wall **can be wet**, that the tufas
  are young and hollow, and that their bases are the weak point. Tufa is
  climbable but detachable; bolts into tufa rather than into the parent
  wall are not trustworthy.
- **Aspect.** West-facing means afternoon sun — the fall-through-spring
  season the book already gives is exactly right, and it is a geologic
  accident: the Caballo block is tilted east, so its steep face looks west
  across the rift at the reservoir.

### 2.3 Percha Creek, Hillsboro

**The name is a geologic landmark in itself.** The **Percha Shale** was
named by **C.H. Gordon in 1907 for exposures at Percha Creek**, with a
type section designated by **F.V. Stevenson in 1945** near Hillsboro,
divided into the lower **Ready Pay Member** (dark fissile and calcareous
shales) and the upper **Box Member** (thicker, more calcareous shale and
limestone). It is Late Devonian (Famennian) and it carries a fish fauna.
It is *not* what the climbing is on — shale does not make crags — but it
means the guidebook can say, truthfully, that the crag stands on the
creek that gave a formation to North American stratigraphy.

**Rock type — resolving andesite vs. basalt.** The crag page notes that
most sources say andesite and at least one says basalt. That second
source is Sierra County's own tourism page, which calls it a "small
**basalt** crag with a series of moderate traditional and sport climbs."
The geology gives grounds to prefer **andesite**, and to name a probable
unit:

- The **Hillsboro 7.5′ quadrangle** (NMBGMR OFGM-242) reports that the
  **Rubio Peak Formation underlies all younger volcanic units** here, and
  that it is a **thick sequence of andesitic to dacitic lava flows and
  interbedded volcaniclastic sediments**, **middle to late Eocene** by
  radiometric age.
- Above it: **Sugarlump Tuff**, then the **35.3 Ma Kneeling Nun Tuff**
  from the Emory caldera to the west (**"well exposed in this part of the
  southern Rio Grande rift"**), then **Mimbres Peak Rhyolite** around
  Kingston.
- The same quad report notes **lacustrine sediment (Tss) exposed near the
  Percha Creek narrows** and **basalt flows exposed south of the Percha
  Creek narrows** — so there *is* mapped basalt in the drainage, just not
  necessarily at the crag.
- The wider Hillsboro district is characterised as dominated by
  "**Cretaceous andesite flows, breccias, and volcaniclastic rocks
  erupted from a volcano**," which is the Copper Flat system; the mapped
  Rubio Peak is Eocene.

`[Inferred from regional geology — unverified]` **The Percha Creek crag
is most likely cut in andesitic lava or volcaniclastic rock of the Eocene
Rubio Peak Formation.** The inference rests on: the crag's location on
Percha Creek immediately west of Hillsboro under the NM-152 bridge; the
Rubio Peak being the mapped andesite unit that underlies everything
younger there; and the climbers' consistent use of "andesite." The
competing possibilities, both mapped nearby, are the **younger basalt
flows south of the Percha narrows** (which would vindicate the "basalt"
source) and the **Kneeling Nun Tuff** (which nobody has called it). A
single reading of OFGM-242's map would end the argument; it is the
highest-value unread document in this file.

**Climbing consequence.** Andesite lava is intermediate in silica: more
viscous than basalt, less than rhyolite. Practically:

- **It cracks.** Percha Creek is the only crag in the county with real
  crack climbing, and that is consistent with a jointed lava or a
  massive volcaniclastic body cut by a stream. Cooling joints in
  andesite are typically blockier and less regular than basalt's
  hexagonal columns — you get **dihedrals, thin corners and finger
  cracks**, not Indian Creek splitters. The recovered route *Nutcracker*
  is described as "an excellent line leading up through an intimidating
  **thin dihedral**, and ending on a long **finger-crack/corner**." That
  is exactly the fracture style intermediate lava gives.
- **Blocks.** Jointed lava sheds blocks along joint faces. The base is a
  creek bottom choked with willows; anything that comes off lands in it.
- **Seepage and shade.** The crag is a **creek bottom under a bridge**;
  "most of the cliff receives direct sun but there are numerous willows
  to provide shade." Cool, damp, vegetated — expect lichen, expect
  slower drying than the desert limestone, and expect the season to be
  the opposite of Mud Mountain's.
- **Age of the climbing.** The crag has been "climbed since the 1980s,"
  which is consistent with the very low Mountain Project area ID the
  research already noted.

### 2.4 Luna Park — pinned to a unit

**This is the file's most useful correction to a published page.** The
crag page currently reads `rock = "Volcanic (specific type unrecorded)"`
and calls rhyolite-or-tuff "a guess, not a finding." It can now be
sourced.

**Rock type.** A climbing source states plainly: Luna Park is "a
well-established climbing area **just south of the San Mateo Mountains**
with many trad, sport, and mixed climbs on **rhyolite**," grades 5.6–5.12;
another that it lies "**eight miles northeast of Monticello**" with "fun
moderate climbing on moderate quality volcanic rock."

**Location and land.** Luna Park Campground is at approximately
**33.496 N, −107.415 W, 6,837 ft**, in the **Cibola National Forest,
Magdalena Ranger District (South)**. **This corrects the crag page and
the census both**, which say "likely Gila National Forest." It is Cibola,
and the district is Magdalena — the same district that administers the
San Mateo Mountains.

**Geologic setting.** Those coordinates put the crag on the southern
flank of the **San Mateo Mountains**, an "isolated, north-trending,
eastward-tilted structural block in the northeastern portion of the
Mogollon–Datil volcanic field," and within reach of the **Nogal Canyon
caldera (28.4 Ma)**, whose fill and outflow dominate the southern range.
The upper felsic sequence there is chiefly the **Vicks Peak Tuff** —
**"a thick, densely to partially welded rhyolite ash-flow tuff,"
crystal-poor and quartz-poor, 28.5 Ma, 0–800 ft thick** — overlain by the
**Springtime Canyon Formation** (rhyolite, quartz latite and latite flows
and tuffs erupted along the eastern caldera boundary). Older beneath is
the **Spears Formation**: andesitic to latitic flows, breccias, ash-flow
tuff and volcaniclastic sandstone. To the immediate east, the **Monticello
graben** (early Miocene) carries the Cañada Alamosa, and the cliffs at
**Monticello Box** are formed by a mapped unit called the **andesite of
Monticello Box**; the regional sequence there is "an older sequence of
andesite, lahar and latite (>38–36 Ma) followed by a younger sequence of
**ash-flow tuffs and rhyolite lavas (~29–22 Ma)** associated with the
Nogal Canyon (28.4 Ma) and Bear Trap Canyon (24.4 Ma) calderas."

`[Inferred from regional geology — unverified]` **Luna Park's rhyolite is
most likely the Vicks Peak Tuff or a closely associated Springtime Canyon
rhyolite/quartz latite of the Nogal Canyon caldera system, ~29–28 Ma.**
The inference rests on the crag's coordinates within the southern San
Mateo Mountains, the published dominance of Vicks Peak Tuff in that
sector, and the climbers' identification of the rock as rhyolite. The
relevant published sheet appears to be the **Sierra Fijardo 7.5′
quadrangle (NMBGMR GM-87)**, which covers the Vicks Peak area in Sierra
and Socorro Counties — unread here.

**Climbing consequence.** A densely welded, crystal-poor rhyolite
ash-flow tuff behaves very differently from the soft Bandelier tuff of
the Jemez:

- **Welding controls everything.** In a single cooling unit the base is
  poorly welded and crumbly, the middle is densely welded and hard, and
  the top devitrifies and re-crystallises. Cliff bands form where the
  densely welded zone outcrops; crumbly bands mark the poorly welded
  tops and bottoms. **Expect the quality of the rock to change with
  height on the wall, not randomly.**
- **Crystal-poor** means few phenocryst knobs to pull on. Holds come
  instead from **flattened pumice (fiamme) weathering out as pockets and
  slots**, from **lithophysae and vapour-phase cavities**, and from
  jointing. Reported features — "three moderates at 5.7–5.8 on a longer
  bolted **flat face**," longer lines on a **buttress left of the slot** —
  read as a jointed, blocky welded-tuff outcrop with faces separated by
  cooling and unloading joints.
- **Friction is generally excellent** on welded tuff (its grain contacts
  have unusually high friction angles) but the surface can be gritty and
  shed sand until traffic cleans it.
- **Bolts hold well in densely welded tuff and badly in poorly welded
  tuff.** The reported "bolted anchors with chains" throughout suggests a
  developer working in the good band.
- **Season.** Mid-March to May and September to mid-November, per the
  page — a 6,800 ft crag on forest land, cold in winter, storm-prone in
  the monsoon. Forest roads here wash out; the "final mile requires
  high-clearance vehicles."

### 2.5 Red Rock Arroyo — the blank page can be partly filled

The crag page says the rock type is unrecorded and that "red rock" is a
guess. A source now speaks directly to both the rock and the setting:
the area "consists of **several smaller crags both uphill and downhill of
Luna Park**, northeast of Monticello, off **Forest Service road #225
within the Cibola National Forest**," reached via FR #139 from Monticello
to the south or from the I-25 west frontage road ~5 miles south of Exit
100 to the north — **and "the volcanic rock can be super (darker red) to
rubbish (lighter buff)."**

That single sentence does three things. It confirms the rock is
**volcanic**; it confirms the name is descriptive — the **good rock is
literally the red rock**; and it identifies the failure mode — the
**lighter buff rock is the rubbish**.

`[Inferred from regional geology — unverified]` **Red Rock Arroyo is on
the same rhyolitic ash-flow tuff / rhyolite lava sequence as Luna Park —
Nogal Canyon caldera system, Vicks Peak Tuff and associated units —
and the red/buff quality split most likely tracks welding and
devitrification within a cooling unit** (dark red = densely welded,
oxidised, vapour-phase recrystallised; light buff = poorly welded, still
vitric and friable). Comparable red lithophysal zones are documented
nearby: at Chupadera Peak near Socorro, "60 m-high craggy cliffs mark an
unusually thick and laterally restricted **dusky red lithophysal zone in
the basal densely welded La Jencia Tuff**." The inference rests on
shared road network, shared forest and district, adjacency to Luna Park,
and the standard welding zonation of ignimbrites. It is not a mapped
determination.

**Also correct the land manager on this page:** Red Rock Arroyo is
**Cibola National Forest**, not "likely Gila." The `land` front-matter
field should change.

**Climbing consequence.** Read colour as rock quality. Climb the red,
distrust the buff. On buff, poorly welded tuff, holds snap, bolts spin
in their holes over time, and the rock gets softer when wet.

### 2.6 The county's setting, for the Part I introduction

If Part I ever wants a paragraph of geologic scene-setting, this is it:

Truth or Consequences sits in the **Palomas Basin of the Rio Grande
rift**, on Pliocene–Pleistocene **Palomas Formation** basin fill, where
intersecting faults let hot water up from depth. To the **east**, the
**Caballo Mountains** are the rift's flank uplift — an east-tilted block
whose broken western edge exposes 3.5 miles of section from Proterozoic
granite through Paleozoic carbonate, and whose cliffs give the county its
limestone. To the **northwest**, the **Mud Springs Mountains** are a
smaller, northeast-tilted block of the same Paleozoic platform, minus the
Silurian and Mississippian. To the **west and north**, everything is
Cenozoic volcanic: **Eocene Rubio Peak andesite** at Hillsboro under the
**Kneeling Nun Tuff** off the Emory caldera, and **Oligocene rhyolite
ash-flow tuff** off the Nogal Canyon caldera at Luna Park. The whole
county is a cross-section through the collision of an old sea floor and a
young rift.

## Part 3 — Area by area, the rest of the state

### 3.1 Northern New Mexico / Taos

**Rio Grande Gorge crags** — John Dunn Bridge, Dead Cholla Wall, Miner's
Crag, Utopian Vistas, Vista Verde, Wild and Scenic, The Bend.
*Unit:* **Servilleta Basalt**, Taos Plateau volcanic field, **5.3–2.95 Ma**
(tighter Ar/Ar: 5.3–3.3 Ma), up to **240 m thick in the gorge**.
*Lithology:* **olivine tholeiite**; "horizontal basalt layers of
dark-gray, **pahoehoe (ropey), vesicular** lava," in **three main flow
units separated by beds of alluvium**.
*Setting:* flood basalt filling the Taos graben of the Rio Grande rift,
then trenched by the river.
*Climbing consequence:* the three-tier layer cake is the crag
architecture — walls are 45–100 ft because that is a flow's thickness,
and the sediment interbeds are the ledges and the choss bands.
**Vesicles are the holds**: "not unlike the finger pockets of limestone."
Cracks follow **cooling joints**, so gear placements tend to be parallel
and cam-friendly, but hard basalt can let cam lobes track out of flaring
placements. Rock quality is called "mostly good" at Vista Verde (~40
routes to 5.12, up to 100 ft, mostly bolts). The universal hazard is
explicit and repeated: "like all New Mexico's basalt cliffs, the area
above and below is covered in **loose, dangerous rocks that can come free
at the slightest touch**," and "yearly **freeze–thaw** can be severe on
New Mexico's basalt." Helmets, and do not top-rope carelessly over a
rim built of loose blocks. Wind and rain strip chalk fast and a fine
silt washes through the cracks.
*Correction to flag:* **Dead Cholla Wall is not basalt.** One source
states "while the rest of the cliffs are basalt, **Dead Cholla Wall is
andesite**" — 60 ft of black and red rock. The Taos Plateau field is
12% olivine andesite by volume, so this is entirely plausible and worth
carrying in the gazetteer's Rock column as "basalt (Dead Cholla Wall:
andesite)."

**Tres Piedras.**
*Unit:* **Tres Piedras Orthogneiss**, U-Pb **1693 Ma**, a pluton on the
southeastern fringe of the Tusas Mountains.
*Lithology:* pink to reddish-orange, faint to obvious **foliation**,
grain size 0.5–5 mm, modal composition **42% quartz, 40% microcline,
12% albite** — i.e. the composition of an **alkali feldspar granite**,
metamorphosed. Intruded ~1.693 Ga, probably when the Yavapai arc docked;
the deformation that made it a gneiss probably ~1.45 Ga.
*Setting:* Proterozoic basement of the Tusas Mountains, exhumed on the
western shoulder of the Rio Grande rift at ~8,000 ft.
*Climbing consequence:* **climbers call it granite; the geologist calls
it orthogneiss, and both are fair** — it is granite that has been
squeezed. High quartz plus coarse feldspar gives the friction slabs the
area is known for. Foliation is the thing to watch: a foliated rock has a
built-in plane of weakness, so exfoliation sheets and flakes part along
it, giving **flake cracks** as well as splitters, and giving some of the
loose blocks. Domes to ~200 ft, so the jointing that matters is
**sheeting (unloading) joints** parallel to the dome surface — the
classic slab-and-flake dome architecture, not columnar.

**Questa Dome.**
*Unit:* `[uncertain — two candidates, unresolved]`. The dome is described
by climbers as **"beautiful white granite," ~500–600 ft, south-facing,
in the Latir Peaks Wilderness**. Two possibilities are live in the
literature: **Proterozoic basement**, since "the Cabresto Lake and
associated bodies occur within a structurally uplifted mass of
Proterozoic rocks" and the Taos Mountains have a ~1.7 Ga metamorphic
core; or a **Tertiary pluton of the Questa caldera / Latir volcanic
field** — the **Rito del Medio, Cañada Pinabete and Virgin Canyon
plutons were intruded ~25.3 Ma**, an older biotite granite ~26 Ma, and
Miocene "younger granite and aplite" is *medium- to fine-grained biotite
granite, massive and structureless, SiO₂ 76–77%*. **"Massive and
structureless" white granite with 76% silica is a very good match for a
white dome that gives runout slabs**, and would explain why Questa Dome
does not look like the foliated Proterozoic rock at Tres Piedras. This
file cannot pick between them from search snippets. USGS I-1907,
*Geologic map of the Latir volcanic field and adjacent areas*, would
settle it in one look.
*Climbing consequence:* whichever it is, the character is reported
consistently — "quality **crack/slab** climbing in the same style as
parts of the South Platte and the Organs," routes following cracks and
seams "linked by **slab and thin-face** sections," a "deserved
reputation for **runout and scary slabs**," and an intimidating roof
around the 350-ft level. That is the signature of a **massive,
poorly jointed, homogeneous granite**: few features, so protection is
sparse and the climbing is friction-dependent. South-facing at altitude:
May–October, best early in the day.

**Comales Canyon.**
*Unit:* `[inferred from regional geology — unverified]` Proterozoic
metamorphic rock of the **Picuris Range** — most plausibly quartzite of
the **Ortega Formation (Hondo Group)**, whose lower member is
"coarse-grained quartzite with thin beds of sillimanite-kyanite gneiss,
2,500+ ft," with the Rinconada schist and Pilar phyllite members above;
total ~6,600 ft, extensively folded, Precambrian. The inference rests on
the crag's position on NM-518 ~18 miles south of Taos near Sipapu, on
the Peñasco quadrangle ground where the Ortega is mapped, and on the
census's report of "metamorphic" rock. **No source directly names the
rock at the crag.**
*Climbing consequence, if quartzite:* extremely hard, low-porosity,
high-friction rock; holds are edges and the occasional bedding-plane
break rather than pockets; gear placements come from joints, and quartzite
joints are often shallow and flaring. Extensive folding means bedding is
not horizontal, so features may run at odd angles. Named walls reported
are "Water Wall" and "Fire Wall," creekside, with bolted sport and some
moderate trad; ~10,000 ft country, a cool-summer option.

**El Rito.**
*Unit:* **El Rito Formation**, **Eocene**, "predominantly an
**alluvial-fan deposit**" with two principal facies, **quartzite
conglomerate** and quartzose sandstone. (Do not confuse it with the
adjacent **Ritito Conglomerate** or the Miocene **Ojo Caliente Member**
of the Tesuque Formation, both of which surface in searches for this
area.)
*Lithology:* "**red-stained quartzite conglomerate** formed into
bread-loaves studded with cobbles — some as big as wheelbarrows"; the
cobbles are **quartzite, granite, schist and metavolcanic**, set in a
**sandstone matrix**.
*Setting:* an Eocene fan shed off Laramide highlands, later cemented and
exhumed in Arroyo Seco Canyon.
*Climbing consequence:* this is New Mexico's cobble crag and it climbs
like Maple Canyon's cousin. **Holds are the cobbles and the sockets they
leave**: "pockets from sharp-lipped hand-gobblers to crypto-sloping
huecos and shallow tri- and bi-doigts," on gently overhanging stone.
**The failure mode is the cobble.** Sources say plainly that "**some of
the cobbles are loose and wiggling**, which makes some of the routes a
little sketchy," and that quality varies wall to wall. A conglomerate's
strength lives in its matrix, so bolts must go into matrix, not into a
clast, and hangers should be checked for spin. 80+ sport routes over 13
walls, 5.7–5.13a, Carson National Forest.

**Roy / Mills Canyon / Mesteño Canyon bouldering.**
*Unit:* **Dakota Sandstone** (Dakota Group), Late Cretaceous
**Cenomanian, ~100–95 Ma** — shoreline and nearshore sand of the Western
Interior Seaway.
*Setting:* the **Canadian River** and its dendritic tributaries have cut
an 800-ft gorge through the **Dakota sandstone caprock** of the High
Plains, exposing "stacks of sandstone cliffs" and shedding boulders into
the canyon bottoms. Public land is largely Kiowa National Grassland
(Forest Service) in a checkerboard with ranch, state and BLM ground.
*Climbing consequence:* the geology explains the two textures climbers
prize. **Where the rock has developed a thick iron-oxide "merlot"
patina, it is "bullet-proof and easy on the hands"** with "perfectly
formed crisp edges and laser-cut cracks." **Where it has sat in a
streambed, it carries a "deep, uniform water polish" that gives "stellar
sloper and compression climbing."** Dry-environment blocks weather
angular with clean edges; wetter locales give slopey, sculpted features.
Between the two is ordinary Dakota, which is variable.
*Hazard:* Dakota is a quartz sand held by a small volume of cement.
**Wet sandstone can lose up to ~75% of its strength**; water attacks the
silica or calcite cement directly. **Do not climb recently wet
sandstone** — this is the single most important ethical instruction in
northeastern New Mexico. 24 hours may do after light rain on a warm south
aspect; 48–72 hours after a real storm or on shade.

**Philmont Scout Ranch** — Miners Park, Cimarroncito, Dean Cow.
*Setting:* Sangre de Cristo front, 140,177 acres near Cimarron, on the
western edge of the Raton Basin. Climbing is BSA program terrain, not a
public destination.
*Units:* mixed, and only partly sourceable from here. The ranch's
signature landmark, the **Tooth of Time**, is an **igneous intrusion of
dacite porphyry, Tertiary, ~40–22 Ma** — the same magmatic episode that
emplaced the **Palisades Sill** in nearby Cimarron Canyon (~**40 Ma**,
variously called monzonite, monzonite porphyry, quartz monzonite
porphyry, dacite porphyry or granodiorite porphyry; phenocrysts of
plagioclase, biotite, hornblende and quartz in a fine gray matrix, and it
makes the canyon's spectacular palisade cliffs). The rest of the ranch
exposes "resistant sandstones of the Raton Basin," and Cimarron Canyon
"exposes nearly two billion years of complex geologic history."
`[Unverified]` **Which of the three climbing sites is on porphyry and
which on Cretaceous–Paleogene sandstone is not established here.** The
NMBGMR *Geologic guide to the trails at Philmont Scout Ranch* (Open-File
Report 22) exists and would answer it.
*Climbing consequence, if porphyry:* dacite/monzonite porphyry is hard,
tough, and jointed — the Palisades' columnar-looking cliffs are the
visual proof. If sandstone: Raton Basin sandstones are variable and
generally softer.

**Cañoncito** — the census's unverified entry.
*Finding:* the census assumed an Albuquerque/Tijeras Canyon location and
found nothing. There is a **"Cañoncito at Apache Canyon" on I-25 about
11.5 miles southeast of Santa Fe**, and it *is* a climbing area:
Mountain Project lists **Apache Canyon (a.k.a. Grasshopper Canyon)
Bouldering**, "a large overhanging chunk of **sandstone** with at least
20 different variations," V0–V10, "rock varies from excellent to total
choss," 20 minutes from Santa Fe.
*Unit:* `[inferred — unverified]` the **Galisteo Formation**, deposited
**50–34 Ma** from sediment shed off the rising Sangre de Cristos: a lower
unit of pink and red sandstone, siltstone and mudstone, an upper unit of
tan to white sandstone with red, tan and grey-green mudstone; it crops
out over a limited area between Sandia Crest and the southern Sangre de
Cristos, which includes this ground. The inference rests on location and
lithology, not a mapped point.
*Recommendation:* the gazetteer's Cañoncito row should either be
rewritten to point at Apache/Grasshopper Canyon near Santa Fe or deleted;
the current "likely not an established area" note is probably wrong about
the name and right about the Albuquerque location.

### 3.2 Santa Fe area

**The Overlook, Gallows Edge and the White Rock crags.**
*Unit:* basalt of the **Cerros del Rio volcanic field**, principal
activity **~2.3–2.8 Ma** (often given as ~2.5–2.6 Ma), lavas erupted
3–1 Ma; described in the most recent work as **hawaiite, a sodic
trachybasalt**, with alkali basalt and basaltic andesite, maars, scoria
cones and viscous flows.
*Setting:* the crags are "a cluster of excellent basalt cliffs scattered
along the **western rim of deep White Rock Canyon**," where the Rio
Grande has cut through the Cerros del Rio lavas into the softer Tesuque
and Chamita Formations beneath. The **Bandelier Tuff** (1.61 and 1.25 Ma)
caps the Pajarito Plateau above and overlies the hawaiite west of the
canyon; a cliff of light Bandelier Tuff filling a paleocanyon cut into
the Cerros del Rio is visible in the canyon wall. So: **the climbing is
on the older basalt, not on the tuff**, even though the tuff is the
region's famous rock.
*Climbing consequence:* one-pitch routes 45–70 ft, "good to excellent"
basalt, both trad crack lines and bolted faces; 375+ documented climbs
across three hubs; two aspects (NW and SE) at the Overlook make it
near-year-round, and Gallows Edge is **east-facing** with three sub-areas
(Main Wall, The Pit, Upper Tier) and short moderate sport routes — the
local beginner crag, re-bolted in Feb–Mar 2025 (34 protection bolts, 13
anchor bolts, 22 steel lowering carabiners). The rock's behaviour is
basalt's: **flow-scale layering, cooling joints for cracks, vesicles for
pockets, and a loose rim.**
*Access geology footnote:* the 1989 White Rock bolting agreement (amended
2004) is a human artefact, not a geologic one, but it is why bolted face
routes are legitimate here.

**Diablo Canyon.**
*Unit:* Cerros del Rio basalt again, at the field's northern end.
*Setting — and this is the good part:* rising basaltic magma hit
groundwater held in the porous sandstones of the older **Tesuque
Formation**, producing a **maar** crater; a large volume of lava then
**pooled inside the crater as a lava lake**, and as it cooled and
contracted it fractured into **over 300 feet of vertical to gently
curved columns**. A narrow vertical **dike** cuts the tilted layers and
feeds a capping flow. Cliffs to ~300 ft.
*Climbing consequence:* **this is the best columnar-basalt architecture
in New Mexico, and it is columnar because it is a ponded lava lake, not a
thin flow.** Thick, slowly cooled lava makes long, regular columns;
that is why Diablo has 300-ft columns and the Taos gorge has 45-ft
walls. Practically: **the columns give corner systems, splitter
column-bounding cracks and dihedrals**; cams work better than nuts in
the parallel-sided cracks; the faces between columns are often blank and
therefore bolted. 100+ sport lines, 5.8–5.13 with the bulk 5.10–5.12,
approaches 5–30 minutes, sub-areas including Winter Wall, Solar Cave and
Coxcomb Crag. **Rockfall risk is called out explicitly by the BLM and by
climbers — helmet recommended.** Columnar rock fails by whole columns
detaching; that is exactly what makes a talus cone of hexagonal blocks
at the base.

**Cochiti Mesa and Capulin Canyon.**
*Unit:* **Bandelier Tuff** — welded ash-flow tuff from the Toledo and
Valles calderas, **1.61 Ma (Otowi Member)** and **1.25 Ma (Tshirege
Member)**.
*Lithology:* compacted pumice and ash with quartz and sanidine crystals
and lithic fragments, welded to varying degrees. Capulin Canyon is
described as "a relatively **high quality welded tuff** that lends itself
well to gear placements."
*History and climbing consequence:* climbers explored the mesa's
**splitter cracks** from the 1970s (mostly in the Dihedrals) and put up
some of the **first sport climbs in the Southwest** here in the
mid-1980s. Then the **2011 Las Conchas Fire**. This is the clearest
natural experiment in fire damage to climbing rock in the United States:
"**the fire was hot enough to separate the layers of patina from the
rock, and without the patina there was nothing solid enough to climb**,"
leaving "the top surface loose and flaky" and many face climbs "**utterly
unclimbable**." The mechanism is thermal shock — during a wildfire the
rock surface heats far faster than its interior, producing a steep
thermal gradient and **fractures parallel to the surface**; oxidation
reddens the rock from ~300 °C; spalling follows during the fire or when
it is extinguished. Tuff is especially vulnerable because its strength
lives almost entirely in a thin case-hardened rind. **Treat Cochiti Mesa
as diminished, and treat any post-fire crag as suspect until it has been
cleaned and re-tested.**

**Jemez Mountains and Jemez Valley** — Ponderosa Bouldering, The Pond,
The Satellite.
*Unit:* **volcanic tuff** — "miles of excellent quality **welded tuff**
boulders." The wider Jemez offers "welded tuff, basalt, granite and
travertine," so the range's climbing is not all one rock.
*Lithology and climbing consequence:* Ponderosa's tuff is described as
"**sharp and coarse and hard on the skin**," giving "rails, pockets, thin
slabs, sharp arêtes, **hollow huecos**, and interesting three-dimensional
scoops." The patina story is precise and geologically informative:
Ponderosa "**doesn't have the thick and solid patina layer** that the
Happys [California] does, and **where there is thick patina here, it is
probably choss**. However, where the patina is thin, it is awesome and
very grainy, making for excellent pocketed face climbs." In other words,
**at Ponderosa a thick rind is a warning sign, not a promise** — the
opposite of the usual desert-varnish rule, and worth quoting in the book
if the Jemez ever gets a page. V-grades from lowball to highball, thin
face to steep overhung. The Satellite's access gate closes in winter; The
Pond's lower slopes turn to mud after snowmelt, which is a
volcanic-ash-soil consequence as much as a weather one.

### 3.3 Albuquerque and the Sandias

**Sandia Mountains (West Side).**
*Unit:* **Sandia granite**, **1453 ± 12 Ma** — gray to pink,
medium-to-coarse-grained **monzogranite** with a secondary granodiorite
phase; porphyritic varieties of such granites carry K-feldspar megacrysts
to several centimetres. Capped by **Sandia Formation** mudstone and
**Madera Group** limestone, **~300–315 Ma**, the whole contact being the
**Great Unconformity** — a 1.1-billion-year gap you can put your hand on.
*Setting:* a classic **rift-flank uplift** on the eastern shoulder of the
Rio Grande rift, with nearly **1.5 km (4,900 ft) of granite exposed** at
Sandia Crest; steep broken west face, gentle east dip slope.
*Climbing consequence:* climbers say it well — "**not as solid as the
granite found in places like Yosemite**," "you will undoubtedly encounter
loose rocks," and the holds are "rounded and gritty." The geology behind
that: a coarse-grained monzogranite at the exhumed top of a pluton, with
1.4 billion years of weathering along its joints, in a semi-arid climate
that favours **grus** (granular disintegration) over clean exfoliation.
Feldspar weathers to clay faster than quartz, so the surface roughens and
crumbles; that is the grit, and it is also the friction. Hundreds of
established routes, mostly moderate, and the range's longest line — the
**SW Ridge of the Needle, IV 5.8, 8–12 pitches**. Note for topo work:
the **white limestone band at the top of the west face** is Madera Group,
and it is a genuinely different rock at the end of a granite climb — one
climbing source calls it "a half-pitch-thick icing." West and southwest
walls such as Muralla Grande are warm and dry even in winter. This is a
mountain, not a crag: real approaches, real weather, real rockfall.

**Sandia Foothills Bouldering** — U-Mound, Three Gun Spring / Tres
Pistolas, Fat City, Indian School, Manitoba/Montgomery.
*Unit:* the same **Sandia granite**, as **coarse-grained boulders** in
the foothills at the edge of the city.
*Climbing consequence:* "The granite here can be quite **rough**" —
"once you know … exactly how to grab specific holds, you can manage to
keep from wearing through your pads." That is coarse monzogranite
weathering to grus: superb friction, brutal on skin, crimps that are
really feldspar and quartz crystal edges. Technical problems, edge-of-
city access.

**Palomas Peak.**
*Unit:* `[inferred from regional geology — near-certain]` **Madera Group
limestone, Pennsylvanian.** The peak (8,250 ft) is "a minor peak in the
Sandia massif" in **Las Huertas Canyon** at the range's north end; "the
top layer of Palomas is limestone, forming … two separate cliff bands;
the higher cliff band (at around 8,000 ft) is the developed climbing
area"; and the Madera Group is the limestone that caps the Sandia massif.
The inference rests on position within the massif and on the Madera being
the mapped cap; no source names the unit at the crag itself.
*Climbing consequence:* over 100 mostly bolted sport climbs to ~70 ft on
"high quality" limestone that is "**sharp, highly textured**" and
"requiring frequent taping/resoling" — i.e. a solution-weathered platform
carbonate with knife-edged crystalline texture, the classic hand-shredder.
SW-facing at 8,000 ft gives a long season and possible year-round
climbing, with spring and fall ideal. Twelve named walls.

**New Canyon and the Manzano Mountains** — *not in the gazetteer, and
should be.* Mountain Project carries a **Manzano Mountains** area and an
**East Side** sub-area in the I-40 Corridor region; **New Canyon** is "a
small, little-known, **limestone** climbing area tucked away near the
town of Manzano," Cibola National Forest, with modern chain anchors and
some steel lowering biners. The range's rock is "**limestone, quartzite
and gneiss**" — the same Proterozoic-basement-plus-Pennsylvanian-cap
stack as the Sandias, since the Manzanos are the Sandias' southward
continuation across Tijeras Canyon. `[Inferred — unverified]` **New
Canyon's limestone is very likely Madera Group.** Tijeras Canyon itself
exposes **Precambrian gneisses and greenstones intruded by granites**
(the Tijeras Greenstone), and there is a short south-facing **limestone
cliff immediately south of the Sandia Ranger Station** in Tijeras.

### 3.4 Central New Mexico / Socorro

**The Box (Box Recreation Area).**
*The correction:* every climbing source calls The Box **rhyolite** —
"a series of cliffs and boulders of **mixed-quality rhyolite**." The
New Mexico Bureau of Geology, describing the same canyon in its Image of
the Day feature on highlining there, says something different: **"the
steep cliffs in Box Canyon are composed of **andesite porphyry lavas of
the Oligocene Luis Lopez Formation**, which filled the Socorro Caldera
following the eruption of the Hells Mesa Tuff."**
*Assessment:* the two are not reconcilable by hand-waving — andesite and
rhyolite are opposite ends of the calc-alkaline series. `[Flagged
disagreement]` **Climbers' "rhyolite" at The Box is contradicted by the
NMBGMR's "andesite porphyry of the Luis Lopez Formation" for the cliffs
of the same canyon.** Two caveats: the climbing area covers a 320-acre
canyon in four sections and may span more than one unit; and climbers
routinely use "rhyolite" as a loose synonym for "hard light-coloured
volcanic rock." The book should say **"volcanic — climbers call it
rhyolite; the state survey maps the canyon's cliffs as Oligocene
andesite porphyry (Luis Lopez Formation)."** That is honest and it is
more interesting than either alone.
*Regional context:* the **Socorro caldera** (~32.5 Ma) erupted the
**Hells Mesa Tuff** (~33 Ma; poorly to densely welded, **crystal-rich,
quartz-rich**, ~42% crystals of sanidine, plagioclase and biotite, with
andesite lithics and crystal-rich pumice, and caldera-wall facies
containing megabreccia slabs). The caldera then filled — first with the
Luis Lopez lavas, later with the **La Jencia Tuff** (~30 Ma), which
reaches **1,700 ft thick** because it ponded in the depression. At
Chupadera Peak nearby, **60-m craggy cliffs mark a dusky red lithophysal
zone in the basal densely welded La Jencia Tuff** — worth remembering as
the model for red-versus-buff quality splits in ignimbrite everywhere in
this state.
*Climbing consequence:* mixed quality is the honest verdict either way.
Andesite porphyry gives blocky jointing, phenocryst knobs, and variable
hardness between flow units; welded tuff gives fiamme pockets and
welding-controlled bands. Four sections, 5.8–5.13b, Southern Box holds
the best routes and boulders; a good winter destination with camping.

**Enchanted Tower (Datil).**
*Unit:* **welded tuff** of the **Datil Group** / Mogollon–Datil field,
Oligocene. The Datil Group in the Datil area is divided into six units
including the **tuff of Rock House Canyon** and the **tuff of Blue
Canyon**; `[unverified]` **which of them the Tower is cut in is not
established here.** The Tower is described as "part of a pyroclastic
flow in the Datil Mountains."
*Climbing consequence:* the classic ignimbrite pocket wall. "The
climbing is reminiscent of **Smith Rock**, except steeper and **more
pocketed**, and with very few nubbins," on a formation that **overhangs
on three sides**, over 100 ft on its tallest side, the fourth side
attached to the canyon wall. Pockets in welded tuff come from weathered
pumice fragments (fiamme), lithophysae and vapour-phase cavities; the
absence of nubbins means the tuff is crystal-poor, so there are no
phenocryst knobs — everything is a hole. Steep, pocketed, bolt-protected.
Access is the problem, not the rock: a rancher's gate closure has cut the
classic approach.

### 3.5 Southern New Mexico / Las Cruces

**Organ Mountains (including Sugarloaf, Rabbit Ears, the Needles, ORP).**
*Unit:* **Organ Needle Quartz Monzonite** of the **Organ batholith**, and
associated cauldron-fill tuffs and lavas of the **Organ caldera**.
Eocene volcanic and plutonic rocks in the range span **~44–34 Ma**;
high-precision U-Pb dating puts the cauldron fill and the underlying
batholith within a **1 m.y. span at 33.7–32.8 Ma**, while other sources
describe the Organ Needles as a **36 Ma quartz monzonite batholith** —
`[flagged discrepancy: 36 Ma vs 33.7–32.8 Ma for the batholith; the
high-precision U-Pb study is the stronger source]`. The **Sugarloaf Peak
Quartz Monzonite** was emplaced 1–2 m.y. after the caldera eruptions,
with zircon ages **34.66 ± 0.06 to 33.46 ± 0.19 Ma**, incrementally.
*Setting:* an **uplifted fault block** on the east side of the Mesilla
Basin, Basin and Range / southern rift. Intrusive rocks related to the
caldera are exposed along the east slopes and crest at **Aguirre
Springs**.
*Climbing consequence:* "the dramatic scenery of the Organs is owed to
the quartz monzonite of the Organ batholith, weathering of which has
yielded the rock monoliths known as the Needles, Soledad Peak, Sugarloaf
Peak and San Agustin Peak," and **Sugarloaf and San Agustin show rounded
outcrops produced by exfoliation.** Exfoliation is the key word: an
intrusive body unloaded by erosion cracks in sheets parallel to its
surface, giving **domes, slabs, and flakes** and — critically —
**very few continuous cracks on the slab faces**. Hence Sugarloaf's
**North Face, 1,800 ft, 5.6+ R, 9–11 pitches, with 50–60 ft runouts most
of the way**, and Mountain Project's summary of another line as "a fun
but **runout lark up slabs and knobs**." Rock quality is "overall very
good, although expect some **loose blocks** and test everything." The
Rabbit Ears carry "huge faces of high quality rock."
*Access as a geologic constraint:* **power drilling is prohibited in the
Organ Mountains Wilderness**, so bolted protection and rappel anchors are
sparse to absent on a rock type that gives almost no natural gear on its
slabs. That combination — exfoliated monzonite plus a hand-drill-only
ethic — is the reason the Organs feel serious at moderate grades.

**Doña Ana Mountains** (Checkerboard Wall, Pizza Boulders, Bear
Boulders).
*Unit:* **Doña Ana Rhyolite** — ash-flow tuff, rhyolite flows, domes and
breccias filling the **Doña Ana caldera**, ~**36 Ma**, with individual
ash-flow beds up to **440 m (1,440 ft)** thick; **monzonite porphyry**
and related dikes form the pyramidal peaks above the lower hills
(Summerford Mountain).
*Correction to flag:* the gazetteer's Rock column says **"Granite."**
The mapped rock is **rhyolite and ash-flow tuff, with monzonite porphyry
on the high peaks** — Checkerboard Wall specifically is "steep towers of
**deeply etched rhyolite**." Granite is wrong; the entry should read
"Rhyolite / ash-flow tuff (monzonite porphyry on the high peaks)."
*Climbing consequence:* "deeply etched" is the whole story. Rhyolite and
welded tuff weather differentially along flow banding, lithophysal zones
and joints, leaving a **fretted, pocketed, edgy surface** — hence the
Checkerboard name and hence quality bouldering. Pizza Boulders are "one
of the highest quality bouldering spots within a 50 mile radius of Las
Cruces," on the western slope opposite Checkerboard Wall, with a large
number of north-facing problems that extend the season into summer.
*Access:* NMSU has closed some acquired parcels including the Columns and
the North Boulder Field.

**City of Rocks State Park.**
*Unit:* **Kneeling Nun Tuff**, erupted from the **Emory caldera** at the
southern end of the Black Range near Hillsboro Peak. Age given as
**34.9 Ma** by the state park geologic tour and **35.3 Ma** in the
formation literature — `[flagged discrepancy; both are published]`.
Volume **over 900 km³**, a VEI-8 event "more than one thousand times
larger than the 1980 eruption of Mt. St. Helens." Lithology: reddish
brown to pinkish gray tuff with abundant **phenocrysts up to 3 mm —
quartz, lesser sanidine and plagioclase, minor biotite**.
*How the pinnacles formed:* hot ash welded into a continuous sheet; it
then cooled and contracted into **columnar joints**, and further
**orthogonal fractures** opened as overlying rock was eroded and the load
released. Weathering — **freeze–thaw**, wind, roots and humic acids —
widened those joints, and **softer pumice and ash layers broke down while
harder rhyolitic components resisted**, sculpting the sheet into the
pinnacles, fins, tors, spires, arches, **tafoni** and **panholes** that
make the "streets" of the City. Some structures rise 40 ft.
*Climbing consequence:* **the shape of every boulder here is controlled
by a joint.** The park's own account is unusually direct about the
climbing: weathering has produced "windows and arches as well as the
**knobs and plates that provide hand holds**." Expect crystal-studded
friction (those 3 mm phenocrysts), tafoni pockets, and the sloping
scoops that differential weathering of ash layers gives. Rock quality is
good — this is a densely welded ignimbrite — but tafoni walls are thin
and break.
*Ethics, and they are geologic:* **no bolting or other fixed protection
is permitted**, chalk is limited, $5/vehicle. This is a state park
protecting a landform, and it is the right call: fixed hardware in a
jointed, tafoni-riddled tuff pinnacle is both unreliable and destructive.
100+ problems, V0–V8, BoulderFest each Halloween weekend.

**Aguirre Springs** — the census's unverified entry, now largely
resolvable. It is the BLM recreation area on the **east side of the Organ
Mountains**, beneath the **Rabbit Ears**, and "**intrusive rocks related
to the Organ caldera are exposed along the east slopes and crest of the
Organ Mountains at Aguirre Springs**." The **quartz monzonite forms the
iconic Rabbit Ears pinnacles**; the **North Rabbit Ear** is "a massive
block of granite [sic — quartz monzonite] that culminates in a nice
rounded summit," easiest known route 5.3, and Mountain Project carries a
**Rabbit Ears Area**. So the census's guess that it is "volcanic/syenite
bouldering related to the Hueco Tanks/Franklin Mountains trend" is
**wrong**: it is the east side of the Organs, on Organ batholith quartz
monzonite, and it is roped climbing on pinnacles as much as bouldering.

**Cornudas Mountains** — the census's other unverified southern entry.
Geologically it is well documented even if the climbing is not:
**Eocene–Oligocene nepheline syenite and syenite laccoliths and plugs
with phonolite, trachyte and syenite sills and dikes**, forming nine
mountains straddling the New Mexico–Texas line east of El Paso and rising
above the Diablo Plateau; intruded into **Permian and Cretaceous
sediments** in two pulses, **37.14–34.5 Ma** and **32.48–26.95 Ma**, just
before or during early Rio Grande rift extension. Wind Mountain is the
best-known laccolith and a noted mineral locality (REE, beryllium,
niobium). `[Climbing unverified]` — the geology is real, and syenite is
a fine-grained-to-coarse alkali feldspar rock that weathers to good
friction, but **no climbing area was confirmed**. The census's instinct
that it is "syenite" was right; its instinct that it is related to Hueco
Tanks was geologically loose (Hueco Tanks is a syenite/alkali-granite
intrusion too, so the family resemblance is real, but they are separate
intrusive centres).

**Sitting Bull Falls** — the census's third unverified entry.
*Geology:* a narrow canyon in the **Guadalupe escarpment**, Lincoln
National Forest, cut into the **San Andres, Grayburg and Cherry Canyon
Formations**, on the remnant of the **Middle Permian Capitan reef
complex, ~260–265 Ma**, at the margin of the Delaware Basin. Groundwater
escapes from caves and springs in the thick Permian limestones and
dolomites, and the highly mineralised water has built **a massive
travertine bluff** over the picnic ground; the canyon was partly refilled
by **tufa (porous travertine)** precipitated from the spring water.
*Assessment:* `[Climbing still unverified]`. The rock is real limestone
and travertine and the setting is dramatic, but nothing in this pass
confirms an established climbing area, and a heavily visited
Forest-Service day-use site around a spring-fed waterfall is exactly the
kind of place where climbing is likely restricted. **Leave it as
unverified, but note that the "swimming hole, no climbing" framing is now
backed by a clear geologic picture rather than by absence of evidence
alone.**

### 3.6 Gila / Southwest — the census gap

The census flagged this region as an unfilled gap, and this pass did not
close it either: **no established climbing area in the Gila National
Forest / Silver City sub-tree was confirmed.** What can be added is the
geologic frame, which is useful for any future search:

- The whole region is **Mogollon–Datil volcanic field**: 35 million years
  of volcanism from ~40 Ma to less than 5 Ma, "dominated by massive
  cliffs of ash-flow tuffs and exposures of shallow batholiths."
- The **Emory caldera** — 15 × 34 miles, at the southern Black Range near
  Hillsboro Peak, partly in the Aldo Leopold Wilderness — is the
  easternmost caldera in the Gila National Forest, and its **Kneeling Nun
  Tuff** is the caprock over a huge area. City of Rocks is its distal
  outflow sheet.
- The **Gila Conglomerate** — late Tertiary to Pleistocene, a
  **volcaniclastic** unit of grains, pebbles and boulders eroded from the
  surrounding volcanic mountains, including clasts of Bloodgood Canyon
  Tuff — is the range's most conspicuous cultural rock: "**due to the
  nature of the rock, many cavities, overhangs, and caves formed within
  the unit**," which is why the Gila Cliff Dwellings are where they are.
  A conglomerate that forms natural caves is, in principle, a bouldering
  and sport-climbing rock; whether anyone has developed it is unknown
  here, and Cliff Dwellings NM is a protected archaeological site where
  climbing would not be appropriate.

If a future pass hunts the Gila, the geologic targets are: **welded tuff
cliff bands (Bloodgood Canyon, Kneeling Nun and equivalents)** along the
canyons, and **Gila Conglomerate overhangs** — not the granite one might
expect from a wilderness range.

### 3.7 Western New Mexico / I-40 corridor

**Mentmore (Gallup).**
*Unit:* **Gallup Sandstone**, **Late Cretaceous** — "a well-cemented
**uplifted shelf** of yellow-tan to creamy-tan Cretaceous Gallup
Sandstone." The formation is "uniform, very well sorted, fine-grained
pink to buff sandstone" deposited as **northeastward-prograding coastal
barrier / delta-front sand** that grades seaward into Mancos Shale and
landward into the Dilco Member of the Crevasse Canyon Formation.
*Climbing consequence:* "very well sorted, fine-grained" is exactly what
you want for a friction sandstone: uniform grain size, uniform behaviour.
Rock quality is "decent, with **little to no choss**," and climbers are
"not weary of holds breaking" — remarkable for sandstone, and a
consequence of good cementation. Routes are **25–45 ft** because that is
the height of the resistant shelf; 50+ bolted top-ropes and 31 sport
routes, 5.0–5.13, "well bolted, with thoughtful clipping stances."
*Hazard:* "**when this sandstone becomes wet it is quite fragile, and
climbing when the rock has recently been wet can result in destroying the
climbs.** Local climbers ask that you wait about **48 hours** following a
heavy rainstorm." The mechanism is the standard one — water attacking the
cement between quartz grains — and the 48-hour figure sits squarely
inside the 24–72 hour range the Access Fund gives for western sandstone.

**El Malpais (the Highway 117 bluffs).**
*Unit:* **Zuni Sandstone**, **Middle Jurassic, ~165–175 Ma**, deposited
as **eolian dune sand**; the eastern escarpment of Cebollita Mesa is
**Zuni Sandstone cliffs capped by Cretaceous Dakota Sandstone**, up to
**400 ft high**, running ~15 miles along NM 117. To the west lies the
Quaternary lava of the **Zuni–Bandera field** (McCartys flow, 3.9 ka —
the youngest rock in New Mexico); at The Narrows the road threads
between the two.
*Climbing consequence:* eolian dune sandstone is the Wingate/Navajo
family — **large-scale cross-bedding, and cracks rather than faces.**
The reported style matches exactly: "the vast majority of the established
routes are **crack climbs**, entirely unlike the uniform cracks of the
Indian Creek variety, but of a more varied and technical nature. **Due to
the soft rock, bolts and other fixed protection are rarely encountered,
but nuts and cams work well.**" The area is "traditionally a **ground-up**
area, with what few bolts and fixed pins that exist placed on the lead,"
and **sport routes are strongly discouraged** — because of the soft rock,
the wilderness overlay, and the Acoma reservation. Excellent bouldering,
approaches under fifteen minutes, season February–November.
*Access:* **climbing is prohibited inside El Malpais National Monument**
(including caves and lava tubes) without a Superintendent's permit; the
climbable bluffs are on adjacent BLM land. Respect the boundary and the
cultural sensitivity of the Acoma ground.

**Zuni Mountains** — dropped from the census as unconfirmed for climbing,
and this pass found no reason to restore it. The geology is nonetheless
worth a line, because it is the state's cleanest example of an exhumed
Proterozoic core: a **northwest–southeast-trending uplift with a core of
Precambrian granite and metamorphic rock**, surrounded by Late Permian
through Jurassic strata. The Proterozoic rocks are dominantly
metaigneous, in three suites — peridotite (crustal cumulate or a slice of
mantle), tonalite/granodiorite with mafic enclaves, and **quartz monzonite
and rhyolite representing shallow-level magmatism at ca. 1.65 Ga** — with
four named granites: Mount Sedgewick, Zuni, Cerro Colorado gneissic
aplite, and Oso. Uplifted first in the **Ancestral Rockies** as part of
the **Zuni–Defiance uplift**, then again in the **Laramide**, with
~20,000 ft of cover stripped off the crest.

### 3.8 Tribal lands — do not climb

**Shiprock (Tsé Bitʼaʼí).**
*Unit:* a **minette diatreme** of the **Navajo (Diné) volcanic field**.
Shiprock is "the erosional remnant of the throat of a **maar-diatreme
volcano**," composed of **fractured volcanic breccia cut by black dikes
of minette** — an unusual potassic rock of alkali feldspar, biotite or
phlogopite and diopside. **Wall-like minette dikes radiate from the
central plug.** Ages: fission-track on the southern dike **27.3 and
32.3 Ma**, K-Ar **30.6 Ma**; the field generally quoted at ~30 Ma, and
the minette at Shiprock commonly at **~27 Ma**. The rock now exposed
formed **2,500–3,000 ft below the contemporaneous surface**; everything
above it has been eroded away.
*Climbing:* **illegal.** The Navajo Nation banned climbing on all tribal
land in 1966; enforcement became strict after a 1970 accident and the ban
was announced as "absolute, final and unconditional." It covers hiking,
camping and driving the access roads as well, and it applies Nation-wide.
Included here only because a geology file that omitted the most famous
volcanic neck in North America would be conspicuous. **Do not climb
here.**

## Part 4 — Cross-cutting reference

### 4.1 The recurring rock types of New Mexico climbing

| Rock type | Where in this book | How it formed | Fracture / jointing | Holds | Friction & durability | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Welded ash-flow tuff (ignimbrite)** | Cochiti Mesa, Ponderosa, Enchanted Tower, City of Rocks, Luna Park & Red Rock Arroyo, parts of The Box | pyroclastic flow deposited hot enough to weld; densely welded in the middle of a cooling unit, crumbly at top and base | cooling (columnar) joints plus unloading joints; weathering exploits both to make pinnacles and "streets" | pockets from weathered pumice (fiamme), lithophysae, vapour-phase cavities, tafoni; phenocryst knobs where crystal-rich | very high friction; hard on skin; durability depends entirely on welding grade | poorly welded bands are choss; a burnt tuff loses its patina and becomes unclimbable; bolts need the welded zone |

### 4.2 Geologic hazards and ethics

**Fire-damaged rock.** New Mexico has lost climbing to wildfire, and the
mechanism is worth stating precisely because it predicts which crags are
at risk. In a fire, rock surface temperature rises far faster than the
interior, creating a steep **thermal gradient** and producing fractures
**parallel to the surface**; thermal oxidation reddens rock from around
300 °C; spalling and cracking follow, during the fire or during
extinction. A study of Spanish crags found at least 6.5% of climbing
areas in fire-affected zones suffered **measurable hold deterioration and
anchor damage**. Granite is among the most vulnerable in the physical
sense, because quartz and feldspar expand at very different rates and
internal stress builds fast; fire cracks in granite blocks have been
measured 1.5 m long and 1 m deep. But **tuff is the worst case for
climbing**, because a tuff route's holds live in a thin patina: at
Cochiti Mesa in 2011 the fire "was hot enough to **separate the layers of
patina from the rock**, and without the patina there was nothing solid
enough to climb." Some crags recover after being cleaned with a pry bar;
Cochiti largely did not. **After any fire: assume every hold, bolt and
anchor is suspect until proven otherwise, and expect a burnt tuff crag to
be permanently diminished.**

**Wet sandstone.** The single most consequential ethic in the state.
Sandstone is quartz grains held by a small volume of cement — typically
around 95% grains, 5% cement. Water reacts with that cement, replacing
silica or calcite with weaker compounds, and **wet western sandstone can
lose up to ~75% of its strength**. Holds snap, classic lines are
destroyed permanently, and **protection can pull**. Drying times: about
**24 hours** after light rain with warm temperatures on a south aspect;
**48–72 hours** after a real storm, on shade, or in winter. Mentmore's
locals say 48 hours; Roy's say do not climb actively or recently wet
rock at all. This applies to Roy, Mentmore, El Malpais, Apache Canyon,
and any Philmont sandstone.

**Rockfall.** Three distinct New Mexico patterns:

1. **Basalt rims.** "Like all New Mexico's basalt cliffs, the area above
   and below is covered in loose, dangerous rocks that can come free at
   the slightest touch," and "yearly freeze–thaw can be severe." Diablo
   Canyon's rockfall risk is called out by the BLM itself. Columnar rock
   fails by whole columns. **Helmet, and do not scramble the rim above
   parties.**
2. **New crags shedding their rind.** Mud Mountain: "since this area is
   new, some climbs still have loose rock and care should be taken to
   protect people at the base." Utopian Vistas: "these are new routes
   that might still give up a hold or two." A freshly developed crag in
   any rock type is a rockfall crag for several seasons.
3. **Big-mountain terrain.** The Sandias and the Organs are ranges, not
   crags. Loose blocks are reported explicitly at both; the Organs add
   off-trail wilderness approaches and no power drilling, so rappel
   anchors are sparse or absent.

**Onion skin and case-hardening.** In desert carbonate and in tuff, the
surface is often harder than the rock beneath it — dissolution and
reprecipitation, or vapour-phase crystallisation, builds a shell. When
that shell separates, it sounds hollow and it fails without warning. It
is the named quality problem at Caballo Lake ("pretty bad onion skin")
and the named *inverted* quality signal at Ponderosa ("where there is
thick patina here, it is probably choss"). **Learn the sound.**

**Bolts in soft rock.** New Mexico has a lot of soft stone — poorly
welded tuff, Zuni Sandstone, weathering-rind limestone. Where fixed gear
is legitimate at all, **glue-in bolts are the correct answer in soft or
porous rock**: they distribute load, do not expand and split the rock,
and can achieve pull-out strengths where the rock fails before the anchor
does. Published testing in weak rock found failure loads of 8–48 kN, with
**chemically bonded anchors the strongest**, and only 10 mm rings and
10 mm U-bolts passing EN 959. Recommended leg lengths run **~80 mm for
limestone up to ~150 mm for sandstone**, and hole cleanliness is
critical — improper hole preparation has shown up to a **90% reduction in
pull-out strength**. Where fixed gear is *not* legitimate — City of Rocks
State Park bans bolting and other fixed protection outright, El Malpais
discourages sport routes, the Organ Mountains Wilderness bans power
drilling — the ban is usually a sound response to the rock, the
landscape, or both.

**Seepage.** Tufa on a wall means water. Percha Creek is a shaded creek
bottom choked with willows. The Pond in the Jemez turns to mud after
snowmelt. Limestone karst drains through the rock, not off it, so a
limestone crag can weep for days after the desert around it looks dry.
