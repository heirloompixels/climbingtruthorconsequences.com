# The Geology of Montana's Climbing Areas

Research notes for *Climbing Truth or Consequences*. The Montana volume of
the same reference work as `research/geology/new-mexico.md`, and it exists
to answer the same single question for every crag in the book: **why is the
rock the way it is, and what does that mean under your hands and feet?**

snippets**. `WebFetch` was tested at the start of the run against
`mbmg.mtech.edu` (the Montana Bureau of Mines and Geology) and

  quadrangle, every NPS interpretive page and every peer-reviewed article
  cited below is cited **from the search engine's summary of it**, not
  from the document itself. Geology summarises unusually well — unit
  names, thicknesses and radiometric ages come through verbatim — but
  they are summaries.
- **No geologic map was read.** Montana's coverage is good: MBMG has
  open-file 1:24,000 quadrangle maps for much of the western half of the
  state, and the USGS Mineral Resources geologic-unit database
  (`mrdata.usgs.gov`) carries unit descriptions county by county. None of
  it could be opened. Every statement of the form "this crag is on unit
  X" that is not directly quoted from a source is marked
  `[inferred from regional geology — unverified]`, with the inference
  named.
- Mountain Project and theCrag remain unfetchable (the standing condition
  of this project); climber descriptions of rock below come from search
  snippets that quote those pages, from the two Montana census and three

- No formation name, age or stratigraphic assignment in this file was

## Part 1 — The statewide framework

Montana is not one geologic story, it is four laid on top of each other,
and a climber can tell which one they are standing in from the parking
lot. Read west to east:

1. A **Mesoproterozoic sedimentary basin** — the Belt Supergroup, 15–20
   km of mud and sand and carbonate laid down between about 1.47 and 1.40
   billion years ago — fills the northwest quarter of the state. It makes
   Glacier, Stone Hill, the Mission and Swan ranges, and the country
   rock of the Bitterroot and Sapphire mountains.
2. An **Archean crystalline basement** — the Wyoming Province, 2.5–3.5 Ga
   gneiss and granite — is exposed where Laramide uplifts punched it back
   to the surface in the southwest and south: the Beartooths, the Madison
   and Tobacco Root and Ruby ranges, the core of the Little Belts, and
   the walls of Gallatin Canyon.
3. A **Paleozoic carbonate platform**, capped by the Mississippian
   Madison Group, drapes both of the above and is the state's limestone:
   Hyalite, the Bridgers, the Big Belts, Allenspur, Mount Helena.
4. A **Cretaceous–Eocene magmatic overprint** — the Boulder Batholith,
   the Idaho Batholith's Bitterroot lobe, the Elkhorn Mountains and
   Lowland Creek volcanics, the Absaroka volcanic pile, and a scatter of
   Eocene stocks and laccoliths in the Crazies, the Little Belts, the
   Castles and the Judith country — cuts through everything and supplies
   the granite spires.

Then the Pleistocene came through and cleaned it up.

### 1.1 The Archean basement: the Wyoming Province, 3.5–2.5 Ga

Southern and southwestern Montana sits on the **Wyoming Province**, one
of the oldest pieces of continental crust in North America. The province
is dominated by **2.8–3.5 Ga gneiss** intruded by **2.5–2.7 Ga granitic
plutons**. In the eastern Beartooth Mountains, high-grade metamorphic
gneisses and metasupracrustal rocks carry crystallisation ages of
**3.5–3.0 Ga**, with **detrital zircons as old as 4.0 Ga** — among the
oldest mineral grains found anywhere in the United States. The main
Beartooth block is built of voluminous Mesoarchean calc-alkaline
magmatic rock dated **2.82–2.79 Ga**, part of the **Beartooth–Bighorn
magmatic zone**, a single vast suite of tonalite–trondhjemite–
granodiorite (TTG) plutonic and metaplutonic rocks of about **2.8–2.9
Ga**.

Along the northern front of the Beartooths sits the **Stillwater
Complex**, a **2.709 Ga** Neoarchean layered mafic–ultramafic intrusion
about **5.5 km thick and exposed for ~50 km** — peridotite at the base
grading up through gabbronorite, norite, troctolite and anorthosite,
with the J-M Reef the only primary platinum-group source in the country.
It is not a climbing rock in any meaningful way, but it is the geologic
celebrity of the range and it explains the mine at Nye.

**What a climber gets from this:** the Beartooth walls and Granite
Peak; the gneiss half of Gallatin Canyon; the basement cores that the
Laramide raised beneath the Big Belts and Little Belts. Archean gneiss
is *not* the same climbing medium as a young pluton — see §3.1.

### 1.2 The Belt Supergroup, ~1.47–1.40 Ga — Montana's signature rock

The **Belt Supergroup** is an immense package, **15–20 km thick**, of
sedimentary rock deposited on Archean and Paleoproterozoic crystalline
basement between about **1470 and 1400 Ma** (some sources carry the top
to **1380 Ma**; the base is variously given as ~1500 Ma). It extends from
Canada south through western Montana into Idaho and west into eastern
Washington, and it was laid down in what is generally read as an
**aborted intracontinental rift basin**. Nothing else in the American
West is quite like it: a billion-and-a-half-year-old sedimentary pile
that was never deeply metamorphosed, so mudcracks, ripple marks and
stromatolites survive intact in rock older than most mountain ranges.

The four-part division that MBMG and the modern literature use:

| Division | Character | Key formations |
|---|---|---|
| **Lower Belt** | southwest-derived deep-water turbidite wedge, locally abundant mafic sills | **Prichard Formation** (deep-water fine clastics; some detrital zircons are non-North American, likely eastern Australian craton) |
| **Ravalli Group** | southwest-derived fluvial–deltaic complex | **Burke, Revett, St. Regis**; the **Spokane** and **Empire** Formations on the east side of the basin |
| **Piegan Group** | cyclic carbonate and siliciclastic sequence, lacustrine or marine, capped by mafic-to-felsic lavas | **Helena Formation / Helena Dolomite** (east), **Wallace Formation** (west) — the two are now treated as one revised group |
| **Missoula Group** | south- and east-derived fluvial succession — the top of the pile | **Snowslip**, **Shepard**, **Mount Shields**, **Bonner Quartzite**, **McNamara**, **Garnet Range** |

The Missoula Group formations are the ones a climber meets most. The
**Snowslip Formation** is the basal Missoula Group unit, conformably
above the Helena and below the Shepard, and consists of *grayish-green
to greenish-gray siltite and argillite couplets*. The Spokane, Empire,
Snowslip, Shepard, Mount Shields, Bonner and McNamara are all
dominantly **clastic — shale, argillite, siltite and quartzite** — with
the Helena the carbonate exception. At the very base of the Belt in the
Little Belt Mountains, the **Neihart Quartzite** is the oldest unit of
all, interpreted as a thick basal sheet sand deposited about **1,450 Ma**.

Three vocabulary items worth carrying into the field:

- **Argillite** — a lithified mud, harder than shale, not a slate. Splits
  along bedding. This is the rock that makes Glacier's red and green
  cliffs, and the rock that makes Glacier's rockfall.
- **Siltite** — the Belt-specific word for lithified silt, usually
  interbedded with argillite in millimetre-to-centimetre couplets.
- **Quartzite** — in the Belt this usually means a well-cemented
  *quartz-arenite sandstone*, hard and often recrystallised, not
  necessarily a metamorphic quartzite in the Ortega/Baraboo sense.
  This distinction is the whole reason Stone Hill climbs well.

**What a climber gets:** Stone Hill's ~500 routes; every cliff in
Glacier National Park; the country rock of the northwest corner; and the
roof pendants and screens in the Bitterroot canyons.

### 1.3 The Paleozoic platform: Cambrian to Mississippian

Above the Belt (or, in the southwest, above Archean basement across a
Great Unconformity of well over a billion years) sits a classic
shallow-marine platform section. The Cambrian sequence in Montana is one
of the most legible in the country, and the ridge walk on **Sacagawea
Peak in the northern Bridgers** exposes essentially all of it:

| Unit | Age | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Flathead Sandstone** | Middle Cambrian | basal transgressive quartz sand on the unconformity; cliff-former |
| **Wolsey Shale** | Middle Cambrian | green-gray shale; slope-former, weak layer |
| **Meagher Limestone** | Middle Cambrian | limestone; ledge- and cliff-former |
| **Park Shale** | Middle Cambrian | shale; slope-former |
| **Pilgrim Limestone** | Late Cambrian | limestone and dolomite |
| **Snowy Range Formation** | Late Cambrian | mixed shale and limestone |
| **Maywood Formation** | Devonian | siltstone and limestone |
| **Jefferson Formation / Jefferson Dolomite** | Late Devonian | dark, fetid, sugary dolomite; cliff-former |
| **Three Forks Formation** | Late Devonian | limestone, carbonaceous shale, evaporitic solution breccias |
| **Sappington Formation** | Devonian–Mississippian | thin transitional clastics |
| **Madison Group** — **Lodgepole Limestone** below, **Mission Canyon Limestone** above | Mississippian, ~340–330 Ma | the great cliff-former |

The **Madison Group** is the single most important climbing rock in
central and southwestern Montana. It accumulated in warm shallow
Mississippian seas about **330–340 Ma**, reaches as much as **2,000 ft
thick** in parts of Montana, and forms prominent gray cliffs across the
state. Its two formations behave differently and the distinction is
worth learning:

- **Lodgepole Limestone** (lower) — thinner-bedded, well-bedded light-gray
  limestone with abundant dark chert, interbedded with calcareous
  mudstone (the **Woodhurst Member** is the type of this). Bedded, ledgy,
  more shale partings, more loose rock.
- **Mission Canyon Limestone** (upper) — **gray, massive limestone with
  chert beds and nodules, and solution-breccia zones**. Massive is the
  operative word: it is the unit that makes the clean big grey walls, and
  its jointing rather than its bedding controls the climbing.

Two features of the Madison matter enormously for climbing. First, **in
late Meramec time the Madison sea withdrew from parts of western Montana
and the Mission Canyon was subaerially exposed and karsted** — karst
deposits and solution-breccia beds are widespread in the upper Madison
across Montana and Wyoming. That fossil karst is the reason Madison
limestone is full of vugs, pockets and collapse breccias. Second, karst
means the Madison drains *through* itself: caves, springs, and — in
winter — the reliable seeps that freeze into Hyalite's ice.

**What a climber gets:** Hyalite rock and ice, the Bridgers, Avalanche
and Hellgate Gulch, Beaver Creek/Refrigerator Canyon, Mount Helena,
Allenspur, the limestone half of the Gallatin, Memorial Falls.

### 1.4 The Mesozoic section

Above the Madison the section turns clastic and, for climbers, mostly
uninteresting — with one large exception on the plains.

- **Jurassic** — Ellis Group (Sawtooth, Rierdon, **Swift**), then the
  Morrison Formation.
- **Early Cretaceous — Kootenai Formation**: basal conglomerate,
  non-marine sandstone, shale and lacustrine limestone, deposited in a
  foreland basin east of the Sevier thrust belt.
- **Mid-Cretaceous — Colorado Group**: the marine shales of the Western
  Interior Seaway, from the top of the Kootenai (Aptian) to the Niobrara
  equivalent (Coniacian) in western Montana.
- **Late Cretaceous — Montana Group**, including the **Eagle Sandstone**:
  a light to brownish-gray to pale yellow-orange fine-grained sandstone,
  shoreline and barrier-bar sand of the retreating seaway. **This is the
  Billings Rimrocks.**
- **Latest Cretaceous — Livingston Group**: a volcaniclastic-rich
  synorogenic pile (the Billman Creek Formation is one of its units)
  shed off rising Sevier–Laramide highlands and the Elkhorn volcanic
  field. It floors Paradise Valley and the Livingston area.

### 1.5 The Sevier fold-and-thrust belt and the Lewis Thrust

From roughly **100 to 70 Ma**, the **Sevier orogeny** shortened the
crust of western Montana thin-skinned style — sedimentary sheets peeled
off their basement and stacked eastward on low-angle thrusts. The
Montana expression is the **Disturbed Belt** along the Rocky Mountain
Front, and the **Helena salient**, the eastward bulge of the thrust belt
between the Lewis and Clark line and the Snowcrest–Greenhorn structures
that carries the Big Belts, the Elkhorns and the country around Helena.

The showpiece is the **Lewis Thrust**. An enormous slab of Belt
Supergroup rock slid **more than 50 miles east** along a nearly
horizontal fault plane and came to rest **on top of much younger
Cretaceous shale and sandstone** of the Western Interior Seaway. The
result is Glacier National Park: **1.4-billion-year-old mudstone sitting
on 75-million-year-old mudstone**, with Chief Mountain the classic
erosional klippe left stranded east of the range front.

Two climbing consequences follow directly and are not obvious:

1. The Belt rock in Glacier is **structurally transported but not
   metamorphosed** — it did not get baked or squeezed hard enough to
   heal. It is 1.4 Ga sediment with 1.4 Ga bedding planes, moved
   wholesale and then fractured through by the transport.
2. The thrust put **strong rock on weak rock**. The Cretaceous shale
   beneath the thrust is incompetent, and the range front is therefore
   prone to large-scale slumping as well as ordinary rockfall.

### 1.6 Laramide basement uplifts, ~75–50 Ma

Where Sevier deformation was thin-skinned, the **Laramide** was
thick-skinned: basement blocks driven up along reverse faults, breaking
their sedimentary cover into asymmetric arches and dropping deep basins
between them. Montana's Laramide is divided into three domains: the
**exposed basement-cored arches of southwest Montana**, the
**structurally inverted central Montana platform**, and **low-amplitude
basement arches beneath the northern plains**.

Named uplifts that carry climbing:

- **Beartooth uplift** — a basement-cored foreland uplift thrust
  northeast over the Bighorn Basin margin along the Beartooth thrust,
  raising a coherent block of Archean crust to 12,000 ft. Apatite
  fission-track work documents both Laramide and post-Laramide uplift.
- **Bridger Range** — the surface expression of the ancestral **Bridger
  arch**, whose Laramide reverse and thrust movement also created the
  asymmetric **Crazy Mountains Basin** next door. The range is a tilted,
  faulted panel of the full Cambrian-to-Mississippian section, which is
  exactly why you can walk the whole stratigraphy on one ridge.
- **Big Belt Mountains** — within the Helena salient, a compound
  structure combining thrusting and basement involvement, exposing
  Belt-through-Paleozoic rock; the two developed climbing gulches cut
  its Madison-bearing flank.
- **Little Belt Mountains** — an Archean gneiss-and-schist core with a
  Belt and Paleozoic cover, exhumed in the **Late Cretaceous** (recent
  thermochronology ties Little Belt exhumation to the development of the
  Helena salient) and later intruded by post-Cretaceous laccoliths,
  stocks and dikes of syenite-porphyry and diorite.
- **Crazy Mountains** — a Laramide *basin* filled with Paleocene–Eocene
  sediment, later intruded and hardened by a swarm of Eocene stocks,
  sills, dikes and laccoliths that now stand up as the range.

### 1.7 The Boulder Batholith and the Elkhorn volcanics, ~81–74 Ma

The **Boulder Batholith** is the granite of southwestern Montana: over
**6,000 km²** between Helena and Butte, emplaced between **81.7 ± 1.4 Ma
and 73.7 ± 0.6 Ma**. Its dominant body is the **Butte Quartz Monzonite**
— now formally the **Butte Granite** — with a U-Pb zircon age of
**74.5 ± 0.9 Ma**, ringed by roughly coeval satellite plutons. The
batholith was emplaced along a reactivated Paleoproterozoic suture in
the **Great Falls tectonic zone**, and it is shallow: it intrudes, and
is partly roofed by, its own volcanic cover, the **Elkhorn Mountains
Volcanics** (andesitic to latitic welded tuff, tuff breccia, lapilli
tuff and lava flows). A batholith that crystallised at shallow depth
cools relatively fast and unloads relatively fast, and the result at the
surface is the sculpture southwest Montana is known for: rounded,
grus-shedding, joint-bounded granite towers and boulder fields.

**What a climber gets:** Spire Rock, Homestake Pass, the Lower Pipestone
boulder field, Humbug Spires, Sheep Mountain, Maiden Rock — and, at
Butte, the Berkeley Pit, because the same batholith hosts the copper.

Butte's other rock is the **Lowland Creek Volcanics**, a ~6,000-ft-thick
Eocene quartz-latite ash-flow and lava sequence covering **>600 square
miles** north and west of town, active **52.9–48.6 Ma** with major
tuff-forming eruptions at **52.9 ± 0.14 and 51.8 ± 0.14 Ma** (older K-Ar
work gave 48–50 Ma and the unit was long miscalled Oligocene). Most of
it is welded tuff.

### 1.8 The Idaho Batholith and the Bitterroot core complex

West of the Bitterroot Valley the rock is the **Bitterroot lobe of the
Idaho Batholith**, Cretaceous-to-Eocene granitic plutonic rock, and the
**Bitterroot metamorphic core complex** — an exhumed **mid-crustal
plutonic–metamorphic complex** that formed during Cordilleran crustal
thickening and was then unroofed during **Paleocene–Eocene collapse** of
the orogen.

The mechanics matter to climbers more than usual here. Extension was
taken up on the **Bitterroot mylonite zone**, a **500–1,500 m thick
shear zone** that deforms granitic intrusions as young as **53–52 Ma**
along with older high-grade metamorphic rocks. Exhumation ran from
**ca. 53 to 30 Ma**, recorded as a downward progression from
amphibolite-facies mylonitisation to greenschist-facies mylonitisation
to chloritic brecciation to brittle faulting. An undeformed rhyolite
dike cutting the mylonitic fabric gives **46.4 ± 0.8 Ma** as a minimum
age; deformed granitoids give **48 ± 1 Ma** as a maximum for
mylonitisation. Younger apatite fission-track ages (**≤26 Ma**) on the
eastern range front show the **present topographic front is Miocene
high-angle faulting** that widened the Bitterroot Valley.

That eastern range front — the abrupt, planar, 6,000-ft wall the
Bitterroot canyons cut into — **is the exhumed mylonite front itself**,
a fossil shear zone turned into a mountain face. It is why the canyon
mouths are so uniformly steep, why the rock is foliated where climbers
call it granite, and why the walls are so continuous.

### 1.9 Eocene volcanism: Absaroka, Challis, and the intrusive centres

The Eocene lit up the whole northern Rockies. The **Absaroka Volcanic
Supergroup** was active **53–43 Ma** and produced roughly **29,000 km³**
of andesite, dacite, rhyolite and pyroclastic deposits; the North
Absaroka Wilderness is underlain almost entirely by Eocene andesitic and
basaltic volcanic rock. This is the pile that makes the crumbling,
banded, spectacular-but-unclimbable ramparts east of Yellowstone and
along the east side of Paradise Valley.

Coeval with it — the **Challis magmatic flare-up** — the **Crazy
Mountains intrusions** were emplaced at **~52–48 Ma**, sodium-rich
alkaline mafic syenites and trachytes alongside potassium-rich diorites
and granodiorites. The Little Belt and Castle Mountains intrusive
centres belong to the same broad episode of shallow stocks, sills and
laccoliths punched into a Paleozoic and Mesozoic cover.

The climbing significance of Eocene volcanism in Montana is largely
**negative**, and it is worth saying plainly: welded ash-flow tuff makes
superb climbing in New Mexico, but Montana's Eocene volcanic pile is
dominantly **andesitic lava, breccia and volcaniclastic sediment**,
which weathers to loose banded cliffs and does not hold routes. Where
Eocene magmatism gave Montana good rock, it did so **intrusively** —
the hypabyssal stocks and laccoliths of the Crazies and the Castles,
which are coarse, joint-bounded and competent.

### 1.10 Extension, the Intermountain Seismic Belt, and the modern valleys

From the **Miocene** onward, western Montana has been pulled apart. The
**Intermountain Seismic Belt** runs through the state from the Flathead
Lake region southeast to Yellowstone — a 100–200 km wide, curvilinear
zone of shallow (<20 km) earthquakes dominated by **normal to
oblique-normal faulting**, with spectacular late Quaternary scarps.
Range-bounding normal faults have produced repeated surface ruptures of
up to several metres. The Bitterroot, Flathead, Mission, Gallatin,
Madison and Deer Lodge valleys are all extensional basins, and their
bounding faults are the reason the range fronts are straight, steep and
young-looking.

For climbers this means two things: the canyon mouths are **fault-line
features**, so the rock at the range front is often more shattered than
the rock a mile back in the canyon; and Montana is **seismically live** —
the 1959 Hebgen Lake earthquake (M 7.3) dropped the Madison Slide, and
the 2017 Lincoln earthquake (M 5.8) was felt across the western half of
the state.

### 1.11 The Pleistocene: ice, and the lake

Almost every good Montana crag owes its shape to ice. The **Cordilleran
Ice Sheet** covered the northwest; **alpine glaciers** filled the
valleys of every range above about 7,000 ft during the **Pinedale
(Wisconsinan) glaciation**, roughly **22,000–13,000 years ago**, and
during earlier Bull Lake advances.

Glaciers do three things a climber cares about:

1. **They cut U-shaped valleys with steep, clean walls.** Blodgett
   Canyon's 1,200-ft walls, East Rosebud's, Hyalite's, and Glacier's
   entire architecture are glacial trough walls. This is the source of
   "the Yosemite of Montana."
2. **They strip weathered rock and expose fresh rock,** which is why
   glaciated granite climbs so much better than unglaciated granite —
   and why the *unglaciated* tops of the same formations are grussy.
3. **They unload the rock,** and unloading opens **exfoliation
   (sheeting) joints** parallel to the new surface. Those joints are
   both the flakes you pull on and the flakes that fall.

Then there was the lake. **Glacial Lake Missoula** formed when the
Purcell Trench lobe of the Cordilleran ice sheet dammed the Clark Fork
near the Idaho border. It covered roughly **3,000 square miles** across
the Missoula, Bitterroot, Jocko, Little Bitterroot, Mission and Ninemile
valleys, reached nearly **2,000 ft deep** at the ice dam, and held over
**500 cubic miles** of water — then failed catastrophically and refilled,
dozens of times. Its shorelines are the horizontal stripes on Mount
Jumbo above Missoula. **The mouths of the Bitterroot canyons — Kootenai,
Bass Creek, Blodgett, Mill Creek — were under hundreds of feet of lake
water**, which is one reason the lower canyons are choked with
fine-grained lake sediment and the crags start where the water line
ended.

## Part 2 — Area by area

Ordered to match the gazetteer (`content/gazetteer/montana.md`). Every
table row there gets a subsection here; where the gazetteer's row is
itself unverified, the geology is given for the place as best it can be
established and the uncertainty is carried forward rather than hidden.

### 2.1 Bozeman area

The Bozeman climbing scene sits at a **three-way geologic junction**, and
this is the single most useful fact about it. Within forty minutes of
town a climber can touch:

- **Archean gneiss**, 2.7–3.8 Ga, in the lower Gallatin Canyon and low in
  Hyalite;
- **Mississippian Madison limestone**, ~340 Ma, on the Bridger crest, at
  Storm Castle, and on Bozeman Pass;
- **Eocene Absaroka volcanic rock**, ~53–43 Ma, up Hyalite Canyon.

The gazetteer and census currently blur this. Both call Hyalite
"limestone." **That is wrong, and the correction matters** — see §2.1.2.

#### 2.1.1 Gallatin Canyon

**The unit.** Gallatin Canyon has two rock types and two climbing areas,
and the split is clean. The **gneiss area near the 35 MPH bridge** — the
Mad Mile — is **Archean gneiss of the Wyoming Province**, described in
regional sources as **3.8–2.7 Ga**, formed at depths **greater than
25–30 km** and temperatures around **780 °C**. **Gallatin Tower** is
"the prominent gneiss formation on the west side of the Gallatin River
just north of the 35 MPH bridge," and it is the concentrated classic
area, holding "some of the better cracks in the canyon." The second
area, **Storm Castle Creek** on the east side of the canyon, is a
**limestone sport area**; Storm Castle Peak itself is described as "a
younger rock, a contrasting pale limestone of the Mississippian Age" —
i.e. **Madison Group** `[inferred to Madison Group specifically —
unverified; the source says Mississippian limestone, which in this part
of Montana is the Madison]`.

**Setting.** The gneiss is basement of the **Wyoming Province**,
metamorphosed at mid-to-lower crustal depth in the Archean and brought
back to the surface by **Laramide uplift of the Gallatin/Madison block**,
then cut through by the Gallatin River. The limestone is the Paleozoic
cover on the flank of the same uplift. The canyon is a river gorge, not
a glacial trough — the valley below Big Sky was not filled by trunk ice
the way the Bitterroot canyons were — so exposure here comes from
fluvial incision, which leaves less clean rock and more vegetated ledge
than a glacial wall.

**Climbing consequence — gneiss.** Gneiss is a *banded* rock: alternating
light (quartz–feldspar) and dark (biotite–hornblende) layers, the
"zebra-stripe" texture. That banding is a mechanical anisotropy, and it
governs everything:

- **Foliation-parallel splitting.** The rock prefers to part along the
  micaceous bands, so gneiss cliffs are full of flakes, thin
  detached-looking laminae and foliation-parallel corners. Some of those
  flakes are bomber; some ring hollow. Learn the difference.
- **Cracks are joint-controlled, not bedding-controlled,** which is why
  Gallatin Tower's classics are true splitters and dihedral systems —
  the standard route's first pitch takes a **two-foot-wide crack** (a
  full offwidth/squeeze) and the second pitch a **double crack line
  through two short overhangs**.
- **Friction is excellent** on the quartzofeldspathic bands, and the
  weathered-out mica layers give positive incut edges.
- **Bolts hold very well** in unfoliated gneiss, and badly if the hole
  crosses a mica-rich band; expect sport development to be concentrated
  on the limestone side of the canyon for this reason.
- **Hazard:** gneiss weathers to **grus** on unglaciated surfaces, and
  the tops of Gallatin formations are grussy and vegetated. The
  roadside base is directly under the highway shoulder in places.

**Climbing consequence — Storm Castle limestone.** Madison-Group
limestone at a south-facing roadside crag is exactly the recipe for
steep, pocketed sport climbing on a winter-friendly aspect, and that is
what Storm Castle is: sport routes **5.10 to stiff 5.13**, with
*Scorched Earth* on a **south-facing wall that can be climbed through
the winter**, and *The Cave* holding the steep hard lines. Solution
pockets and vugs from the Mission Canyon's fossil karst are the hold
inventory; chert nodules stand proud as sharp knobs; bedding partings
make the roofs. Note for the historical record that **Scorched Earth was
the crag whose Forest Service closure helped trigger the founding of the
Southwest Montana Climbers Coalition** (see `research/featured/bridgers.md`).

#### 2.1.2 Hyalite Canyon — a correction to the gazetteer

**The gazetteer and census both give Hyalite's rock as "limestone," for
both the rock climbing and the ice. Neither is right.**

**The units.** Hyalite Canyon cuts down through the northern **Gallatin
Range**, which is built of the **Eocene Absaroka Volcanic Supergroup**.
The named unit at the top of the canyon is the **Hyalite Peak
Volcanics**, "a sequence of andesitic flows and flow breccias thought to
be the youngest volcanic unit in the northern Gallatin Range," assigned
to the **Washburn Group of the Absaroka Volcanic Supergroup**, early
Tertiary (Eocene). Beneath the volcanic pile, **Hyalite Canyon's
basement Archean metamorphic rocks are covered by more than 4,400 feet
of Paleozoic and Mesozoic sandstone, shale and limestone**; driving up
the canyon you pass **Archean gneiss**, then the **Flathead Sandstone,
Wolsey Shale and Meagher Limestone**, before the volcanic rock takes
over.

So the honest one-line answer is: **Hyalite's rock climbing is on
Archean gneiss low in the canyon and Eocene volcanic rock high in it;
Hyalite's ice forms on Eocene volcanic cliffs.** The Cambrian limestone
is a band you drive past, not the medium anyone climbs.

**Rock climbing.** "Hyalite Canyon also features some very high quality
and popular **gneiss** rock climbing lower in the canyon at **Practice
Rock** and **Crocodile Rock**, with a lesser-visited **volcanic** area
higher up in the canyon on the **Magic Wall**." Practice Rock is a small
gneiss crag with trad, sport and mixed lines **5.6–5.12d**; Crocodile
Rock is roadside on the north side of Hyalite Canyon Road with routes
**5.9–5.12a**. Note also for the corridor pages: **Practice Rock is in
Hyalite, not the Bridgers** — a confusion the Bridgers research file
already flags.

**Ice — why it forms here and nowhere else nearby.** This is the best
geology-to-climbing story in the state and it is worth telling properly.
The Absaroka pile in Hyalite is **not** a stack of clean lava flows; it
is dominantly **epiclastic — lahars (volcanic mudflows), volcanic
gravel, sand and mud — with subordinate ash-fall pyroclastics and
relatively rare andesitic lava flows**. Those alternating layers have
wildly different permeability: **lahar and volcaniclastic beds are
permeable to groundwater, lava flows are not.** Groundwater sinks
through a lahar bed, hits an impermeable flow, and comes out sideways at
the contact — as a line of springs partway up a cliff. Stack that
relationship a dozen times and you get Hyalite: **a canyon of
cliff-banded, spring-fed waterfalls**, each hanging off a permeability
boundary, each freezing every winter. Add a **north-trending canyon on
the cold side of the Continental Divide**, which keeps the walls out of
the sun, and you have **over 150 ice routes in less than three square
miles** — the most concentrated natural ice-climbing venue in North
America.

Palisade Falls, the canyon's showpiece, freezes over **vertical
hexagonal columnar jointing** in an andesite flow — the same cooling
joints that make columnar basalt.

**Climbing consequence.** Two very different rocks:

- **Andesite lava flows** — "mostly solid rock on the andesite," with
  "clean hand cracks in perfect andesite rock" reported. Columnar
  jointing gives corners, flares and column-bounded cracks; the rock is
  hard and holds tool placements and bolts well.
- **Lahar / volcanic conglomerate** — the notorious "**classic Hyalite
  conglomerate choss**," singled out at the base of Mummy Four as
  "uninspiring for pure dry-tooling but a necessary evil." A lahar is a
  matrix-supported mudflow deposit: rounded and angular clasts floating
  in a fine matrix, cemented weakly. Clasts pull out. Tool placements
  key on clasts that then rotate. **Bolts in a lahar bed are only as
  good as the matrix, which is why Hyalite's mixed routes are bolted
  where they are and not where you would like them to be.** The
  canyon's mixed-climbing culture — conservative bolting on beginner dry
  routes, an explicit "choss scratching" idiom — is a direct cultural
  adaptation to a volcaniclastic rock.
- **Seepage is the point, not a defect.** The same permeability contrast
  that makes the ice makes the rock wet: expect wet streaks and weeping
  bands on summer rock routes at exactly the horizons that freeze in
  winter.

#### 2.1.3 Bridger Range / Bridger Canyon

**The unit.** **Madison Group limestone, Mississippian, ~360–320 Ma**
(commonly cited as ~340 Ma for deposition). "Along the trail to
Sacagawea Peak you'll see mostly the massive limestones of the Madison
Formation … which form the prominent cliffs that outline the skyline of
the Bridger Mountains." **Ross Peak (9,003–9,008 ft) is explicitly
"composed of Mississippian-age limestone of the Madison Group."** The
corridor page's "Madison limestone" is therefore **correct and can be
sharpened to Madison Group, Mission Canyon Limestone the likely
cliff-former** `[member-level assignment inferred — unverified: the
inference rests on the Mission Canyon being the massive cliff-forming
upper Madison across Montana, and on the described cliffs being massive
rather than thin-bedded]`.

The Bridgers also expose the full section beneath the Madison. The
Sacagawea Peak ridge walks down through **Sappington, Three Forks,
Jefferson and Maywood Formations** (Devonian) into the Cambrian —
**Snowy Range Formation, Pilgrim Limestone, Park Shale, Meagher
Limestone, Wolsey Shale, Flathead Sandstone** — and finally onto Archean
basement. This is why the range is a teaching classic and why its rock
quality is famously variable: **it is not one rock, it is fourteen**.

**Structure.** The range is the surface expression of the **Bridger
arch**, a Laramide basement-cored uplift; "with the help of a large
thrust fault, the rocks buckled and the continental crust pushed the
range up," and the range records "at least five major mountain-forming
events." The Bridgers run **~43 miles (70 km) north–south** and expose
"**a thick sequence of sedimentary rocks totalling about 27,000 feet
(8,200 m), deposited from the Mesoproterozoic Belt Supergroup
(approximately 1.4 billion years ago) through the Cretaceous**." The
Cretaceous-to-Eocene contraction here was **thin-skinned, Sevier-style
fold-and-thrust deformation**, and the range's **Pass Fault** "had to be
active in the Paleozoic as well, because the thicknesses of the
sedimentary units in the northern and southern Bridger Mountains are
different" — an inherited basement structure reused twice. The section
is tilted and faulted, which is why the resistant Madison forms a
**discontinuous ridge-line band of cliffs along ~50 miles of range**
rather than one continuous wall — exactly the "50 miles of rock" the
climbing sources describe. Note for the corridor page: **the Bridgers
contain Belt Supergroup rock at their base too**, so this one range
holds nearly every rock type in this file except the batholiths.

**Climbing consequence.** The Bridgers' reputation — quality "from
extremely good to extremely poor" — is a **stratigraphic** fact, not a
matter of luck:

- **Where a crag is in massive Mission Canyon limestone,** the rock is
  the good end: **Wolverine Bowl**, described by climbers as "stunning,
  well-weathered blue" limestone and by *Outside Bozeman* as **"the best
  limestone around,"** steep, technical, **sharp-edged and pocketed**,
  hard on the fingers, sustained enough to want a 70 m rope and ~16
  draws. Well-weathered grey-blue Madison develops the classic
  sharp-edged, pocketed, incut surface as rainwater etches out the
  matrix between fossil fragments and dissolves along micro-joints. It
  is superb, and it shreds skin.
- **Where a crag is low in the Madison (Lodgepole) or in the Cambrian
  band,** the rock is thin-bedded with shale partings, and the result is
  the friable, "kitty-litter" rock reported at **Rocky Canyon (Morgan
  Cemetery)** on the south face of Blacktail Mountain in the north end
  of the range `[unit assignment inferred — unverified; the inference
  rests on the range's stratigraphy and on the reported friability being
  characteristic of bedded, shale-parted carbonate rather than massive
  Mission Canyon]`.
- **Ross Peak** is bolted multipitch on **sharp, sometimes slabby**
  Madison to a summit, "generally good" with a few poor pitches — a
  normal profile for a peak-scale route that crosses more than one bed.
- **Altitude and aspect.** These are 8,000–9,600 ft crags with a
  mid-June-to-early-October window; freeze–thaw at that elevation
  loosens blocks every winter, so early-season rock is the most
  dangerous rock.
- **Bolts** hold well in sound Madison; the risk is bolting into the
  case-hardened rind or across a bedding parting. Sport development is
  concentrated at Wolverine Bowl for good reason.

#### 2.1.4 Bozeman Pass area (Bear Canyon, Chestnut Mountain, Frog Rock, Practice Rock, Squaw Creek)

**The units.** The gazetteer's "mixed limestone/gneiss" is broadly right
but can be improved. **Bozeman Pass "features limestone and is primarily
sport climbing"** and is called "a prime example of local limestone
outcrops" — Paleozoic carbonate on the structural saddle between the
Bridger and Gallatin uplifts `[Madison Group assignment inferred —
unverified]`. **Bear Canyon is different: sandstone is reported there**
— "you can find sandstone in Bear Canyon, and in the Bridgers between
bands of limestone." That is a real distinction and the gazetteer does
not carry it.

`[Inferred from regional geology — unverified]` **The Bear Canyon
sandstone is most likely Cretaceous clastic rock of the Colorado Group
or Kootenai Formation, or the Cambrian Flathead Sandstone where the
section is exposed low.** The inference rests on: (a) the Bozeman
Pass–Bear Canyon corridor exposing the Mesozoic section on the flank of
the Bridger arch, and (b) the Flathead being the only pre-Mesozoic
sandstone in this section. **Do not print a formation name for Bear
Canyon.** The MBMG Bozeman 30′×60′ geologic map (open file, text
published) would settle it in one look.

**Climbing consequence.** If any Bozeman Pass crag is genuinely
sandstone, then the **wet-rock ethic applies there and nowhere else near
Bozeman**: sandstone loses a large fraction of its strength when
saturated, holds snap and protection pulls, and 24–72 hours of drying is
the standard. The limestone crags have no such rule. This is worth a
line in the guidebook because a climber who learns "Bozeman rock is fine
in the wet" on Gallatin gneiss will get it badly wrong in Bear Canyon.

The individual crags remain `[unverified]` beyond the *Rock Climbs of
Southwest Montana* table of contents, as the census says; the geology
above does not change that.

#### 2.1.5 Madison River area / Big Sky (Beehive Basin, Red Cliff)

**The unit.** **Archean gneiss.** Beehive Peak sits in the **Spanish
Peaks** of the northern **Madison Range**, in the Lee Metcalf
Wilderness; "the rock here, **1.6-billion-year-old gneiss**, began
crystallising during the Precambrian Age and has eroded away to sculpt
buttresses, fins and walls," and "at the southeast end, cracks in gneiss
fins attract rock climbers." Climbing sources describe **multipitch trad
routes 5.6–5.11 on high-quality "granite gneiss"** on Beehive's
southeast face and on **The Prow**, "an impressive gneissic tower at the
head of Beehive Basin," first climbed in **1976**.

**Note a real discrepancy.** The Beehive source gives **1.6 Ga**; the
Gallatin Canyon sources give **3.8–2.7 Ga** for the same Wyoming
Province basement. Both are in circulation. The Spanish Peaks basement
is generally assigned to the Archean Wyoming Province, so **1.6 Ga is
probably either a Paleoproterozoic metamorphic overprint age or an
error**; this file carries both rather than averaging them.
`[Age discrepancy unresolved — unverified.]`

**Setting.** Basement-cored **Laramide uplift** of the Madison Range,
scoured by **Pleistocene alpine glaciers** — the "buttresses, fins and
walls" and the cirque that holds Beehive Basin are glacial. The Big Sky
corridor along US 191 runs **from the 35 MPH bridge south to Red Cliff
Campground**, and the outlying crags in that stretch are in the same
gneiss.

**Climbing consequence.** Glaciated Archean gneiss is the best
crystalline climbing medium in southwest Montana: ice stripped the
weathered rind, so the surface is fresh rather than grussy; foliation
and joints give **fins** with clean **crack systems** on their faces;
friction is high. The costs are the usual alpine ones — a 9,000-ft
cirque, a wilderness approach, a short season, and afternoon
thunderstorms. Loose flakes along foliation planes are the specific
hazard.

### 2.2 Butte area

Butte climbing is Boulder Batholith climbing, with one important
exception (Maiden Rock, §2.2.5) that the gazetteer has filed in the
wrong category.

**The batholith, restated for the crag pages.** The **Boulder Batholith**
is "a relatively small batholith in southwestern Montana … exposed at
the surface as granite — more specifically **quartz monzonite** — and
serving as the host rock for the rich mineralised deposits at Butte." It
lies between Butte and Helena and between the Deer Lodge (upper Clark
Fork) and Broadwater (upper Missouri) valleys, covers **>6,000 km²**,
and consists of **at least seven and possibly as many as fourteen
discrete plutons** emplaced about **73–78 Ma** (the wider published
window for the batholith and its satellites is **81.7 ± 1.4 to 73.7 ±
0.6 Ma**; the Butte Granite itself gives **74.5 ± 0.9 Ma** by U-Pb
zircon). Published composition-and-age statements vary — one source
gives Butte quartz monzonite as 69–87 Ma — and this file carries the
peer-reviewed 81–74 Ma range as the reliable one.

**Quartz monzonite vs. granite, for the guidebook's Rock cell.**
Climbers say granite; geologists say quartz monzonite. Both are right in
the way that matters: quartz monzonite is "somewhat related to granite
but containing far less quartz" — roughly equal alkali feldspar and
plagioclase, with 5–20% quartz, where a true granite has more. **In the
hand and under the boots it is a coarse, light, crystalline plutonic
rock and it climbs like granite.** The one practical difference is that
less quartz and more feldspar means **more of the rock is weatherable**,
which is exactly why the Boulder Batholith weathers to rounded boulders
rather than to Yosemite-style domes.

**Why the landscape looks like that.** Two processes, both visible from
I-90:

1. **Spheroidal weathering.** Chemical weathering attacks jointed
   bedrock from the joint faces inward, working corners fastest, so a
   joint-bounded cube rots into a sphere with concentric shells of
   decayed rock around a fresh **corestone**. The batholith "was named
   for the prominent rounded boulders that typify its landscape, the
   result of spheroidal weathering of fractured granite."
2. **Stripping.** **Pleistocene periglacial freezing and frost-heaving
   eroded away the soil and exposed the massive boulders** — the
   corestones were rotted out underground and then exhumed. This is why
   the boulders sit *on* the hillside rather than *in* it.

Layered on top of that, **exfoliation** — thin convex sheets sloughing
off the exposed surface, accelerated by frost wedging — rounds the tors
and produces the sheeting flakes that climbers use as holds and
sometimes pull off.

**The general climbing consequence for all four Butte-area granite
areas:**

- **Crack climbing dominates.** The rock's structure is a set of
  intersecting joints; erosion opens them; the result is
  **joint-bounded cracks, offwidths and chimneys** rather than face
  climbing. Spire Rock's crack-and-offwidth reputation is a direct
  reading of batholith jointing.
- **Face holds are crystals and shallow scoops,** not pockets. Feldspar
  phenocrysts stand proud as the matrix weathers; the rock is coarse and
  the friction is very high.
- **Grus and rind.** Feldspar-rich plutonic rock decomposes to grit.
  Expect gravel on ledges, gritty smears, and an outer weathering rind
  on unglaciated surfaces that is softer than the corestone beneath.
  Brush the holds; test the flakes.
- **Bolts** hold extremely well in sound quartz monzonite and badly in a
  spheroidally weathered shell. The visual tell is concentric shelling
  or a punky, sugary surface.
- **Toppling and rockfall.** A tor built of stacked corestones is a
  stack of loose blocks by definition. The summit blocks on batholith
  spires are frequently detached from what they sit on.

#### 2.2.1 Spire Rock (Pipestone Pass)

**The unit.** **Boulder Batholith quartz monzonite** — "Spire Rock is the
classic crag of the Boulder Batholith." Located about **21 miles east of
Butte**, off I-90 at the **Pipestone exit (#241)**, on the dirt road
toward **Delmoe Lake**; "the granite outcroppings poke through the
ground from the Boulder Batholith below." The two formations are the
**King** (larger, holds the multipitch) and the **Queen** (crack and
offwidth, easy top-rope setups, bouldering).

**Climbing consequence.** A textbook batholith tor: the King and Queen
are corestone masses exhumed from a weathered joint network, so the
climbing is up joint-controlled cracks with easy walk-around access to
the tops for anchors — which is exactly why it is a top-rope and
teaching crag. Offwidth is not an accident of taste here; it is what
weathered granite jointing produces at this spacing. Grades stay
moderate (**5.4–5.10**) because the rock offers few blank steep faces.

#### 2.2.2 Homestake Pass

**The unit.** The **Homestake Pluton of the Boulder Batholith**, ~10
miles east of Butte in and around the Continental Divide. The
"distinctive boulder formations … especially noticed along the roadways
ascending and descending Pipestone and Homestake passes near Butte" are
this pluton's exhumed corestones.

**Climbing consequence.** Same rock, same forms, smaller scale — "the
rock is **coarse, solid granite**," and the pass "is scattered with
boulders and small crags with some developed areas and no doubt lots
more to be done," with climbs **up to two pitches**. Named sectors
include **Dragon's Back** ("dramatic"), **Gillette Edge** and **Railroad
Rocks** (park at the westbound I-90 rest area near the top of the pass;
for Dragon's Back, the westbound shoulder near mile marker 238), and the
Butte guidebook carries a separate section on **Whiskey Gulch
bouldering**. The interstate cut means road noise and trivial access;
the down side is that roadside batholith outcrops are the most
frost-shattered ones, and that a corestone landscape beside a highway
has been picked over by frost, traffic vibration and blasting.

#### 2.2.3 Humbug Spires

**The unit.** **Boulder Batholith quartz monzonite** — the BLM's own
description calls Humbug Spires "one of the most spectacular
outcroppings of quartz monzonite within the Boulder Batholith, a large
late Cretaceous granitic intrusion," and notes that the Spires are
"quartz-monzonite, somewhat related to granite but containing far less
quartz."

**Setting and form.** An **11,175-acre BLM Wilderness Study Area** about
24 miles south of Butte off I-15 (Moose Creek exit); **nine of the
spires rise 300–600 ft** above their surroundings and "dramatically show
the effects of erosion." The main trail runs up Moose Creek into the
spires. The Wedge (~500 ft) is the signature summit.

**Why spires and not domes.** The difference between Humbug and a
Yosemite dome is **joint spacing**. Where a pluton has widely spaced
sheeting joints you get domes; where it has a closely spaced,
steeply-dipping orthogonal joint set, weathering opens the joints into
clefts and leaves the intervening rock standing as **fins and spires**.
Humbug is the second case, and the same jointing that isolates the
spires supplies the crack systems that climb them. The area is
unglaciated, so the spires are **weathered in place** — rounded profiles,
grussy summits, a soft rind on the low-angle faces and much better rock
on the steep ones (steep faces shed their rind).

**Climbing consequence.** Multipitch trad, **5.5–5.10+**, on cracks and
knobs; The Wedge summits at 5.8. Because it is a **WSA**, expect the
usual wilderness-study constraints on fixed anchors and power drilling
and plan for **gear-protected, ground-up** climbing with natural or
minimal fixed belays. Two-rope descents and dirty summit blocks are the
norm. The Moose Creek drainage holds one of Montana's better stands of
old-growth Douglas fir, which is worth mentioning in the book but also
means the approach is shaded, damp, and slow to dry.

#### 2.2.4 Lower Pipestone Forest (bouldering)

**The unit.** **Granite (quartz monzonite) of the Boulder Batholith** —
the area is explicitly described as "granite (quartz monzonite)
bouldering set among sage, junipers, pine and prickly pear cactus,"
spread along the **Delmoe Lake road and Whitetail Road** north of the
"4 Corners," between **Whitehall and Butte** on the east slope of the
Continental Divide. Regional sources call this stretch of the Divide
"more granite climbing than anywhere in the Northern Rockies."

**Climbing consequence.** The exhumed-corestone landscape at boulder
scale — hundreds of blocks, most of them joint-bounded on three sides
and rounded on the fourth. Development history maps the geology neatly:
"people seemed to think that all of the boulders were blank and of
pretty bad quality," and then climbers found "lots of established
problems and numerous excellent first ascents." That is what a
spheroidally weathered granite field looks like before and after
brushing — the rind reads as blank and chossy; the corestone underneath
is excellent. Expect **slopers, crystal crimps and sloping topouts**,
high friction, and pads-on-uneven-talus landings. Many boulders remain
unclimbed, and the ethic is open-project.

#### 2.2.5 Maiden Rock — resolving a gazetteer `[unverified]`

**The gazetteer lists "Maiden Rock, near Butte" under "Other areas not
independently confirmed," on the strength of a single theCrag page. It
is real, it is significant, and it is not granite.**

**What it is.** **Maiden Rock(s) is a limestone sport crag**, "a
secluded limestone sport climbing crag with a high concentration of
'hard' climbing (5.13)," though "it offers all levels of sport climbs";
another source credits the area with "**over 200 climbs on classic
limestone**" and a sector called **Try Hard Maiden** known for "steep
and blank limestone walls … absolutely adored by the upper 5.12 and 5.13
sport climbers." It sits **a short distance off I-15 between Butte and
Dillon, six miles south of Divide**. The approach takes **Maiden Rock
Road over the Big Hole River, then up Lower Canyon Creek 1.5 miles to a
meadow with a large historical lime kiln**.

**That lime kiln is the geologic confirmation.** Lime kilns are built
where there is limestone to burn. Independently, **the Big Hole River
"slices through Maiden Rock Canyon," having begun cutting through the
Pioneer Mountains about 17 million years ago**, and downstream the river
enters the **Divide Valley, an extensional graben** filled with
sediments — a Basin-and-Range structural setting.

`[Inferred from regional geology — unverified]` **The Maiden Rock
limestone is most likely the Mississippian Madison Group, with the
massive Mission Canyon Limestone the cliff-former.** The inference rests
on (a) the Madison being the dominant cliff-forming carbonate of
southwest Montana, (b) the Pioneer Mountains front exposing the
Paleozoic section, and (c) the described character — steep, blank,
sustained walls carrying hard sport climbing — matching massive Mission
Canyon rather than bedded Lodgepole. **Do not print a formation name
until an MBMG quad is read.**

**Access caution to carry to the gazetteer:** *the climbing is reported
to be on private property, with camping on surrounding BLM land.* That
is a materially different access picture from the BLM/USFS pattern of
the rest of the Butte entries and belongs on the page.

**Climbing consequence.** Steep Madison-type limestone at a low
elevation on a river canyon: expect **pockets, tufa-like calcite
runnels where seeps have been active, edges and pinches**, a
polished-in-places sharp surface, and the standard limestone hazards —
seepage after snowmelt (the canyon drains a large karst catchment), an
occasional loose flake in the weathering rind, and bolts that hold well
in solid rock but not in the rind. A **5.13-heavy grade distribution is
itself diagnostic**: it means steep, featured, continuous rock, which in
Montana means well-karsted massive Madison.

### 2.3 Helena area

Helena's climbing splits cleanly on the same line as Butte's: **granite
of the Boulder Batholith to the south and west, Paleozoic limestone to
the east and north.** The Helena Valley sits between them, and the
Helena Climbers' Coalition maintains both.

The regional frame, from Montana's own geologic literature: the **South
Hills** of town "consists of thick Paleozoic sedimentary rocks, uplifted
and tilted down to the south, exposing the Devonian-age **Jefferson
dolomite**, **Three Forks shale**, and Mississippian **Madison
limestone** atop Mount Ascension," while "the broad crustal arch of the
**Big Belt Mountains** on the east side of the Helena Valley exposes
some of the oldest rocks in the region — Precambrian Belt Supergroup
sedimentary rocks," with the range core "mostly pale grey mudstones of
the **Greyson Formation** and light-grey limestone of the **Newland
Formation**, moderately deformed and recrystallised." Around Townsend,
patches of Tertiary **Renova Formation** sit on Deep Creek Pass,
"which indicates that these rocks were once under a valley floor as
recently as 25 million years ago and have been uplifted into mountains
since then" — an important reminder that this is a **young, actively
rising** landscape.

**A live ambiguity to state honestly.** The Big Belts contain *two*
limestones: the Mesoproterozoic **Newland Formation** limestone in the
range core, and the Mississippian **Madison Group** on the flanks. The
gulch crags sit on the western flank. The reasons for assigning them to
the Madison are given crag-by-crag below, but no map was read and the
alternative is not absurd.

#### 2.3.1 Sheep Mountain (Clancy)

**The unit.** **Boulder Batholith granite / quartz monzonite.** Sheep
Mountain rises above Lump Gulch on **BLM land** south of Helena, reached
via Lump Gulch Road then Sheep Mountain Road; the climbing is on
"granite cracks and towers," with "boulder fields, stone pinnacles and
slab faces." The Boulder Batholith "lies roughly between Butte and
Helena," and Sheep Mountain is inside that footprint.
`[Specific pluton unidentified — unverified; the batholith comprises
7–14 plutons and no source names which one underlies Sheep Mountain.]`

**Climbing consequence.** The same spheroidally weathered, joint-bounded
batholith rock as Butte, but with more of it and a wider grade span
(**5.easy–5.14**, reportedly including Montana's first 5.14 at the
Devil's Thumb / Middle Rock / Right Rock cluster). The mixed inventory
the gazetteer records — **trad cracks, sport, mixed and bouldering** —
is exactly what a granite tor field with both slabs and steep faces
produces:

- **Cracks and towers** on the joint-bounded pinnacles (Haystack, Happy
  Hour) — walk-off tops, easy top-ropes, moderate grades.
- **Slab faces** on the exfoliation surfaces — friction climbing on
  crystals, sparse gear, hence bolts.
- **Steep bolted faces** on the shed-rind sides of the larger masses,
  where the hard grades live.
- **Bouldering** on the exhumed corestones scattered across the hillside.

Grus underfoot, gritty topouts and detached summit blocks are the
hazards; the rock is otherwise very good.

#### 2.3.2 Avalanche Gulch

**The unit.** Limestone on the western flank of the **Big Belt
Mountains**, east of Canyon Ferry Lake, in the Helena–Lewis and Clark
National Forest. Climbing sources agree on the character: "roadside
sport climbing on **high quality limestone**," "routes that tend to be
more moderate than its northern neighbor [Hellgate]," and "the complex
canyon creates shade and makes this an inviting summer destination."
Sixteen named crags; **Nude Beach** the most developed.

`[Inferred from regional geology — unverified]` **The Avalanche Gulch
cliffs are most probably Mississippian Madison Group limestone, with
Mission Canyon Limestone the cliff-former.** The inference rests on (a)
the Big Belts' structure — Belt Supergroup core, Paleozoic section
dipping off the western flank, which is where the gulches cut; (b) the
neighbouring **Gates of the Mountains** gorge, in the same structural
package a short distance north, being explicitly mapped as **Mission
Canyon Formation of the Madison Group**, deposited ~325 Ma; and (c) the
climbers' description of *pocketed* limestone, which fits the karsted
Mission Canyon far better than the recrystallised Belt Newland
limestone. The alternative — Newland Formation limestone — is not
excluded and would change the age from ~340 Ma to ~1,450 Ma.

**Climbing consequence.** A shaded, complex, north-to-northeast-trending
canyon in massive karsted carbonate gives:

- **Pockets and solution features** as the primary hold type; **jugs
  where a bedding parting or a collapsed vug has opened**; sharp incut
  edges elsewhere.
- **Moderate angles** — Avalanche is the moderate canyon (**5.6–5.11
  ish**), Hellgate the steeper one, and that difference is a difference
  in how deeply the two gulches have cut into the massive unit.
- **Bolt-friendly rock.** Solid Mission Canyon takes stainless expansion
  bolts well; the local practice of tagging routes at the first bolt
  with name, grade and developer (reported at Nude Beach) reflects a
  well-organised sport crag on good stone.
- **Shade means slow drying.** Karst limestone drains through itself,
  so after a wet spell a shaded gulch crag stays damp for days while the
  hillside above looks dry. Seeps at bedding partings are normal.
- **Hazards:** the OHV traffic on the access road, which every source
  mentions, plus the ordinary limestone risks — an occasional loose
  block along a bedding plane, and rockfall from the rim above the
  crags.

#### 2.3.3 Hellgate Gulch

**The unit.** Same package as Avalanche, one drainage north: **"the
canyons on the western side [of the Big Belts] have abundant limestone
outcrops and cliffs, and some of the rock is even good quality,"** with
Hellgate specifically holding "**larger, more exposed walls of pocketed
limestone**" ("funky pockets"), 30 miles southeast of Helena and 8 miles
past Canyon Ferry Dam. `[Madison Group / Mission Canyon inferred —
unverified, same basis as §2.3.2.]`

**Climbing consequence.** Bigger walls mean a thicker exposure of the
massive unit and a steeper canyon profile, so Hellgate gets both the
harder sport routes (**5.7–5.12+**) and the backcountry multipitch. The
"funky pockets" are worth explaining to readers: **Mission Canyon
solution pockets are not the smooth, drilled-looking monos of French
limestone.** They are irregular, often interconnected, sometimes opening
into voids behind the face, because they are inherited from a **fossil
karst system** — Mississippian caves and dissolution voids that were
buried, filled, and are now being re-exhumed. That is also why they are
sometimes full of soft fill, and why a pocket can be deeper and hollower
than it looks. The corollary caution: **a pocket in Madison limestone
can be the rim of a thin case-hardened shell over a filled void.** Tap
it.

The regional caveat that "some of the rock is even good quality" is the
honest summary — the Big Belts include real choss, and Avalanche's
reputation rests on being better than the average of its own range.

#### 2.3.4 Beaver Creek / Refrigerator Canyon (York)

**The unit.** Limestone, and the strongest case in the Big Belts for a
firm Madison assignment. **Beaver Creek "flows from the crest of the Big
Belts to the Missouri River, cutting through many outcrops of limestone,
creating some of the area's best and most extensive sport crags,"** and
**Refrigerator Canyon is a slot canyon carved by a tributary to Beaver
Creek** — "a narrow canyon with **200-foot-high limestone walls**," which
at the quarter-mile point narrows to a **10-foot-wide slit carved
through limestone by a small mountain stream**, with air 20 °F cooler
inside than out. The trail continues **into the Gates of the Mountains
Wilderness** and on to Meriwether Landing on the Missouri.

`[Inferred — unverified, but well supported]` **Mission Canyon
Limestone, Madison Group, Mississippian.** The Refrigerator Canyon trail
runs directly into the **Gates of the Mountains**, whose gorge is
explicitly "carved into rocks of Mississippian age, predominantly the
**Mission Canyon Limestone**, deposited 325 million years ago," and
whose rock "is highly soluble, so it often develops caves and karst
topography."

**Climbing consequence.** A slot canyon is a **joint-guided dissolution
feature**: the stream found a vertical joint in massive soluble rock and
enlarged it. That has direct consequences for the climbing.

- **The walls are joint faces** — planar, steep, continuous, and
  parallel. This is why a canyon with 200-ft walls yields **over 200
  pitches** including **multipitch classics on the Eye of the Needle**,
  and why the vast majority of the routes are concentrated in the upper
  reaches near the trailhead where the slot is deepest.
- **Quality varies from "typical Big Belt choss to some of the region's
  best,"** and the discriminator is whether a given wall is in massive
  Mission Canyon or in a bedded, chert-and-shale-parted interval.
- **The "refrigerator" effect is a climbing fact, not a curiosity.**
  Cold air pools in the slot. It makes the canyon a summer venue and a
  miserable spring one, and it keeps the rock damp: cold, shaded,
  stream-floored karst is the slowest-drying rock in Montana.
- **Karst hazards:** solution cavities behind the face, calcite crusts,
  and — in a slot canyon with an active stream — **flash-flood
  potential** at the base and ice in the shoulder seasons.

#### 2.3.5 Trout Creek Canyon — a fourth Big Belt drainage, not in the gazetteer

**"Beaver Creek, Trout Creek, Hellgate Gulch and Avalanche Gulch have
the most developed climbing in the Big Belt Mountains."** Trout Creek is
the one the gazetteer misses.

**What it is.** "**Trout Creek Canyon, northeast of Helena, is among the
most interesting and scenic areas in the Big Belt Mountains. Limestone
cliffs rise above the valley for hundreds to thousands of feet.**"
Climbing runs "from traditional and bolt-protected multipitch endeavors
to short single-pitch sport." Access from the lower trailhead at
**Vigilante Campground**, or from the upper canyon after "a long and
circuitous drive from Beaver Creek"; about **3 miles of trail** between
trailheads, bike-friendly. Guidebooks: **Ron Brunckhorst's *The Big
Empty*** and **Jake Mergenthaler's *Central Montana Rock***.

**Climbing consequence.** The rock-quality description is the most
useful sentence anyone has written about Big Belt limestone: "**the rock
quality is variable, ranging from bullet-hard blue-grey slab to brown
choss.**" That is a two-formation, or two-facies, canyon in one line.
**Blue-grey** is fresh, massive, unweathered carbonate — the good
Mission Canyon. **Brown** is the weathering rind, the iron-stained and
partly decalcified surface that develops on bedded, argillaceous or
cherty intervals. **In Montana limestone, colour is the quality tell:
blue-grey is climbable, brown is not.** Carry that rule to Hellgate,
Avalanche, Refrigerator Canyon and the Bridgers, where the same
distinction runs through everything.

#### 2.3.6 Mount Helena

**The unit.** **Madison Group limestone**, in town. Mount Helena City
Park's landmark feature is its **"massive Madison limestone cliffs,"**
and the popular **1906 Trail** "follows the base of the limestone
cliffs." Historically the mountain was quarried for its limestone, which
is the most practical possible confirmation of what it is made of. On
the same tilted Paleozoic panel, the South Hills expose **Jefferson
dolomite** and **Three Forks shale** beneath the Madison, with the
Madison capping **Mount Ascension**.

**Climbing consequence.** A small in-town crag on massive Madison, sport
and top-rope, **5.6–5.11**, developed in coordination with the city.
Expect the good version of Madison — vertical to slightly overhanging
walls, pockets and incut edges, quick-drying south and west aspects —
with two urban-crag qualifications: the rock is at the foot of a heavily
used trail system (rockfall onto hikers is the reason the city was
involved), and the cliff base hosts **Kelsey moss and nesting birds**,
which is a real seasonal-closure consideration for a raptor-bearing
limestone wall. Treat the natural-resource sensitivity of this crag as
part of its geology: **limestone cliffs are the preferred nesting
substrate for raptors across Montana**, and closures on limestone are
more common than on granite for exactly that reason.

### 2.4 Missoula area and the Bitterroot canyons

This is the most geologically coherent climbing region in Montana and
the one whose rock the gazetteer names least accurately. Every developed
Bitterroot crag sits on the **eastern front of the Bitterroot Range**,
and that front is not an ordinary mountain face: **it is an exhumed
ductile shear zone.**

**The regional unit.** The Bitterroots are built on the **Bitterroot
lobe of the Idaho Batholith** — "a 200-mile-long mass of igneous rock
that intruded into the existing sedimentary layers between roughly 100
and 70 million years ago," covering about **35,000 km²** in central
Idaho, composed of **granodiorite and granite with biotite and local
muscovite**. The Bitterroot lobe proper is dated **66–53 Ma** in one
source and Late Cretaceous–Paleocene in another; the granitic intrusive
rocks on the *east* side of the lobe "developed by melting of Belt and
pre-Belt basement schists and gneisses," which is why the eastern
margin is so full of metamorphic rock.

**The structure that makes the crags.** The eastern side of the lobe is
the **Bitterroot metamorphic core complex**, and along the range front
runs the **Bitterroot mylonite zone** — a **500–1,500 m thick** shear
zone that deformed granitic rock as young as **53–52 Ma**. USGS mapping
records "**well-foliated and well-lineated mylonite … along the front of
the Bitterroot Range, with rocks having a granitic protolith south of
Bass Creek**," and notes that "the gneissic structure in the rocks of
the range front is related to the intrusion of the Idaho batholith."
Exhumation ran **~53–30 Ma**; the present, brutally straight range front
is younger still — **Miocene high-angle normal faulting** (apatite
fission-track ages ≤26 Ma) that widened the Bitterroot Valley.

**Then the ice.** Every canyon is a **U-shaped glacial trough**. The
Bitterroot front holds "more than two dozen scenic canyons deeply carved
into the eastern flanks," each rising "from the floor of the Bitterroot
Valley through classic glaciated valleys past ancient moraines, hanging
valleys, vertical granite cliffs and cirques to the summit ridge." Then
**Glacial Lake Missoula** flooded the canyon mouths to depths of
hundreds of feet — its shorelines are still visible on Mount Jumbo and
Mount Sentinel above Missoula.

**The naming problem, stated plainly.** Climbers, the gazetteer, the
census and the tourism boards all say **granite**. The geologists' word
for much of the range front is **mylonitic gneiss / granitic gneiss**,
and at least one climbing source gets it right: climbers here "can
expect **1,000-ft walls of granitic gneiss**, a type of rock that almost
reminds one of granite, though its **metamorphic history gives it a
lighter grayish colour with plenty of pockets and slopers**." Both are
useful and the guidebook should carry both, because **the difference is
audible in the climbing**:

- **True batholith granite** (deeper in the canyons, away from the
  mylonite zone): isotropic, coarse, splitter cracks, crystal face
  holds, superb friction.
- **Mylonitic granitic gneiss** (at and near the range front, which is
  where most of the crags are): a **strong planar foliation** and a
  **stretching lineation**, which means the rock parts along foliation
  planes; the surface is smoother and greyer; holds are **pockets and
  slopers** where boudinaged or recrystallised patches have weathered
  differentially, rather than the sharp crystal edges of fresh granite.
  Cracks are more often foliation-parallel than joint-controlled.

That last point explains a lot about Bitterroot climbing: **why so many
faces are described as "smooth," why sloper-and-pocket face climbing is
so common in a supposedly granite range, and why the best splitters are
in specific canyons rather than everywhere.**

#### 2.4.1 Kootenai Canyon (Stevensville)

**The unit.** Range-front rock of the Bitterroot lobe, **south of Bass
Creek**, hence within the belt where USGS mapping gives the mylonite a
**granitic protolith**. `[Specific mapped unit — unverified; the
inference rests on the crag's position on the range front south of Bass
Creek and on the range-front mylonite mapping.]`

**Climbing consequence.** The climbers' description is unusually
diagnostic: "**steep vertical walls of mostly smooth granite that
doesn't crumble like limestone**," with "**smooth granite walls
[providing] durable holds and pitches 60 feet or longer**." Smooth,
vertical, continuous, durable — that is a foliated, well-annealed
crystalline rock, and it is why the canyon supports **100+ routes across
several buttresses (1st Buttress alone has 20+, 5.6–5.13a)** with both
trad and sport in the mix. The best walls are 15 minutes from the
trailhead because the **canyon mouth is the range front**, where the
mylonite zone and the Miocene fault put the steepest rock at the lowest
elevation.

Hazards are the crystalline-rock standards — **foliation-parallel flakes,
lichen on north-facing walls, and a fast, cold creek at the base** —
plus the access situation the census already records (private/USFS land
at the canyon mouth, a formal permit system after landowner disputes).
Note that the access problem is itself geologic in origin: the range
front is a **straight fault line**, the alluvial fans at the canyon
mouths are the only farmable ground, so private land occupies exactly
the strip a climber must cross.

#### 2.4.2 Blodgett Canyon (Hamilton)

**The unit.** Explicit and well sourced: "**the glacial valley is carved
into granitic rocks of the Bitterroot Lobe of the Idaho Batholith, a
Late Cretaceous to Paleocene intrusive body emplaced by subduction
beneath the western margin of North America.**"

**Setting.** The showpiece glacial trough of the range: "when you walk
into Blodgett Canyon, you're walking up a glacial valley with **vertical
2,000-foot walls on both sides**," with moraines, hanging valleys,
cirques and "steeply carved granite mountains, cliffs and cirques." The
trailhead is at ~4,000 ft just west of Hamilton.

**Climbing consequence.** This is why Blodgett is "the Yosemite of
Montana," and the comparison is geologically legitimate rather than
merely promotional: **a granitic pluton, glacially quarried into a
U-shaped trough, with the walls plucked back to fresh rock along
sheeting joints.** The consequences:

- **Big, clean, continuous faces** — hence dozens of long routes,
  **5.6–5.11 and mostly multipitch**, and the need for **two-rope
  rappels** (the census's detail, and a direct measure of wall height).
- **Exfoliation/sheeting joints parallel to the wall** are the dominant
  structure: they make the flakes, the left-facing and right-facing
  corners, and the roofs where a sheet terminates.
- **Glacial polish** low on the walls, where ice was in contact — very
  low friction, and a genuine hazard on approach slabs.
- **Rockfall from the sheeting joints.** A glacially unloaded wall is
  still relaxing; slabs pop off along sheeting joints, and the talus
  cones at the base of every Bitterroot canyon are the evidence.
- **Aspect matters more than in a cragging area.** North-facing trough
  walls stay wet, mossy and cold well into summer; south-facing walls
  are the ones that climb.

#### 2.4.3 Lost Horse Canyon (Darby)

**The unit.** **Granite of the Bitterroot lobe** — "the rock here is
granite and solid by nature," in a canyon of "rugged, glacially carved
crags and granite walls," with "giant crags [shooting] up from the crisp
clean waters of Lost Horse Creek while the canyon floor is littered with
house-size boulders."

**Climbing consequence.** Lost Horse is the Bitterroot canyon where the
rock behaves most like classic granite rather than gneiss, and the
climbing record reflects it: **"some of Montana's best cracks,"** a
600-ft splitter-crack crag, multipitch (the *Mountaineers Route*), and
extensive bouldering (*Bloody Fingers Boulder* is a name that tells you
the crack quality). The two features are one feature:

- **Splitters** come from a **widely spaced, steeply dipping joint set
  in isotropic granite** — no foliation to divert the fracture, so the
  crack runs straight for a rope length.
- **House-size boulders on the canyon floor** are the same joint set
  read at the other end of its life: joint-bounded blocks calved off the
  walls by glacial plucking and postglacial rockfall, delivered to the
  valley floor. **A boulder field beneath a glacial trough wall is
  rockfall debris**, which is worth remembering both when you are on top
  of one and when you are underneath the wall that supplied it.
- Bouldering quality here is a function of **freshness**: blocks that
  fell recently have sharp crystals and no rind; blocks that have sat
  since deglaciation are grussy and lichen-covered. Locals' "best
  all-around area in the state" reputation is well founded.

#### 2.4.4 Mill Creek — three corrections at once

**The gazetteer and census both list this as "Mill Creek (Lolo)," near
Lolo, with rock type "not confirmed." All three parts need fixing.**

1. **It is not near Lolo.** "The **Mill Creek drainage sits north of
   Hamilton, MT** in the Bitterroot Valley" — that is roughly 40 miles
   south of Missoula, near Woodside/Corvallis, not the town of Lolo ten
   miles south of Missoula. `[The "(Lolo)" tag is most likely a
   carry-over from the Lolo National Forest name or from a Mountain
   Project region label — inferred, unverified.]`
2. **The rock is known.** It is **Bitterroot-lobe granitic rock**, and
   the best climber-facing description in the sources applies squarely
   here: **1,000-ft walls of granitic gneiss**, greyish, "with plenty of
   **pockets and slopers**." Routes are "**10–12 pitches long and mostly
   suited to the 5.10–5.12 climber**, with most climbs a mix of sport
   and trad," including big-wall aid lines and the 8-pitch 5.13a *Super
   Ultra Mega*. That is not the "small–moderate, TR to multipitch trad"
   area the census describes.
3. **The ticks have a name and an access history.** The census's
   "notoriously tick-heavy" is almost certainly an echo of **the Tick
   Farm**, a developed sport area of roughly **50 bolted and trad
   routes** less than a mile from the trailhead in lower Mill Creek. The
   Bitterroot National Forest supervisor placed a **bolting moratorium
   on the Tick Farm in 2016**; around 2020 it was expanded to a
   **canyon-wide ban on all new routes in Mill Creek**, which the Access
   Fund and the Western Montana Climbers' Coalition contested; the
   canyon-wide ban was subsequently **lifted and refined back to the
   Tick Farm alone**, with guidance that new routes be widely dispersed
   and **fixed anchors camouflaged and used only as a last resort where
   removable protection is unavailable**.

**Climbing consequence.** Big, foliated, pocketed granitic gneiss walls
with a low-elevation south-facing aspect (the "good winter sun exposure"
the census records) — a genuinely year-round venue by Montana standards.
The fixed-anchor policy is the practical consequence to print: **this is
a canyon where the rock will take a bolt but the land manager may not
let you place one.**

#### 2.4.5 Bass Creek (Florence)

Not currently in the gazetteer; it should be. **The Bass Creek Crags are
"a group of mostly flat-topped granite spires that sit at the easternmost
edge of the ridge that separates Bass Creek and Kootenai Creek," and
"the spires are 200 to 350 feet of exposed granite."** The Bass Creek
trail follows a **U-shaped glacial valley** to Bass Lake eight miles in,
from a trailhead just west of Florence, on Bitterroot National Forest
land.

**Geologic significance beyond the climbing:** Bass Creek is the
**mapped boundary** in the USGS description of the range front —
mylonite with a **granitic protolith south of Bass Creek**, implying a
different (metamorphic/Belt-derived) protolith to the north. A climber
working north to south along the Bitterroot front is crossing a real
lithologic transition at this canyon, and it is the reason the crags
change character between the Lolo end and the Darby end.

**Climbing consequence.** Flat-topped 200–350 ft spires on a ridge crest
are **joint-bounded remnants left standing between two glacial troughs**
— an arête sculpted from both sides. Expect walk-off or short-rappel
tops, exposed positions, gear-protected crack lines, and the wind that
comes with a ridge-crest crag.

#### 2.4.6 Pattee Canyon / Rattlesnake (Missoula bouldering)

Still **`[unverified]` as a documented climbing area** — the census's
judgment stands and nothing in this pass changed it. The geology,
however, is knowable and worth recording so a future pass knows what it
would be looking at.

`[Inferred from regional geology — unverified]` **Missoula's hills are
built of Belt Supergroup rock — Missoula Group formations (Mount
Shields, Bonner Quartzite, McNamara, Garnet Range) above the Piegan
Group carbonates.** The inference rests on the state geologic-unit
database listing Missoula County bedrock as Missoula Group, Piegan
Group, Ravalli Group and Lower Belt formations, and on the Missoula
Group being defined as everything in the Belt above the Helena/Siyeh
carbonate. **Mount Jumbo (4,768 ft, in the Rattlesnake Mountains) and
Mount Sentinel (5,158 ft)** both carry the **Glacial Lake Missoula
shorelines** on their faces.

If bouldering exists at Pattee or in the Rattlesnake, the likely medium
is **Belt quartzite or siltite–argillite**. Belt quartzite (Bonner, or
the quartzite members of Mount Shields) makes hard, sharp, edgy
bouldering — the same rock family as Stone Hill, §2.8. Belt argillite
makes bad bouldering: it splits along bedding and sheds plates. **Which
of the two a given hillside exposes is the whole question**, and it
cannot be answered from here.

### 2.5 Billings — the Rimrocks

**The unit.** **Eagle Sandstone, Upper Cretaceous (Campanian).** The
gazetteer already names it, and the naming is correct — a rarity worth
noting. The Eagle at Billings is "a light to brownish-gray to pale
yellow-orange, fine-grained sandstone," **deposited 70–80 million years
ago as shoreline and offshore bars of the great [Western Interior] sea**,
and "the Cretaceous Eagle Sandstone (Campanian) exposed in the rimrocks
at Billings, Montana, contains **at least three members**." The basal
member is the **Virgelle**, "deposited along an eastwardly prograding
coastal-interdeltaic mainland shoreline," generally **100–125 ft thick**
and locally over 200 ft. The Eagle at the Rimrocks has been interpreted
variously as a **clastic shallow sea, a barrier island, a shelf sand
lens, and tidal point-bar deposits** — that spread of interpretations is
itself the key to the climbing, because it means the rock changes
character laterally over short distances.

The formation "contains areas of **crossbedding** and local shale
members," and — importantly — "**large (up to 15 feet in diameter) sandy
calcareous concretions**."

**Setting.** A retreating-shoreline sand body on the western margin of
the Western Interior Seaway, now flat-lying and forming the caprock
escarpment that rings the city. There is no structure here to speak of:
the Rimrocks are an **erosional escarpment on nearly horizontal beds**,
which is why they form a continuous rim rather than a mountain.

**Climbing consequence.** The Rimrocks are Montana's only significant
sandstone climbing, and every rule of sandstone applies, hard:

- **Bedding and crossbedding control everything.** Individual beds vary
  from well-cemented to friable within a single route. Crossbed sets
  give the diagonal breaks and the sloping features.
- **Holds are huecos, pockets, sloping shelves and crossbed edges,**
  not crystals or crimps — with the **calcareous concretions** producing
  the genuinely bizarre features: a fifteen-foot cannonball of harder,
  calcite-cemented sandstone weathering proud of the softer rock around
  it, giving jugs, undercuts and cave-like scoops.
- **Cementation is the whole quality story.** The census's "rock is
  generally soft/poor except where established and cleaned" is exactly
  what a poorly-to-variably cemented shoreline sandstone gives: the
  routes that exist are on the cemented lenses; everything between them
  is sand held together by hope.
- **Wet rock is the cardinal rule.** Wet western sandstone loses a large
  fraction of its strength — holds snap and protection pulls. Give the
  Rimrocks **24 hours after light rain and 48–72 hours after a real
  storm**, and never climb rock that is still dark with water. This
  applies to bolts too: a bolt in wet, soft sandstone can pull.
- **Bolts** in Eagle Sandstone should be **glue-in**, long, and in the
  hardest bed available. Expansion bolts split soft sandstone.
- **Aspect and season.** The census's "brutally hot in summer" is a
  geologic fact as much as a climatic one: a pale sandstone rim facing
  an urban heat island, with no shade and no canyon to pool cold air.
  Spring and autumn.
- **Top-out and rim hazard.** A flat-lying caprock escarpment sheds
  **joint-bounded blocks** from its rim. The talus below the Rimrocks is
  fresh. Helmets, and stay off the rim above other parties.

### 2.6 Red Lodge, East Rosebud and the Beartooths

The Beartooth front is the one place in Montana where a climber can
touch some of the oldest rock on the continent and, twenty minutes
away, a bolted limestone sport crag.

#### 2.6.1 The range

**The unit.** **Archean crystalline basement of the Wyoming Province.**
"The most abundant rocks in the Beartooths — **gneiss, amphibolites and
granites**, as well as the Stillwater Complex — are **2.9–2.7 billion
years old**," and "the Beartooth basement granite has been dated to
around **2.8 billion years**." The eastern Beartooths hold **Archean
metasupracrustal rocks — mafic amphibolite, quartzite, tonalitic gneiss,
granitic gneiss, ironstone and pelites** — with crystallisation ages of
**3.5–3.0 Ga** and detrital zircons to **4.0 Ga**.

**Structure.** "The Beartooth Mountains are a **block of largely Archean
bedrock uplifted along high-angle reverse faults of Laramide age**" —
a single coherent basement block shoved up and northeast over the
Bighorn Basin margin. That is why the range is a **plateau** rather than
a set of ridges: the block went up more or less intact, preserving an
old erosion surface on top.

**Glaciation.** The Beartooth Plateau "has been extensively sculpted by
glacial processes during the Pleistocene, including classic **'biscuit-
board topography'** with plateau remnants partially dissected by
cirques." Biscuit-board is the perfect image for what a climber finds:
**a flat upland with bites taken out of its edges**, and every bite is a
cirque with a headwall.

#### 2.6.2 Granite Peak

**Granite Peak (12,807 ft)** is Montana's highpoint, in the
Absaroka-Beartooth Wilderness, and it is Archean basement — the name is
approximately honest, though "granite, granitic gneiss and amphibolite"
would be more so. It is "often considered the second most difficult
state highpoint to climb after Denali … due to technical climbing, poor
weather, and route finding."

**Climbing consequence.** Archean crystalline rock in a glaciated alpine
setting is generally excellent, but three things make Granite Peak
serious and they are all geologic:

1. **Compositional banding.** Basement of this age is a mixture —
   amphibolite bands, pelitic schist screens and quartzite lenses inside
   the granitic gneiss. The mafic and pelitic bands weather faster and
   produce the loose, blocky bands that break up otherwise good rock.
2. **Frost shattering at 12,000 ft.** Above treeline, freeze–thaw works
   every joint every day of the season. Summit ridges are rubble by
   process, not by accident.
3. **Route-finding on a biscuit-board plateau.** Cirque headwalls all
   look alike, and the plateau top gives no landmarks.

Class 3–4 for the standard route, with harder technical variations —
but the standard route's difficulty is exposure, weather and terrain,
not moves.

#### 2.6.3 East Rosebud

**The unit.** Archean basement of the Beartooth block; climbing sources
describe "**towering granite walls on both sides**" as the canyon
narrows above Elk Lake, and name the **East Rosebud Towers ("the Four
Sisters")** as "**old granite or gneiss, with crack and featured face
climbing**." **The Doublets** are "two massive alpine big walls situated
up-canyon from East Rosebud Lake (east side)," **west-facing with
afternoon sun**, holding hard free climbing plus "a few moderate and aid
lines."

**Setting.** A **glacial trough** cut into the Beartooth block — the same
architecture as Blodgett, on older and more heterogeneous rock. East
Rosebud Creek is a designated Wild & Scenic river.

**Climbing consequence.** The honest local summary is the useful one:
"**a mix of crumbly rock to beautiful granite can be found within these
mountains.**" That variability is the compositional banding of §2.6.2
expressed at wall scale — a homogeneous granitic gneiss panel gives a
1,000-ft clean wall; an amphibolite or schist band across the same wall
gives a loose ledge system. Practical consequences:

- **Some of the longest rock routes in Montana** (the census's "up to
  20-pitch outings"), because a glacial trough in coherent basement
  gives continuous vertical relief.
- **West-facing walls (the Doublets) climb in the afternoon**, which in
  a range with daily convective storms is a real planning constraint.
- **Beta is scarce** — "not the place to find a pitch-by-pitch breakdown
  by gear, grade and photos." Treat published grades as approximate.
- **Wilderness rules** and long approaches; the Absaroka-Beartooth
  Wilderness restricts mechanised drilling, so expect ground-up,
  gear-protected ethics on the alpine walls and bolts only on the
  single-pitch crags lower down.

#### 2.6.4 Red Lodge area crags — a gazetteer improvement

**The gazetteer records Red Lodge's rock as "Mixed." That is right, and
it can be made specific: Red Lodge has two distinct rock bands, and
climbers use both.**

1. **The Palisades limestone band.** "The **Palisades** formations —
   **large limestone slabs tilted upright along a fault line** — surround
   the foothills of the Beartooth Mountains," and the crags **Meeteetse,
   The Ovens, The Palace, The Outer Banks, Nippinino and Red Lodge
   Creek** are "**of the same limestone band**," giving climbing "on
   mostly **steep sharp blue rock**," with "**all routes bolted with
   sport anchors**." `[Formation not named in sources — inferred to be
   Madison Group; the inference rests on the Madison being the
   cliff-forming carbonate of the Beartooth front and on "blue" being
   the standard climber's description of weathered Madison.
   Unverified.]`
2. **The Precambrian crystalline band.** "**Precambrian granite and
   gneiss, some of the oldest exposed rock on Earth (~2.8–3.5 billion
   years old)**" — the crags **Confluence Crag, Headstrong Buttress,
   Footloose, Ying Yang Walls and Squirrel Wall** "make up the primary
   granite climbing," offering "a mix of crack and face climbing,
   requiring draws and occasional gear."

**Why the two are next to each other.** The Beartooth uplift drove
Archean basement up along **high-angle reverse faults**, and the
Paleozoic cover that once lay on top of it was dragged up and **tilted
to vertical** against the fault. Standing at Red Lodge you are standing
on the hinge: Archean rock inside the range front, Paleozoic limestone
turned on its edge along it. The Palisades' upended slabs *are* the
Laramide fault, made visible.

**Climbing consequence.** Two entirely different climbing games:

- **The limestone band** — steep, sharp, blue, fully bolted; classic
  Madison-type sport climbing. Because the beds are **tilted to
  vertical**, the *bedding planes* now form the *faces* and the
  *joints* form the *cracks*, an inversion of the usual limestone
  geometry, which is why these crags read as slabs and walls rather
  than as bedded ledges.
- **The crystalline band** — crack and face on Archean gneiss and
  granite; mixed protection, coarse and high-friction, with the
  foliation-flake caution of §2.1.1.
- **Ice.** Rock Creek Falls and Silver Run Falls freeze because a
  glaciated crystalline canyon delivers meltwater over resistant
  steps — the same hanging-valley and knickpoint mechanism as anywhere,
  but without Hyalite's permeability trick, so the ice here is fed by
  streams rather than by wall seeps and is correspondingly more
  variable year to year.

### 2.7 Livingston, Paradise Valley and the Crazy Mountains

Paradise Valley is a **structural corridor** between two completely
different geologies: the **Absaroka Range** on the east, "eroded
remnants of one of the largest eruption sequences in North American
history, active 53 to 43 million years ago," and the **Archean-cored
Beartooth/Gallatin blocks** west and south. Livingston itself sits on
the **Livingston Group**, "a thick Upper Cretaceous sequence of rocks
consisting chiefly of volcanic debris." South of town "the Yellowstone
River slices through a bed of **Madison limestone**" — that cut is
Allenspur.

#### 2.7.1 Allenspur

**The unit — and a correction.** The gazetteer says "Limestone." The
climbers' own description is more specific and more interesting: **"the
rock varies from excellent black dolomite to the lighter variety which
still can be very good."** Black dolomite is not a description of the
Madison.

`[Inferred — unverified, two live candidates]` **Either (a) the Devonian
Jefferson Formation / Jefferson Dolomite, which is characteristically
dark, fetid, sugary-textured dolomite and a cliff-former throughout
southwestern Montana, or (b) dolomitised intervals within the
Mississippian Madison Group, which the regional description of the
Yellowstone's cut at Paradise Valley names directly.** The inference for
(a) rests on the reported *black* dolomite and on the Jefferson sitting
immediately beneath the Madison in the section that the river cuts
here; the inference for (b) rests on the published statement that the
river slices Madison limestone in this reach. **A single look at the
Livingston-area geologic mapping — USGS PP 526-B, *Stratigraphy of
Madison Group Near Livingston Montana*, is the obvious document —
settles it.** The guidebook should say **"limestone and dolomite"** and
name no formation.

**Setting.** A **sunny, southwest-facing** wall on the tilted Paleozoic
panel that the Yellowstone River has cut through at the Allenspur gap,
five miles south of Livingston on US 89. The gap exists because a river
crossing a resistant carbonate ridge produces a **water gap**, and the
gap's walls are the climbing.

**Climbing consequence.** The dolomite/limestone distinction is a
climbing distinction, and this is the place in the book to explain it:

- **Dolomite** is a magnesium carbonate. Dolomitisation replaces calcite
  crystal-for-crystal, which **destroys primary fabric** (fossils,
  bedding detail) and produces a **sugary, crystalline, harder, more
  homogeneous** rock. It is more resistant to dissolution than
  limestone, so it forms **fewer big pockets and more small, sharp,
  crystalline edges**, and it **holds bolts superbly**. "Excellent black
  dolomite" is a climber correctly noticing that the dark bands take
  gear and stay solid.
- **Limestone** in the same crag dissolves faster, gives the pockets and
  the tufa streaks, and is the "lighter variety which still can be very
  good."
- **Aspect drives the season.** Southwest-facing, so "climbing is
  possible year-round here but probably best in the spring and fall" —
  the crag bakes in summer and thaws in winter. This is the standard
  Montana pattern for a sun-facing carbonate crag at valley elevation.
- **Grades cluster at 5.9–5.11** with the concentration the census
  records at 5.8–5.10, "with some newer routes currently being
  established on the larger part of the Main Crag."
- **Access is the real hazard.** "**Allen Spur access is fragile and
  much effort has gone into keeping the climbing open**"; **most of the
  climbs are on BLM land, but the terrain between the crags and the road
  is private property**, and the practice is to **park at Carters Bridge
  and follow designated access routes**. This is more specific than what
  the gazetteer currently carries and should replace the vaguer "verify
  current access."

#### 2.7.2 Mill Creek (Paradise Valley) — resolving a gazetteer `[unverified]`

**The gazetteer and census list this area as entirely unconfirmed —
rock, style, grades, all "Not confirmed." It can now be filled in.**

**What it is.** Mill Creek is "located near Livingston and hosts **many
single-pitch trad and sport lines on roadside granite** in a beautiful
alpine valley," **"further up in the Absaroka/Beartooth mountains,"**
with "a more traditional summer season (**late May through October**)."
Directions: from Livingston or Bozeman take US 89 toward Gardiner for
**~17 miles**, turn left on **Mill Creek Road**, and continue **14.3
miles** until "obvious **large granite formations** appear on the left
side of the road." **Approaches are short — some less than ten seconds
from the car.** The valley **burned in 2007**, "leading to a neat new
life feeling in the valley," and mountain weather here can differ
sharply from Bozeman's.

**The unit.** `[Inferred from regional geology — unverified, but well
supported]` **Archean crystalline basement — gneiss, schist, quartzite
and amphibolite of the Wyoming Province — rather than a true granite.**
The inference rests on two published facts: "**the Mill Creek area
contains outcrops of Archean crystalline rocks (gneisses, schists, some
quartzites and amphibolites) that are overlain by the Eocene Absaroka
Volcanics**," and the **Mill Creek–Stillwater fault zone**, a
Precambrian structural discontinuity later reactivated by Laramide
faulting, "extends 65 miles from the **confluence of Mill Creek and the
Yellowstone River near Pray, Montana** in the northwest to the Clarks
Fork Canyon area in the southeast." **The climbing is therefore on the
Archean basement window in lower Mill Creek, beneath the Absaroka
volcanic cover — and the crags sit essentially on a 2.7-billion-year-old
fault.**

**Climbing consequence.** Roadside Archean gneiss and granitoid, cleaned
by a 2007 fire and by glacial and fluvial scour, on a valley floor at
moderate elevation:

- Short, single-pitch, mixed trad and sport on **crystal face and
  joint-controlled cracks**, with the usual foliation-flake caution.
- **A recently burned crag is a shedding crag.** Fire drives a steep
  thermal gradient into rock and pops surface-parallel spalls; granitic
  rock is physically vulnerable because quartz and feldspar expand at
  very different rates. Nearly two decades on, most of the loose stuff
  will have come down, but burned-slope deadfall and a destabilised
  approach hillside remain real.
- **The fire is also why the crags are "obvious" from the road.**
- **A crag on a reactivated Archean fault zone** can be expected to have
  a shattered, mylonitised or cataclastic band somewhere in it. Expect
  quality to change abruptly across the valley.

**Also note:** the corridor pages and gazetteer must keep this Mill
Creek distinct from the Bitterroot Mill Creek (§2.4.4) — two areas, two
ranges, two rock types (Archean basement here, Idaho-batholith granitic
gneiss there), and Mountain Project files them in two different regions.

#### 2.7.3 Crazy Mountains — resolving the "no roped climbing" question

**The census flags as `[unverified]` its own claim that the Crazies have
no developed roped climbing. The geology explains why the claim is
almost certainly true, and that explanation is worth printing.**

**The units.** The Crazy Mountains are **mid-Eocene alkalic stocks,
laccoliths, sills and dikes emplaced into Cretaceous and Paleocene
sedimentary strata** — specifically, intrusions "penetrated the **Fort
Union Formation** over a 2–4 million year period near the end of the
early Eocene (**48–52 Ma**)," coeval with the **Challis magmatic
flare-up**. The southern Crazies hold **subalkaline stocks, sills and
radiating dikes** — the **Big Timber stock** and the **Loco Mountain
stock** — while the northern part holds **super-alkaline laccoliths,
sills, dikes and three volcanic necks** plus a small diorite stock. The
Big Timber intrusion "consists of **coarse-grained diorite and gabbro**
that was intruded by a fine-grained **quartz monzodiorite**."
Compositions are "sodium-rich, alkaline mafic syenites and trachytes and
subalkaline… potassium-rich diorites and granodiorites."

**The mountain-building story.** The Crazies are not an uplift. They are
a **Laramide basin** — the asymmetric **Crazy Mountains Basin**, formed
against the ancestral Bridger arch — that was **filled with soft
Paleocene sediment and then stiffened by a swarm of Eocene intrusions**.
Erosion has since stripped the soft Fort Union shale and left the
intrusions standing as the range. That is why the Crazies are so
extraordinarily prominent (Crazy Peak has the greatest topographic
prominence of any Montana peak) and so isolated: **they are the hard
bits left behind.**

**Climbing consequence, and why there are no crags.** Three reasons,
all geologic:

1. **The competent rock is gabbro and diorite, and it is shattered.**
   "**The bedrock of the Crazies is gabbro and diorite, with shattered
   sharp rock dominating the steep-sided, glaciated peaks.**" Mafic
   plutonic rock cooled as a shallow intrusion inside cold wet
   sedimentary country rock develops **dense, closely spaced cooling and
   contraction joints**. Close joint spacing plus alpine freeze–thaw
   equals rubble, not routes. Route reports match: the Crazy Couloir has
   "**tons of loose rock to negotiate**," and the North Rib is "**loose,
   wet and covered with moss**," while the standard **West Ridge** is
   "mostly good quality."
2. **The host rock is Fort Union shale**, which supports nothing. Any
   wall that is not intrusive rock is mudstone.
3. **Access.** The Crazies are famous for a checkerboard of private and
   public land and long-running public-access litigation, which
   suppresses development independently of the rock.

**Conclusion for the gazetteer:** the entry can stand as written —
alpine scrambling terrain, Class 3–4 West Ridge, no developed roped
climbing — but the reason should be given. **"Metamorphic/igneous alpine
rock" in the Rock column should become "Eocene gabbro, diorite and
alkalic intrusive rock; shattered."**

### 2.8 Whitefish / Kalispell / Lake Koocanusa — Stone Hill

**The unit.** **Belt Supergroup quartzite**, Mesoproterozoic. Stone Hill
sits about **15 miles from Eureka** above **Lake Koocanusa** on the
Kootenai River, in the heart of the Belt basin's northwestern
exposures. The regional Belt section here is "a thick sequence of
**argillite, siltite, quartzite and impure carbonate rocks up to 9,000 m
thick**," and the local Missoula Group formations in the
Libby–Eureka–Koocanusa country are the **Bonner, McNamara, Libby,
Roosville and Phillips Formations**. Two are especially relevant:

- **Bonner Quartzite** — "feldspathic quartzite with subordinate siltite
  and argillite."
- **Phillips Formation** — "the overlying **subaerial quartzites of the
  Phillips Formation** form a northward- and westward-thinning marker
  unit," sitting above the **Roosville Formation**. The Roosville is
  Mesoproterozoic and part of the Missoula Group.
- **Libby Formation** — for contrast, "light to dark gray and greenish
  gray **siltite and argillite** with subordinate quartzite in
  **mud-cracked couplets**," up to 2,300 m thick. This is the
  *unclimbable* end of the same section.

`[Inferred from regional geology — unverified]` **Stone Hill's cliffs
are cut in a quartzite-dominated formation of the Missoula Group, with
the Phillips Formation quartzite (or the equivalent quartzite members of
the Bonner) the best candidates.** The inference rests on: (a) the
climbers' description — "an **extremely hard quartzite derivative**" —
matching a quartzite formation rather than an argillite one; (b) the
Phillips quartzites being the named regional quartzite marker in exactly
this corner of the Belt basin; and (c) the geographic proximity of the
type area at Roosville, on the Canadian border directly north of Eureka.
**The Rexford / Eureka MBMG quadrangle mapping would settle it in one
look. Do not print a formation name until then.**

**Why Stone Hill's Belt rock is *good* when Glacier's is *bad*.** This
is the central puzzle of Montana climbing geology, and the answer is
**which formation, not which supergroup**. See §3.2 for the full
argument. The short version: **quartzite is a cemented, recrystallised
sand with strength in three dimensions; argillite is a lithified mud
with strength in two.** Stone Hill is on the sand. Glacier is on the
mud.

**Setting.** The Kootenai valley here was overrun by the **Cordilleran
ice sheet**, which stripped and polished the Belt bedrock. The lake is
the Libby Dam reservoir. Aspect is **south-facing** — the census and
gazetteer both have this right — which makes Stone Hill a spring and
autumn venue with a long shoulder season.

**Climbing consequence.** Belt quartzite is one of the most distinctive
climbing media in the Rockies and the sources describe it precisely:
"**the rock is an extremely hard quartzite derivative, and is
characterised by painfully sharp edges, vanishing cracks, steep slabs
and small horizontal roofs.**" As of 2024, **more than 650 routes and
100 established boulder problems**, **5.7–5.13d** (an update the
gazetteer's "~500" should take), with over half the routes protected by
**a mix of bolts and traditional gear** because "the cracks tend to be
very protectable." Steve Stahl alone established **over 260 lines in
2000–2003**.

Reading that back as geology:

- **"Painfully sharp edges."** Quartzite is quartz sand welded by silica
  cement; it does not round off when it weathers, it **fractures
  conchoidally and keeps a knife edge**. Bring tape. Skin, not strength,
  is the limiting factor on a Stone Hill day.
- **"Vanishing cracks."** Bedding in a Belt quartzite is
  centimetre-to-decimetre scale, and the joints that cut it are
  discontinuous, so a crack opens, runs, and dies — which is exactly why
  half the routes are mixed bolt-and-gear rather than pure trad.
- **"Steep slabs."** The dominant structure is the **bedding surface**,
  and where a bedding plane is exposed you get a large, planar, slightly
  overlapping face — a slab with edges rather than a friction slab.
  Friction on quartzite is high but the rock is so hard that smearing
  gives less than on granite; **edging is the technique.**
- **"Small horizontal roofs."** The steps between bedding planes.
- **Bolts hold outstandingly** in quartzite — it is among the strongest
  rock types a bolt can go into — which is why a single developer could
  put up 260 lines and why they are still there.
- **Hazards:** the sharpness itself (rope damage over edges is a real
  risk on this rock — pad your anchors), lichen on north-facing panels,
  loose plates where an argillite or siltite interbed separates two
  quartzite beds, and **ticks in spring**, which multiple climber
  accounts single out.

### 2.9 Glacier National Park

**The units.** Glacier is the finest exposure of the **Belt Supergroup**
anywhere, and the park's stratigraphy is worth giving in full because
every route and every scramble in the park is on one of these:

| Formation | Thickness | Lithology and setting |
|---|---|---|
| **Altyn Formation** | ~780–840 ft | ~1.35–1.45 Ga **limestone and dolomite**, primarily **oolitic dolostone** with occasional quartz sand — "indicators of rough weather on the ancient coastline of the Belt Sea." Eastern park. |
| **Appekunny Formation** | ~700 m | **green argillite**, fine-grained terrigenous mud, offshore/deltaic. **The green is chlorite** — "one of the few signatures that the Belt rocks at Glacier experienced burial metamorphism." |
| **Grinnell Formation** | 605 m | **red argillite**, terrigenous mud deposited largely on an **alluvial plain**. |
| **Empire Formation** | ~400–500 ft | transitional **argillite and siltite** between Grinnell below and Helena above. |
| **Helena (Siyeh) Formation** | 750 m–1 km | **bluish limestone weathering honey-coloured**; impure dolostone and dolomitic limestone, shallow subtidal to intertidal; hosts the **Conophyton Zone**, a 30 m band of extraordinarily well developed **stromatolites**. |
| **Purcell Sill** | — | a **diorite sill** intruded into the Helena **650 million years after** the sediment was laid down; makes the **conspicuous black stripe** across peak faces, with **contact-metamorphosed (bleached) rock above and below**, and epidote alteration. |
| **Snowslip Formation** | 385 m | slightly dolomitic fine terrigenous strata, intertidal; **includes 95 m of basalt lava flows — the Purcell Lava.** |
| **Shepard Formation** | 270 m | impure dolostone and argillite, subtidal to intertidal. |

**The structure.** The whole pile rode east on the **Lewis Thrust** —
"an enormous slab of Belt Supergroup rock slid **more than 50 miles
east** along a nearly horizontal fault plane," coming to rest on much
younger **Cretaceous** sedimentary rock, during the **Sevier orogeny,
~100–70 Ma**. Then Pleistocene ice carved the whole thrust sheet into
horns, arêtes, cirques and hanging valleys.

**The red and green.** The colours that make Glacier famous are an
**iron-oxidation record**: red where iron was oxidised in an
oxygen-rich, subaerial or shallow setting (the Grinnell's alluvial
plain), green where iron stayed reduced and chlorite formed in deeper,
oxygen-poor water (the Appekunny). Alternating red and green couplets in
the Snowslip "reflect variations in mineral composition and oxidation
conditions." This is a beautiful fact and it is also a **climbing**
fact: colour banding is bedding, and bedding is where Glacier's rock
comes apart.

**Climbing consequence — why the rock is famously bad.** The sources are
blunt and the gazetteer is right to repeat them, but the *mechanism*
should be printed alongside the warning:

- "**Most of the rock in Glacier National Park is brittle, so thin holds
  tend to break easily, and it is also generally very loose.**"
- "**Sometimes handholds are used that will sustain a downward pull, but
  come out easily if pulled horizontally.**" — This is the signature of
  a **bedded, thinly laminated rock**: a plate of argillite resting on
  its bedding plane is stable in compression and free in shear.
- "**Nearly all ledges are covered to some extent by loose talus or
  scree.**" — Because argillite weathers into plates, and plates
  accumulate on every bedding-controlled ledge. Everything above you is
  a projectile.
- **Protection barely works.** The best statement of the problem found
  in any source: "**a cam placed in a horizontal crack between two thin
  argillite tiers has an effective anchoring depth that approaches zero,
  with the failure cone bounded by the bedding plane, not the gear,
  causing both tiers to displace together — making your 'bombproof' cam
  now a loose block.**" That is the single most important sentence about
  climbing in Glacier, and it generalises: **in a thinly bedded rock,
  the strength of your placement is the strength of the bedding plane,
  not the strength of the rock.**
- **Consequently, the park invented its own grades.** "Because the rock
  in Glacier National Park is extremely poor by rock-climbing standards,
  classical rock-climbing ratings do not really apply in most places,"
  and **J. Gordon Edwards** and the **Glacier Mountaineering Society**
  developed park-specific systems in which "the rating … involves
  **relative danger as much as technical difficulty**." *A Climber's
  Guide to Glacier National Park* remains the reference.

**Where the rock is *better*, and it is worth saying:**

- **The Altyn Formation** (oolitic dolostone, eastern park) is a
  carbonate, not an argillite, and behaves accordingly — it is the rock
  of the cliff bands around Many Glacier and it is materially more
  competent.
- **The Helena/Siyeh limestone** is massive over hundreds of metres and
  makes the big clean walls (the Garden Wall, the great east faces).
- **The Purcell Sill diorite** is an igneous rock — hard, jointed,
  isotropic, and by far the most competent stripe on any Glacier face.
  Where a route crosses the sill, the rock changes completely for a
  pitch.
- **The Purcell Lava** basalt in the Snowslip is similarly competent
  where it is not vesicular.
- The **argillite formations — Appekunny, Grinnell, Empire, Shepard —
  are the bad ones**, and they are the majority.

**Access and ethics.** NPS-managed; **overnight backcountry climbing
requires permits**. There is effectively no bolting culture and should
not be one; the rock would not hold bolts reliably in the argillite
anyway. Treat Glacier as **alpine scrambling and mountaineering terrain
in a very old sedimentary pile**, not as a rock-climbing destination —
which is exactly what the gazetteer already says.

### 2.10 Little Belt Mountains, White Sulphur Springs and the Castles

#### 2.10.1 The Little Belts

**The units.** The Little Belts are a layer cake with an igneous
overprint, and all four layers matter: "the Little Belt Mountains
originated from **Precambrian sedimentary rocks of the Belt Formation**,
overlaid by **Paleozoic limestones, shales and quartzites**, with a
**core of Archean crystalline gneisses and schists**, all uplifted and
**intruded by post-Cretaceous igneous bodies such as laccoliths, stocks
and dikes** of syenite-porphyry and diorite." The local Belt section is
subdivided (ascending) **Neihart quartzite and sandstone, Chamberlain
shale, Newland limestone, Greyson shale, Spokane shale, Empire shale,
Helena limestone, Marsh shale** — "the best sections exposed in the Big
Belt and Little Belt Mountains."

Recent thermochronology ties **Late Cretaceous exhumation of the Little
Belt Mountains** to the development of the **Helena salient** — i.e. the
range came up as part of the Sevier thrust-belt bulge, before the Eocene
intrusions arrived.

**The Madison** is here too: "look for pale grey stone outcrops of
**Madison limestone at Sluice Boxes State Park and Monarch** along
Montana Highway 89," where **Belt Creek cuts a deep, steep-walled
canyon**. The limestone "formed about 350 million years ago when much of
Montana was submerged under a shallow sea." **Sluice Boxes State Park**
(Cascade County, established 1974) protects "the northernmost eight
miles of the **Belt Creek canyon**" as it "winds out of the Little Belt
Mountains," and is "highlighted by **large cliffs and ledges**" — "**a
beautiful canyon carved in limestone**," reached by a **primitive,
unmaintained trail** with **16 creek crossings** over ~7.5 miles and
explicit warnings about steep cliffs and cold, swift water. **No rock
climbing record surfaced for it in this pass** — but a limestone slot in
the Madison, eight miles long, on state park land, is the most obvious
undocumented prospect in central Montana and is worth a line in the book
as exactly that: a prospect, not an area. `[Unverified as a climbing
area.]`

#### 2.10.2 Memorial Falls — a flat correction

**Both the gazetteer and the census give Memorial Falls' rock as
"Limestone." It is not limestone. It is Precambrian quartzitic
sandstone, and it is the oldest formation in the Belt Supergroup.**

**The unit.** Memorial Falls is "a series of two waterfalls in Cascade
County, Montana, on Memorial Creek, on **US 89 between Neihart and
Showdown Ski Resort**." **"The waterfalls both sit on pieces of
Precambrian sandstone formed from the Neihart Formation over
1,450,000,000 years ago."** The **Neihart Formation** "is interpreted to
be a thick sheet sand at the base of the Belt Supergroup, making it the
**oldest part of the Belt**"; the sand was "largely fluvial (including
some probable braided-stream deposits on coastal plains)," with parts
formed in nearshore settings — beaches and offshore sand bars. **The
waterfall itself is young — "probably initiated near the end of the last
glacial period about 12,000 years ago."**

**Why the falls exist, and why they freeze.** A knickpoint at a
**resistance contrast**: Memorial Creek runs off the softer overlying
Belt shales onto — or over the edge of — the hard, well-cemented Neihart
quartzitic sandstone, and the harder rock holds the step. The falls are
only 12,000 years old because postglacial stream incision created the
step. Freezing is straightforward: a small, steady, shaded,
high-elevation creek at a vertical step, in a canyon that holds cold air
on the east side of the Continental Divide.

**Climbing consequence.**

- **For ice:** a quartzite-backed waterfall gives a **stable, competent
  backing wall** — good screw placements in the ice with no risk of the
  rock behind delaminating, unlike a limestone or argillite-backed flow.
  This is a good ice venue for that reason.
- **For rock:** the Neihart is a **quartzitic sandstone** — hard,
  well-cemented, sharp — and would climb like a slightly softer, more
  bedded Stone Hill. But the gazetteer's `[unverified as a developed

#### 2.10.3 The Castle Mountains (the White Sulphur Springs corridor area)

The corridor page `content/corridor/montana/white-sulphur-springs.md`
gets the essentials right and can now be given its geology.

**The units.** The Castle Mountains are "an island range east of White

"The **eastern side consists of dry, porous limestone hills**, and **the

spires on the western slopes**." The range hosted the **Castle Mountain
mining district** (lead, silver, copper), the classic signature of an
intrusion emplaced into carbonate country rock — replacement ore bodies
form where a hot pluton meets limestone.

`[Inferred from regional geology — unverified]` **The Castles are an
intrusive centre — stocks and laccoliths of Late Cretaceous to Eocene
age — punched into a Paleozoic carbonate section, with the intrusive
rock forming the western spires and the limestone the eastern hills.**
The inference rests on (a) the explicit description of igneous spires
west and limestone hills east; (b) the lead–silver–copper replacement
district, which requires an intrusion in carbonate; and (c) the regional
pattern of Eocene alkalic and subalkaline stocks and laccoliths across
central Montana (Crazies, Little Belts, Highwoods, Judiths), all part of
the same **Challis-age flare-up**. **The specific pluton, its
composition and its age are unverified.** The climbers' word "granite"
should be treated as the field term, not a mapped composition — in this
part of Montana the intrusive rock is as likely to be **syenite,
monzonite, diorite or quartz monzonite** as true granite.

**The three areas, with the rock now attached.**

- **Grasshopper (Grasshopper Rocks)** — "mostly comprised of **granite
  spires on the western portion** of the Castle Mountains. There are

  **expect most routes to be trad or mixed protection.**" So Grasshopper

- **Limestone Ridge** — the name is the rock; on the eastern,
  carbonate side of the range. No route detail surfaced.
- **Green Canyon** — "**one of the prettiest limestone climbing

"**The Big Empty** by Ron Brunckhorst" is the area guide, sold at **Base

**Climbing consequence.** Two rocks in one range, half an hour apart:

- **The intrusive spires (Grasshopper)** — 50-ft turrets are
  **joint-bounded remnants of a shallow intrusion**, exhumed by
  weathering of the surrounding sedimentary cover. Expect **crack and
  mixed-protection climbing on coarse, joint-defined faces**, walk-off
  or short-rappel summits, grussy tops, and the shelling/rind caution of
  §2.2. That "most routes are trad or mixed" is exactly what
  joint-bounded intrusive spires give.
- **The limestone (Green Canyon, Limestone Ridge)** — "dry, porous
  limestone hills," which is a description of **karst**: porous means
  dissolution has been at work, and dissolution means pockets. A moderate
  limestone canyon at 6,000–7,000 ft with 40 routes at 5.4–5.12b is a
  classic Montana carbonate sport venue. `[Formation unverified — likely

- **Season and elevation.** An island range at 6,000–8,589 ft, exposed

#### 2.10.4 Great Falls area — Wolf Butte

**The unit.** **Limestone.** Wolf Butte is "east of Great Falls on
Highway 87/200 toward **Geyser**, MT, accessed via **Lone Tree Road**,"
with "a **short approach into a shallow limestone canyon**" and problems
"from 8 ft up to 35 ft." Two sectors: **the Atrium** — the first rocks
visible from the parking lot — and **the Graveyard**, about 500 yards up
the canyon. Notable problems include *Just a Little Prick* (V3) and
*Power Plant* (V4).

**A second rock is reported:** "there is also **potential for granite
climbing (traditional and bouldering) higher up Wolf Butte.**"

`[Inferred from regional geology — unverified]` **Wolf Butte is most
likely a laccolith or small intrusive dome with a Paleozoic carbonate
carapace** — which would explain both the limestone canyon low down and
the "granite" higher up. The inference rests on the regional statement
that central Montana's "limestone buttes are **laccolith domes capped by
sedimentary rock**," and on the butte's isolated position on the plains
between the Little Belts and the Highwoods, a belt of Eocene alkalic
intrusive centres. Alternatively the butte is simply an erosional
outlier of Madison limestone and the "granite" is a misidentification.
**Both are live; neither is confirmed.**

**Climbing consequence.** The best climber's description of fresh
limestone in any source consulted for this file: "**the 'new' limestone
feel of this place is tough on the hands, like getting chewed by puppy
teeth, but it should buff out with more traffic and while it lasts it is
very grippy.**" That is precisely what an **undertravelled carbonate
surface** does — a microscopically rough, unpolished, slightly
case-hardened rind that gives enormous friction and destroys skin, and
that smooths as it is used. It is also the reason the local ethic of
leaving first ascents unnamed and open makes sense here: the rock is
still changing.

Hazards: a shallow canyon on the high plains means **sun, no water and
rattlesnakes**; and a young limestone area means **loose blocks in the
weathering rind** on the taller (35 ft) problems, which is highball
terrain.

### 3.2 The Belt Supergroup's peculiar climbing personality

Nothing else in the American West climbs like the Belt, and the reason
is that it is a **sedimentary pile that is older than most metamorphic
rock and yet was never properly metamorphosed.** Fifteen to twenty
kilometres of Mesoproterozoic mud, silt, sand and carbonate were buried,
compacted, cemented, and then — instead of being cooked into slate and
schist — were lifted and shoved sideways more or less intact. In Glacier
the only metamorphic signature anyone points to is **chlorite**, the
green mineral in the Appekunny, "one of the few signatures that the Belt
rocks at Glacier experienced burial metamorphism." Mudcracks, ripple
marks and stromatolites survive in rock 1.4 billion years old.

**What that means under your hands.** A 1.4-billion-year-old argillite
behaves like a mudstone because that is what it is. Age did not make it
strong; only cementation and recrystallisation could have done that, and
in the argillite formations neither went far enough. Hence the state's
two extreme reputations, which are the *same rock family at two ends of
a grain-size spectrum*:

**Why Stone Hill is good.**

- The medium is **quartzite** — quartz sand welded by silica cement.
  Quartz has a hardness of **6–7**; the cement is quartz too; and there
  is no weak mineral phase for a fracture to run along. **Strength is
  three-dimensional.**
- Bedding is present but the beds are **thick, competent and welded to
  each other**, so a bedding plane is a feature, not a failure surface —
  it gives you the horizontal roofs and the slab faces.
- The rock **fractures conchoidally and keeps a knife edge** rather than
  rounding off, hence "painfully sharp edges."
- **Bolts hold outstandingly**, which is why one developer could put in
  260 routes that are still there.
- **Glaciation** stripped the weathered rind: Cordilleran ice ran over
  the Kootenai valley, so what you climb is fresh rock.

**Why Glacier's Belt rock is bad.**

- The medium is **argillite and siltite** — lithified clay and silt.
  Hardness **2–3**. The rock has "obvious bedding planes with
  **anisotropy in mechanical properties**" and "**low deformational and
  strength parameters**." (A useful technical nuance: argillite is
  formally *not* fissile the way shale is — it does not split into paper
  — but it is thinly bedded and **anisotropic**, and in a mountain face
  that distinction is academic.)
- Deposition was in **mm-to-cm couplets** — one lamina of silt, one of
  clay, repeated for hundreds of metres. Every couplet boundary is a
  potential parting.
- The **Lewis Thrust** then moved the whole pile fifty miles. Transport
  on that scale fractures rock pervasively without healing it.
- **Freeze–thaw at 8,000–10,000 ft** works every one of those partings
  every year, so the rock arrives at the surface pre-broken. "Nearly all
  ledges are covered to some extent by loose talus or scree."
- Therefore **holds are tiles**: stable under a downward pull, free
  under an outward one.
- And therefore **protection is not protection**: "a cam placed in a
  horizontal crack between two thin argillite tiers has an effective
  anchoring depth that approaches zero, with the failure cone bounded by
  the bedding plane, not the gear."
- Which is why Glacier climbers **invented their own grades**, weighting
  danger as heavily as difficulty.

**The rule to carry into the field:** *in the Belt Supergroup, ask which
formation, never which supergroup.* Belt quartzite (Bonner, Phillips,
Neihart, the quartzite members of Mount Shields) is world-class. Belt
carbonate (Helena/Siyeh, Altyn, Newland) is competent and makes big
walls. Belt argillite and siltite (Appekunny, Grinnell, Empire, Shepard,
Libby, Snowslip away from its lavas) is scrambling terrain. The
formations are interleaved, so **a single Glacier face can cross all
three**, and the black stripe of the **Purcell Sill** across the middle
of it is the only truly solid pitch on the wall.

### 3.3 Geologic hazards and ethics in Montana

**Freeze–thaw is the master hazard.** Montana's climbing sits at high
latitude and, mostly, high elevation, and "the freeze/thaw process set
into motion by cold temperatures is one of the main contributors to
loosening rock"; water frozen in cracks overnight exerts immense
pressure and breaks rock down. Practical consequences:

- **Early-season rock is the most dangerous rock of the year**, at every
  Montana crag, in every rock type. Everything the winter loosened is
  still sitting there in June.
- **Test every hold in spring** — "check every hold with a little tap
  before trusting it."
- At the **subalpine limestone crags** (Wolverine Bowl, Ross Peak) and
  the **alpine crystalline walls** (Granite Peak, East Rosebud, the
  Crazies) the effect is at its worst. The Crazies' "shattered sharp
  rock" is freeze–thaw acting on closely jointed gabbro.

**Wildfire.** Montana's most common natural disaster, peaking
**July–September**. Fire damages rock directly: surface temperature
rises far faster than the interior, producing a steep thermal gradient
and **surface-parallel fractures**; granitic rock is especially
vulnerable because quartz and feldspar expand at very different rates.
Two Montana crags in this book have burned:

- **Mill Creek (Paradise Valley)** burned in **2007**; the valley is
  described as having "a neat new life feeling," and the granite
  formations are now conspicuous from the road because the timber is
  gone.
- **Mount Maurice**, above **Red Lodge**, burned in the **Robertson Draw
  Fire, June 2021** — human-caused, **29,885 acres**, contained in
  mid-September, and it threatened Red Lodge itself.

**Note a probable error in the existing pages:** both the gazetteer and
the census attach "Mount Maurice area burned in the 2021 wildfire" to
the **Bridger Range** row. **Mount Maurice is south of Red Lodge, in the
Beartooth front, ~180 miles from the Bridgers.** The wildfire note
belongs in the Red Lodge row.

After any fire: assume every hold, bolt and anchor is suspect; expect a
burned approach slope to be unstable and full of deadfall for years; and
expect rockfall frequency to rise for several seasons.

**Wet rock.** One rule, one place: **the Billings Rimrocks.** Eagle
Sandstone loses a large fraction of its strength when saturated, and
wet-sandstone damage is permanent. Give it 24 hours after light rain,
48–72 hours after a storm. **Montana's other rock types do not carry
this rule** — limestone and granite are climbable damp, if unpleasant —
and it is worth saying so explicitly, because a climber who generalises
in either direction gets it wrong.

**Seepage and karst.** Limestone drains *through* itself rather than off
itself, so **a Montana limestone crag can weep for days after the
hillside looks dry** — most acutely at the shaded, complex, cold-air
canyons: Avalanche Gulch, Refrigerator Canyon, Hellgate. In Hyalite the
seepage is the *point*: the permeability contrast between lahar and lava
that produces the springs is what produces the ice.

**Rockfall, by rock type.** Montana gives four distinct patterns and
they want four distinct habits:

1. **Bedded carbonate and argillite** (Bridgers, Big Belts, Glacier) —
   failure along **bedding planes**, delivering plates and tiles.
   Helmets; never climb directly under another party; distrust
   horizontal placements.
2. **Glacially unloaded crystalline walls** (Blodgett, Kootenai, East
   Rosebud, Lost Horse) — failure along **sheeting joints**, delivering
   slabs. The talus and the house-size boulders on the canyon floor are
   the historical record of it.
3. **Spheroidally weathered granite tors** (Spire Rock, Humbug, Sheep
   Mountain, Pipestone) — **stacked corestones and hollow shells**. Test
   summit blocks before you sling them.
4. **Flat-lying sandstone rims** (Billings) — **joint-bounded blocks**
   calving off the rim. Stay off the rim above parties.

**Bolting, by rock type.** A short field guide:

- **Excellent bolt rock:** Belt quartzite (Stone Hill), Madison
  limestone where it is sound, Jefferson dolomite, Archean gneiss away
  from mica bands, sound Boulder Batholith quartz monzonite, andesite.
- **Poor bolt rock:** the weathering rind or spheroidal shell on
  granite; case-hardened rind on limestone; **lahar/volcaniclastic
  matrix** in Hyalite; **Eagle Sandstone** (glue-ins only, long, in the
  most cemented bed); **Belt argillite** in Glacier (do not).
- **Where the rules forbid it regardless of the rock:** the **Humbug
  Spires WSA** and the **Absaroka-Beartooth Wilderness** constrain fixed
  anchors and mechanised drilling; the **Bitterroot National Forest**
  maintains a **bolting moratorium at the Tick Farm in Mill Creek**,
  with guidance elsewhere in the canyon that new routes be dispersed and
  **fixed anchors camouflaged and used only as a last resort**; **Glacier
  National Park** has no bolting culture and should not acquire one.

**Wildlife and season.** Three Montana-specific items that behave like
geology because they track rock type and aspect:

- **Ticks** cluster at **warm, low-elevation, south-facing crag bases**
  through spring — Stone Hill and the Bitterroot's Tick Farm are both
  named for it in the sources.
- **Prairie rattlesnakes**, Montana's only venomous snake, live at
  **lower elevations** — the Billings Rimrocks, Wolf Butte, the Big Hole
  canyon at Maiden Rock, the lower Big Belt gulches. Most bites happen
  when a snake is handled or touched accidentally by someone walking or
  **climbing**.
- **Raptor nesting.** Nationally, roughly **85–100 climbing areas close
  annually in late winter and spring**, about half of them for
  **peregrine falcons**, whose nesting season runs **February to
  August**, with closures commonly **1 February – 31 July**. Peregrines
  prefer tall, steep cliffs — which in Montana means **limestone**.
  Mount Helena's cliffs are explicitly noted as habitat for nesting
  birds. `[No Montana-specific closure list was found in this pass —
  unverified. Check the land manager before a spring trip to any
  limestone wall.]`

**Access, and why it is geologic.** Two of Montana's three long-running
access fights are consequences of landform:

- **The Bitterroot canyon mouths** (Kootenai, and the Mill Creek
  bolting dispute) — the range front is a **straight fault scarp**, and
  the alluvial fans at the canyon mouths are the only irrigable ground,
  so private land sits exactly where the trail must start.
- **Allenspur** — a **water gap** where the Yellowstone cuts a resistant
  carbonate ridge, so the crag is on BLM land but the approach crosses
  private valley-bottom ranchland. Park at Carters Bridge and use the
  designated route.

## Part 5 — What stayed unverified

- **No geologic map was read directly.** Every "this crag is on unit X"
  that is not a direct quotation is an inference, marked as such.
- **The Big Belt gulches' formation** — Avalanche, Hellgate and Beaver
  Creek are inferred to **Madison Group / Mission Canyon**, on the
  strength of the Gates of the Mountains mapping next door. The
  **Mesoproterozoic Newland Formation limestone** remains a live
  alternative and would change the age by 1.1 billion years. Needs an
  MBMG Townsend/York-area quadrangle.
- **Stone Hill's formation** — inferred to a Missoula Group quartzite
  (Phillips or Bonner-equivalent), not mapped. Needs the Rexford/Eureka
  quadrangle.
- **Maiden Rock's formation** — inferred to Madison Group. Needs the
  Divide-area mapping.
- **Allenspur's unit** — genuinely open between the **Devonian Jefferson
  Dolomite** and **dolomitised Madison**. Needs USGS PP 526-B or the
  Livingston-area map.
- **Mill Creek (Paradise Valley)'s unit** — inferred to Archean
  basement, on the strength of published Archean outcrops in the Mill
  Creek area and of the Mill Creek–Stillwater fault zone starting at the
  Mill Creek/Yellowstone confluence near Pray. Not mapped.
- **The Castle Mountains' intrusive rock** — no composition and no age
  found. "Granite" is a field term here and may be syenite, monzonite or
  diorite. The Castles' limestone is also unassigned — the range sits
  where both Belt-age and Mississippian carbonates are plausible.
- **Wolf Butte** — the laccolith interpretation is an inference from a
  general statement about central Montana's limestone buttes; the
  reported "granite higher up Wolf Butte" is uncorroborated.
- **Bear Canyon's sandstone** — one source, no formation, no
  corroboration. If it is real it changes the wet-rock advice for the
  Bozeman Pass crags.
- **Rocky Canyon (Morgan Cemetery)'s unit** — the friability is
  reported; the assignment to Lodgepole or a Cambrian interval is
  inference from the Bridger section.
- **Sheep Mountain's and Blue Cloud's specific plutons** — the Boulder
  Batholith has 7–14 of them and no source names which.
- **Two age discrepancies carried forward rather than resolved:** the
  Spanish Peaks/Beehive gneiss at **1.6 Ga** versus the Gallatin
  basement at **2.7–3.8 Ga**; and Boulder Batholith quartz monzonite
  quoted at **69–87 Ma** in one popular source against the
  peer-reviewed **81.7–73.7 Ma**.
- **Memorial Falls as a rock-climbing area** — the *rock* is now known
  (Neihart Formation quartzitic sandstone); the *climbing* is still
  undocumented at route level.
- **Pattee Canyon / Rattlesnake** — still not confirmed as a documented
  climbing area. The Belt bedrock assignment is inference from county
  geologic-unit listings.
- **Sinbad's Fortress** — still not found.
- **Montana-specific raptor closure lists** — not found in this pass.
- **Kings Hill / Little Belts and Sixteen Mile as climbing areas** —
  still nothing; the White Sulphur Springs research file's conclusion
  stands. **Sluice Boxes State Park** is the one Little Belt location
  with obviously climbable Madison limestone, and no route record
  surfaced for it either.
