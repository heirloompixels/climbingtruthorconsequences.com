## Summary

Tres Piedras ("Three Rocks") is a cluster of Precambrian granite
(technically orthogneiss) domes on the southeastern edge of the Tusas
Mountains, about 30 miles / 20–30 minutes west-northwest of Taos, New
Mexico, just off US 64 near its junction with US 285. Sitting at roughly
8,000 feet, it's one of northern New Mexico's most approachable trad
areas: friction slabs, splitter and flake cracks, and some bolted faces
on domes up to ~200 feet tall, split across a half-dozen or so named
rock formations (North, Middle, South, West, Far, and Mosaic
Rock/Mosaic Rock North), with grades reported from 5.4 to 5.13 and a
strong bouldering scene alongside the roped climbing. It sits on Carson
National Forest land (Tres Piedras Ranger District), with a small
private-land corner in the southwest that the landowner has historically
tolerated climbing on. The two standing print references are Jay
Foley's *Taos Rock* (2005) and Dennis Jackson's *Rock Climbing New
Mexico*, both said to cover the area in essentially the same routes and
photos.

## Location & Approach

- Tres Piedras sits at the junction of US 285 and US 64 in Taos County,
  northern New Mexico, adjacent to Carson National Forest, at roughly
  30 miles (about 20–30 minutes' drive) west/northwest of the town of
  Taos.
- **From Taos:** drive west ~30 miles on US 64, crossing the Rio Grande
  Gorge Bridge and passing the Greater World Earthship Community. At the
  junction with US 285, continue west on US 64 for about 0.75 mile to a
  large water tower; turn right onto the dirt road and follow it about
  0.5 mile to the trailhead/parking.
- A near-identical description from the other direction: from the
  US 285/US 64 junction in the village, go west on US 64 about 0.7 mile
  (passing the Tres Piedras Ranger Station) to an unmarked dirt road on
  the right, marked by the green water tank. Go through the barbed-wire
  gate (close it behind you), follow the road about 0.5 mile to the
  parking area (left at the "T," left at the "Y"), then walk in through
  a gateway to reach the rocks.
- Elevation of the town is reported as roughly 8,081–8,123 feet;
  the crags themselves are at similar high-desert elevation (~8,000 ft),
  unconfirmed to the foot for any individual formation.

## Rock & Style

- **Rock type:** granite — more precisely, the Tres Piedras Orthogneiss,
  a Precambrian pluton in northern New Mexico with a radiometric age of
  about 1,693 million years, per Wikipedia's geology entry. The crags sit
  on the extreme southeastern fringe of the Tusas Mountains, where that
  range flattens out into the Rio Grande Valley.
- **Formation count:** sources disagree on exactly how to count the
  rocks — one description says "six major rock formations," another
  says "four or five clusters of granite outcroppings." Named
  Mountain-Project sub-areas turned up in search results include North
  Rock, Middle Rock, South Rock, West Rock (split into West Rock One,
  next to Mosaic Wall, and West Rock Two, next to the Sundeck Wall and
  North Rock), Far Rock, Mosaic Rock, and Mosaic Rock North — so "six"
  roughly matches North/Middle/South/West/Far/Mosaic, with Mosaic Rock
  North as a further split. Approximate per-formation route counts
  surfaced in one search summary (unconfirmed against a live MP page):
  Mosaic Rock 29, West Rock 20, Mosaic Rock North 19, South Rock 15,
  Middle Rock 11, North Rock 8, Far Rock 5.
- **Style:** described consistently as quality granite slabs, cracks,
  and steeper faces — trad-protected crack and slab climbing is the
  core of the area, with some bolted/mixed slab and face routes,
  especially on Mosaic Rock's south face (its tallest wall at ~200 ft,
  and reportedly the highest concentration of routes and the most
  popular formation). Both single-pitch and multi-pitch lines exist;
  domes reach up to about 200 feet.
- **Bouldering:** described as excellent and heavily used — "not
  uncommon to see as many crashpads as ropes." A dedicated Mountain
  Project sub-area, "Tres Piedras Bouldering," exists separately from
  the roped-climbing area page. See also the *Rocky Mountain Raider*
  blog post "Tres Piedras Bouldering: Granite Problems in Northern New
  Mexico" for a boulderer's account (content not independently fetched
  here — title/URL only, from search results).
- **Difficulty range:** 5.4 to 5.13 across the whole area, per SummitPost
  material surfaced in search.
- North Rock is described as the place to find solitude when the rest of
  the crag is crowded, approachable from the west (past "Aspen Alley"),
  over the saddle between Middle and West Rocks (past the route
  "Alien"), or by circling around from the east.

## Season

- Multiple sources describe Tres Piedras as often best fall through
  spring, with summer capable of getting hot on exposed faces —
  consistent with its high-desert setting.
- One source frames it geographically: Tres Piedras sits in the dry part
  of the San Luis Valley corridor, between the wetter Southern San Juans
  and Southern Sangre de Cristo ranges, which lets boulderers climb here
  through much of the winter on dry spells.
- At ~8,000 ft, expect cool mornings and the possibility of afternoon
  thunderstorms in summer monsoon season (this last point is general
  New-Mexico-high-desert knowledge, not confirmed by a Tres-Piedras-
  specific source, and should be checked against the guidebooks).

## Camping & Logistics

- Dispersed camping is allowed on the surrounding Carson National Forest
  land. One route to it: coming from the south on US 285, turn west onto
  US 64 and go a couple of miles to Forest Road 64G on the right for
  dispersed sites.
- The nearest developed/established campground cited is the USFS
  campground at Hopewell Lake, about 20 miles west on US 64.
- A Mountain Project forum thread, "Tres Piedras – Camping nearby?",
  exists and likely has current beta but was not fetchable in this
  session — see Sources.
- Campendium and other camping-info sites list a "Tres Piedras Dispersed
  Camping" area near the village — see Sources for the link; details not
  independently confirmed here.

## Access

- Land manager: **Carson National Forest**, Tres Piedras Ranger
  District. Ranger station address surfaced in search: 22280 US
  Highway 64, Tres Piedras, NM 87577, phone (575) 758-8678 — worth a
  direct call before a trip to confirm current conditions.
- A **southwest corner of the crag area sits on private land**; per
  search-result summaries, the landowner has historically not forbidden
  climbing there but has asked that visitors sign a waiver before
  climbing on that portion. Confirm current status before relying on
  this — private-land access arrangements can change without notice.
- **Raptor closures:** nesting-season sensitivity is called out for
  roughly mid-February through late May, with awareness recommended
  through the end of August. The Forest Service is described as wanting
  to preserve climbing access while protecting nesting raptors, relying
  on climbers to self-regulate — check Mountain Project's area page and
  any posted closure notices for which specific formations/routes are
  affected in a given season.
- **Current alerts (time-sensitive, verify before a trip):** the Carson
  National Forest's own site lists a "Beehive Fire Road Closures (Tres
  Piedras Ranger District)" alert and general wildfire/fire-restriction
  alerts. These are exactly the kind of season-dependent notice that
  should be re-checked at fs.usda.gov immediately before any visit
  rather than trusted from this write-up.

## History

- Climbing at Tres Piedras appears to date to at least the 1970s: an
  "aging, incomplete guide" credited to Bill Johnson, John Joline, Cayce
  Weber, and Lee Sheftel is hosted on the LA Mountaineers site
  (lamountaineers.org), implying development activity there that decade
  — this predates any published guidebook. (The LA Mountaineers route
  guide page itself could not be fetched in this session; see Sources.)
- **Jay Foley** — a Taos-area climbing guide based out of Arroyo Seco,
  head of Mountain Skills Rock Guides, and described as having lived and
  climbed in the Taos area for 25+ years and as "an avid pioneer in the
  Taos rock climbing scene" — wrote and published *Taos Rock: Climbs &
  Boulders of Northern New Mexico* in 2005 through Sharp End Publishing.
  This is repeatedly described as the area's first proper guidebook.
- **Dennis Jackson**, a professional guide and outdoor educator based in
  Taos, is the author of *Rock Climbing New Mexico* (Globe Pequot /
  FalconGuides, State Rock Climbing Series), which also covers Tres
  Piedras alongside other New Mexico areas such as Cochiti Mesa and the
  Enchanted Tower.
- One source (SummitPost, via search snippet) describes the two
  guidebooks as "excellent and virtually identical as far as route
  descriptions and photos" for the Tres Piedras chapter specifically.
- Tres Piedras is repeatedly contrasted with Questa Dome — "not as
  dramatic as Questa Dome to the east" — but noted as more accessible
  and, despite that, still comparatively obscure, with all of its major
  rocks carrying several established routes.
- No individual first-ascent stories or specific FA parties for
  particular Tres Piedras routes turned up in the available search
  results; this would need the guidebooks or a direct, fetchable
  Mountain Project/theCrag page to fill in and should not be treated as
  covered by this file.

## Nearby Climbing Between Taos and the Colorado Border

*(Brief, as requested — not exhaustively verified.)*

- **Questa Dome (Latir Wilderness, Carson National Forest):** the
  centerpiece of multi-pitch trad climbing in the Taos area, located
  north of Taos toward Questa/the Colorado border. A ~500-foot white
  granite dome with routes of roughly 4–5 pitches, grades in the
  5.10–5.12 range, following cracks and seams linked by slab and
  thin-face sections, with an intimidating roof around the 350-foot
  level on the center routes. *Question of Balance* is called out as
  possibly the best trad route in the state. Season is roughly
  May–October; approach is via a forest trail alongside a creek, about
  45 minutes to the base. See
  [Mountain Project's Questa Dome area](https://www.mountainproject.com/area/105979184/questa-dome)
  and [visitquesta.com/climbing](https://visitquesta.com/climbing/).
- **Rio Grande del Norte National Monument / Wild Rivers Recreation
  Area (Big Arsenic / Little Arsenic):** BLM land along the Rio Grande
  and Red River gorges north of Taos toward the Colorado border. Search
  results describe this area primarily as a hiking and scenic
  destination (steep switchback trails down into the gorge, petroglyphs,
  a $3 day-use fee at Wild Rivers) rather than a documented technical
  climbing area — no confirmed route data surfaced. It's mentioned in
  passing in general Taos-climbing marketing copy as offering "splitter
  cracks and bolted basalt faces" in the Rio Grande del Norte high
  desert, but this reads as broad regional flavor text rather than a
  specific, verifiable crag; treat as unconfirmed and worth an on-the-
  ground check rather than a citation.
- **Comales Canyon** turned up in the same search results but is
  **south** of Taos (about 18 miles via NM-518, near Sipapu Ski Resort),
  not between Taos and the Colorado border, so it's noted here only to
  flag that it should *not* be filed under this section despite
  appearing in the same search results — it has bolted sport climbing
  and some moderate trad next to a creek ("Water Wall" and "Fire Wall"),
  per search snippets, but belongs in a different corridor of the
  guidebook.
