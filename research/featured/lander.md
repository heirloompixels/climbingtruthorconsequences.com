# Lander, Wyoming

## Summary

Lander is a small city (roughly 7,500–8,000 people) in west-central Wyoming, wedged against the southern end of the Wind River Range and bordering the Wind River Indian Reservation. It is the base town for two distinct, closely spaced climbing areas — Sinks Canyon (sandstone, limestone/dolomite, and granite in one drainage just outside town) and Wild Iris (a high-altitude dolomite sport-climbing complex about 30 minutes southwest) — plus a scatter of newer satellite crags (Fossil Hill, Sweat Lodge, Wolf Point) documented in the current Bechtel guidebook. Lander's climbing identity was largely built by Todd Skinner, who moved to town in 1989–1990, helped develop Wild Iris, opened Wild Iris Mountain Sports with Amy Skinner in 1990, and co-founded the International Climbers' Festival in the early 1990s — an event still running today and now one of the longest-continuously-running climbers' festivals in the world. Lander is also the international headquarters of NOLS (the National Outdoor Leadership School), giving the town an outdoor-education identity that runs alongside its climbing scene.

## Location & Approach

Lander sits in west-central Wyoming at the southern edge of the Wind River Range, bordering the Wind River Indian Reservation to the north. The climbing splits into two main zones:

- **Sinks Canyon** — begins essentially at the edge of town and runs southwest up into the Wind River foothills along Sinks Canyon Road (WY-131), partly within Sinks Canyon State Park.
- **Wild Iris** — roughly 24 miles southwest of Lander via Highway 28, near South Pass, at about 9,000 feet elevation (locally called Limestone Mountain); approaches to individual walls run from roadside to about a 30-minute uphill walk. Wolf Point, a satellite cliff off the Wild Iris road, requires a "long bumpy drive" plus a steep ~1-hour hike (roughly 1,500 ft down and 1,000 ft back up).
- Newer/secondary areas — **Fossil Hill**, the **Sweat Lodge** (Little Popo Agie Canyon), and others documented in the current guidebook sit at various points along these same access corridors.

## Rock & Style

- **Sinks Canyon** has three sub-zones by elevation/rock type: the **lower canyon** is desert-like sandstone suited to crack climbing; the **middle canyon** is limestone (locally also described as dolomitic, per the current guidebook's coverage of "dolomite, sandstone, and granite") with steep, bolted face climbs; the **upper canyon** is granite offering crack, face, and slab climbing from beginner through expert level. Trad climbing happens on the granite and sandstone; sport climbing and bouldering happen across all three rock types.
- **Wild Iris** is white dolomite (often described casually as "limestone"), part of the broader Madison Group carbonate formation that runs along the Wind River Range and becomes progressively more dolomitic to the southeast. It offers 100+ routes from 5.4 to 5.14d across many separate walls, known for bulletproof rock, pockets, edges, and angles from slab to horizontal roof; described as approachable but "best suited for moderate and advanced climbers." Many Wild Iris walls face south/get significant sun.
- **Wolf Point**, a satellite cliff, is steep, cave-like terrain with routes commonly 30–40+ meters and concentrated in the 5.13–5.14 range, plus some 5.10c and up.
- **Sweat Lodge**, in the Little Popo Agie canyon, is formed by a huge dolomite flake fallen against the main cliff, forming a shaded "A-frame" cave; only ~15 routes, starting around 5.12c and getting harder, with pumpy pocket-pulling and crimping.
- **Fossil Hill** is a ridge-side crag with 70–80 routes, described as less ideal for true beginners but with plenty of 5.10s.

## Season

- **Wild Iris** sits at roughly 9,000 feet and is treated as a late-spring-through-fall (roughly May–October) crag: the season starts once snow melts in early June and runs into late fall. High altitude keeps summer days pleasant but changeable — wind and cold storms can hit any time in summer — and many south-facing walls get "toasty" at midday, so climbers plan around chasing shade.
- **Sinks Canyon**, lower and closer to town, is climbable earlier and later in the season than Wild Iris; within the canyon, individual walls have their own microclimates by sub-canyon and aspect (e.g., the Scud Wall area is a popular, sunny, crowded sub-5.10-and-under zone; the Wilds sector faces southwest and gets morning shade with lots of afternoon sun).
- **Wolf Point** and **Fossil Hill** are both cited with a similar May–October season window.
- Combined, the area's marketing claims roughly 320 climbable days a year town-wide, crediting the combination of sun-exposed Sinks Canyon rock with the cooler, higher-elevation Wild Iris limestone/dolomite as complementary options through the year.

## Camping & Logistics

- **Lander City Park** — free camping in town for up to 3 nights, at 4th and Fremont streets on the Popo Agie River; 28 tent sites and 7 RV sites (first come, first served, no hookups, no showers); restrooms, drinking water, tables/fire grates, and shade, walking distance to downtown restaurants and bars. Very popular with climbers as a free, central base. [The Dyrt, "Lander City Park Camping"](https://thedyrt.com/camping/wyoming/lander-city-park); [Wind River Country, "Lander City Park Public Campground"](https://windriver.org/venue/lander_city_park_public_campground-10/)
- **Wild Iris Campground** (Shoshone National Forest) — 12 camping/dispersed sites near the crag with bear boxes, fire rings, a hardened parking area, and picnic tables; no potable water. As of July 2024 the Forest Service began charging a $10/night fee (previously free); first come, first served, 16-day stay limit, services generally available mid-May through mid-September. Food storage in the bear boxes is required. [USFS, Shoshone National Forest, "Wild Iris Campground"](https://www.fs.usda.gov/r02/shoshone/recreation/wild-iris-campground); [Jackson Hole News&Guide, "Camping at Wild Iris may no longer be free"](https://www.jhnewsandguide.com/news/environmental/local/camping-at-wild-iris-may-no-longer-be-free/article_4f6b7733-cdb5-51cf-9db0-f0b5ca93e8c0.html)
- **OK Corral** dispersed camping — additional dispersed camping near the Wild Iris/OK Corral sector. [The Dyrt, "Wild Iris OK Corral Camping"](https://thedyrt.com/camping/wyoming/wild-iris-ok-corral)
- **Aspen Glade Campground** (Shoshone National Forest) — another developed option along the same access road toward Wild Iris/South Pass. [USFS, Shoshone National Forest, "Aspen Glade Campground"](https://www.fs.usda.gov/r02/shoshone/recreation/aspen-glade-campground)
- Wild Iris Mountain Sports (166 Main Street, Lander) functions as the town's climbing-shop hub for gear, beta, and current area/closure conditions.

## Access & Ethics

- **Wind River Indian Reservation** — home to the Eastern Shoshone and Northern Arapaho tribes, borders Lander to the north; the reservation's southern boundary was itself set by the 1874 "Lander Purchase," when the Eastern Shoshone ceded land south of the 43rd parallel. Climbing areas covered here (Sinks Canyon, Wild Iris, Fossil Hill, Sweat Lodge, Wolf Point) are on Shoshone National Forest, BLM, and Sinks Canyon State Park land rather than on the reservation itself, but the reservation is immediately adjacent and central to the region's history. [Wikipedia, "Wind River Indian Reservation"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_River_Indian_Reservation); [History Jackson Hole, "An Introduction to the Wind River Indian Reservation of Wyoming"](https://jacksonholehistory.org/learn/archives-research/an-introduction-to-the-wind-river-indian-reservation-of-wyoming/)
- **Raptor closures at Fossil Hill** — a voluntary seasonal closure runs (as posted for the current season) roughly June 9 through August 15, covering all routes from Barber Paradox (open) west approximately 200 meters, including Milk Bone, plus a ban on further route development in the closure zone or on the Fossil Logic Wall during that window. A separate closure in the Fossil Hill West area covers all routes between Burly But Sensitive and Sound of Silence. Exact dates are posted annually by Wild Iris Mountain Sports and should be checked before a Fossil Hill trip. [Wild Iris Mountain Sports, "Area Conditions"](https://www.wildirisclimbing.com/area-conditions)
- **Local ethic at Wild Iris** — Access Fund's local-etiquette guide notes Wild Iris is rarely crowded and that avoiding crowds is itself part of the local ethic: if a route is occupied, the norm is to move to a quieter wall (the Lower Remuda is cited as an example) rather than lining up, and locals dislike climbers monopolizing a route as a "project" if they can't get past the second or third bolt. [Access Fund, "Climb Like a Local: Wild Iris"](https://www.accessfund.org/latest-news/open-gate-blog/climb-like-a-local-wild-iris)
- **Sinks Canyon State Park** manages part of the canyon jointly with the Shoshone National Forest and the Sinks Canyon Natural Resource Council, which maintains climbing-specific trail and access information. [Sinks Canyon Natural Resource Council, "Climb"](https://www.sinkscanyonstatepark.org/activities/climb/trail.html)
- Camping fees at the Wild Iris Campground (see above) are a recent (2024) access-cost change worth flagging to visitors expecting free dispersed camping.

## History

Climbers were on Sinks Canyon's rock earlier, but Lander's modern identity as a climbing destination dates to 1989–1990, when Todd Skinner and Amy Whistler (later Amy Skinner) moved to town after hearing about the dolomite cliffs nearby and began developing what became Wild Iris. Skinner — already one of the era's leading big-wall and free climbers — brought both his own route-development energy and a wider community of climbing partners to Lander; he is credited with discovering dozens of routes in the area before his death in a rappelling accident on Yosemite's Leaning Tower in 2006. To support themselves and fellow climbers, Todd and Amy opened **Wild Iris Mountain Sports** in 1990, first in a small house at Third and Lincoln, later moving to larger Main Street locations (1991, 1998, and 2013) as the shop and town grew around the sport. In the early 1990s (most secondary sources describing a "30th anniversary" in 2023 point to 1993, though at least one source cites 1994) Skinner and friends founded the **International Climbers' Festival** to celebrate the local community and draw outside climbers to the newly developed crags — it is now billed as the longest-running climbers' festival in the United States. Separately, Lander has been the international headquarters of **NOLS** (the National Outdoor Leadership School) since the school's founding in 1965, giving the town a long-running outdoor-education identity that predates and runs alongside its climbing reputation. In recent years development has continued to expand outward from Wild Iris and Sinks Canyon into satellite areas such as Fossil Hill, the Sweat Lodge (discovered/developed starting in 2010), and further Sinks Canyon sub-sectors, as reflected in the growing route count of the Bechtel guidebook series (roughly 1,700+ routes area-wide as of the 2023 edition).

## Sectors (Major Crags)

- **Sinks Canyon** — three rock-type sub-zones (sandstone lower canyon, limestone/dolomite middle canyon, granite upper canyon). Named sub-sectors identified this session include **Killer Cave** (steep pocketed limestone cave with curtain walls of easier vertical lines flanking harder cave routes up to 5.14a), **Scud Wall** (popular, crowded, mostly 5.10-and-under), **The Wilds & Achin' For Booty** (the leftmost wall of the main Sinks limestone cliff, SW-facing), **The Joint** (a complex, multi-tiered series of walls near the start of the Waterfall Trail), **The ZoZone** (home to Frogger and Zozo's Dance), **Hardware Wall**, **Grade II Wall**, **Aspen Grade Wall**, and **Fairfield Hill**.
- **Wild Iris** — the largest single zone, organized around **the Main Wall** (the original, still-central sector, 5.4–5.14d), **OK Corral** (includes La Vaca/Right Side, Claim Jumper, Pronghorn Pinnacle), **Rode Hard Wall** (vertical faces and big roofs, includes the popular moderate Easy Ridin'), **The Erratic** (a clean white face sector reached via the Aspen Glades fork), **Wapiti Wall**, **Five Ten Wall**, **Lower Remuda**, and **Wild Horses Wall / Hot Tamale Wall** (crowd-pleasing crags).
- **Wolf Point** — a separate, remote satellite cliff off the Wild Iris road; steep, cave-like, mostly hard (5.10c–5.15a) with a serious approach (roughly 1,500 ft down / 1,000 ft back up on a ~1-hour hike each way).
- **Fossil Hill** — ridge-side crag divided into **Fossil Hill Central**, **Fossil Hill West**, and **The Hideout**; 70–80 routes, largely mid-to-upper 5.10 and harder, subject to seasonal raptor closures (see Access & Ethics).
- **Sweat Lodge** — small, shaded dolomite-flake cave in the Little Popo Agie canyon; ~15 routes, all 5.12c and harder.
