# Colorado Rock Climbing Area Census

## Research method and limitations

This census was compiled for a guidebook appendix using web search against
Mountain Project, theCrag, guidebook-publisher catalogs, and land-manager
pages. Two constraints shaped what could be verified directly in this
session:

1. **Direct page fetches to `mountainproject.com`, `thecrag.com`, and most
   other outside domains were blocked by this environment's network egress
   proxy.** All Mountain Project data below therefore comes from search-engine
   result snippets (titles, URLs, and summarized page text), not from loading
   the pages directly. Route counts pulled this way should be treated as
   approximate and current as of whenever Mountain Project's index was last
   crawled by the search engine, not as a live count.
2. **The session's web-search budget (200 queries) was exhausted before every
   area on the target list could be individually queried.** Areas with a
   search result cited in the Sources section below are marked verified for
   this session. Areas without a citation are included because they are
   well-established, widely documented Colorado climbing destinations, but
   they are marked **[unverified this session]** and should be re-confirmed
   against Mountain Project before publication. No route counts, grades, or
   other figures were invented for these entries — where a figure is not
   confidently known it is left blank or described qualitatively.

Nothing below should be read as a live Mountain Project export. Treat route
counts as rough scale indicators ("dozens" vs. "over a thousand"), not exact
figures.

## Colorado's climbing character

Colorado is one of the two or three densest climbing states in the country —
one widely cited count puts the state at roughly 39,000 documented routes
across its area tree — and it earns that density from geological range as
much as sheer volume. The Front Range stacks Boulder's gritty Fountain
sandstone slabs, Eldorado Canyon's runout traditional classics, and the
Flatirons' alpine-scale slab formations within a half-hour of a million
people, then backs them with the granite domes of the South Platte drainage
and the volcanic porphyry of the Golden mesas. Push west over the Divide and
the rock turns to limestone at Rifle and Shelf Road — two of the country's
best sport-climbing destinations, one absurdly steep and technical, the other
moderate and forgiving — and to Wingate and Navajo sandstone towers at
Colorado National Monument and in the canyons of the West End near Naturita
and Paradox Valley. The San Juans in the southwest mix alpine granite (Ouray),
volcanic tuff, and pocketed sport rock (Telluride) with a still-developing
sandstone scene around Durango. The San Luis Valley anchors the south with
welded-tuff sport climbing at Penitente Canyon and the state's only Grade VI
big-wall terrain in the Black Canyon of the Gunnison. Season and elevation do
a lot of the organizing work here: low-desert sandstone and limestone crags
run best fall through spring, while the high granite of Independence Pass,
Guanella Pass, and Mount Evans is a short subalpine summer season squeezed
between snow.

Access is a live, moving target almost everywhere in the state. Front Range
crags on Jefferson County Open Space, Boulder OSMP, Colorado State Parks, and
Roosevelt/Arapaho National Forest land carry seasonal raptor-nesting closures
(typically February–July) that shut specific walls, not whole canyons, and
the closed list changes year to year with where the birds actually nest.
Royal Gorge itself is closed to unauthorized climbing under Cañon City
municipal code. Garden of the Gods requires free climber registration with
Colorado Springs Parks and Recreation and bans chalk outright. Land managers
worth tracking by area: Colorado State Parks (Eldorado Canyon, Staunton,
Mueller), Boulder OSMP and Jefferson County Open Space (Boulder Canyon,
Flatirons, Clear Creek Canyon, Golden), USFS Arapaho/Roosevelt and Pike/San
Isabel National Forests (South Platte, Poudre Canyon, Independence Pass,
Buena Vista), BLM (Shelf Road, Penitente Canyon, Unaweep, the West End), and
NPS (Rocky Mountain NP, Black Canyon of the Gunnison NP, Colorado National
Monument).

## Area count

Counting each row once (Shelf Road, Clear Creek Canyon Bouldering, and
Rotary Park appear in two tables and are counted only in their primary
table), this census lists **55 named areas/sub-areas**: 24 in the Front
Range, 7 in the Central Mountains, 6 on the Western Slope, 6 in the
Southwest, 8 in the South/San Luis Valley, and 4 additional cross-region
bouldering areas. Roughly two-thirds carry a direct citation from this
session's research; the remainder are flagged **[unverified this session]**
and should be individually confirmed on Mountain Project — with a working
direct connection to the site — before this census is treated as final
guidebook copy.
