# Montana Rock Climbing Areas: A Census

Montana's climbing is scattered, low-key, and geologically all over the
map — there is no single signature crag the way Colorado has Eldorado or
Utah has Indian Creek. Instead the state offers pockets of very good
rock tucked into national forest canyons within an hour of its handful
of college and mining towns: Gallatin limestone and gneiss outside
Bozeman, granite spires south of Butte, Big Belt limestone east of
Helena, Bitterroot granite south of Missoula, and a long band of
quartzite up near the Canadian border. Winters are long enough, and the
water reliable enough, that Bozeman's Hyalite Canyon has become one of
the most concentrated natural ice climbing venues in the Lower 48 —
arguably Montana's single most famous piece of vertical terrain, rock or
ice. Most areas are managed by the Forest Service or BLM with informal,
volunteer-driven access (local climbers' coalitions in Helena, the
Bitterroot, and the northwest corner do much of the bolt maintenance and
landowner diplomacy); a few marquee areas — Kootenai Canyon, Allenspur —
have gone through real access fights with private land. Documentation is
thin and scattered compared to climbing-first states: Mountain Project's
Montana pages are explicitly incomplete by local consensus, several
areas exist mainly in a single small-press guidebook or a climbers'-
coalition web page, and a number of named spots turned up in searches
without enough independent confirmation to include here. Where that is
the case below, the entry is marked **[unverified]** rather than
invented.

Grades below are the range of documented free-climbing difficulty at
each area, not a promise of coverage at every grade in between. Route
counts are approximate and drawn from Mountain Project area pages,
guidebook marketing copy, or press coverage — treat them as rough
orders of magnitude, not audited totals.

## Bozeman area

Southwest Montana's climbing center, built around Gallatin Canyon,
Hyalite Canyon, and the Bridger Range, with Montana State University and
a dense outdoor-industry scene sustaining it. Hyalite's winter ice
season is a genuine international draw and anchors the annual Bozeman
Ice Festival.

## Butte area

Historic mining town with easy interstate access to granite spire
climbing and a large, informal bouldering field.

| Area | Nearest town | Rock | Style | ~Routes | Grades | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spire Rock (Pipestone Pass) | Butte | Granite | Trad, some multi-pitch, TR | Dozens | 5.4–5.10 | On I-90 near Homestake/Pipestone Pass east of Butte; "King" and "Queen" formations give crack, offwidth, and easy top-rope setups. Long-time local favorite. |

## Helena area

Big Belt Mountains limestone and Elkhorn-range granite within a short
drive of the state capital; access and bolting maintained largely by the
Helena Climbers' Coalition.

| Area | Nearest town | Rock | Style | ~Routes | Grades | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| Avalanche Gulch | Townsend / Helena | Limestone | Sport (mostly), some trad | Moderate | 5.6–5.11ish | Big Belt Mountains east of Canyon Ferry Lake; roadside, moderate grades, high-quality rock relative to its neighbor Hellgate. |

## Missoula area / Bitterroot Valley

Granite canyons cutting east out of the Bitterroot Range south of
Missoula; access here has been genuinely contested (private land at
canyon mouths), and the Bitterroot Climbers Coalition is the active
local steward.

## Red Lodge / East Rosebud / Beartooth Mountains

Gateway to the Beartooth Plateau and Montana's only real high-alpine
climbing, plus waterfall ice in the shoulder seasons.

| Area | Nearest town | Rock | Style | ~Routes | Grades | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Red Lodge area crags (incl. Rock Creek Falls, Silver Run Falls) | Red Lodge | Mixed | Sport/trad rock; waterfall ice in winter | Small–moderate | Beginner–moderate rock; ice varies | Local cragging and training venue for the bigger East Rosebud and Beartooth objectives. |
| East Rosebud | Roscoe / Red Lodge | Granite/gneiss (alpine) | Trad, multi-pitch, some aid | Dozens of long routes | 5.easy up to 20-pitch outings; some aid | Via MT-78 to Roscoe then East Rosebud Rd; walls on the East Rosebud Plateau offer some of the longest rock routes accessible in the state. |
| Beartooth Mountains / Granite Peak | Red Lodge | Granite/gneiss, alpine | Alpine mountaineering, scrambling, technical rock on some routes | — | Class 3–4 standard route; harder technical variations exist | Granite Peak (12,807 ft) is Montana's highest point, inside the Absaroka-Beartooth Wilderness (Custer Gallatin NF); commonly ranked the second-hardest US state highpoint after Denali for its exposure, routefinding, and weather. Standard approach via West Rosebud/Mystic Lake trailhead. |

## Guidebooks

- **Bozeman Rock Climbs**, 3rd edition (2009), Bill Dockins & Tom Kalakay, High Gravity Press. ~600 routes, 304 pp. Covers Gallatin Canyon, Hyalite Canyon, Bear Canyon, Chestnut Mountain, the Madison River area, the Bridger Range, and Big Sky. Successor to Dockins' earlier 1987 and 1995 editions.
- **Rock Climbs of Southwest Montana**, Kyle Vassilopoulos & Joe Josephson, Sharp End Publishing. ~700 routes, 25 areas including Bozeman Pass, Allenspur, Mill Creek, Bridger Range, Hyalite Canyon, Gallatin Canyon/Tower, Big Sky, and the Madison River. First local guide to cover the Boulder, Paradise, Gallatin, and Madison valleys together since the mid-1990s.
- **The House of Hyalite**, 2nd edition (2023), Joe Josephson. Ice and mixed climbing guide to Hyalite Canyon's four sub-canyons; 400+ routes in the 2nd edition (up from 300+ in the 2019 1st edition). Successor to Josephson's earlier *Winter Dance* (2004).
- **Central Montana Rock**, 2nd edition (2022), Jake Mergenthaler with the Helena Climbers' Coalition, Sharp End Publishing, 256 pp. Covers crags between Helena and the Rocky Mountain Front — granite trad and limestone sport, including Sheep Mountain, Avalanche/Hellgate Gulch, and Beaver Creek.
- **Rock Climbing Montana** (Falcon Guides state series), Randall Green. A statewide introduction covering 500+ recommended routes, including Missoula, Helena, Butte, and Bozeman local crags plus remote areas like Stone Hill and Granite Peak.
- **The Rock Climber's Guide to Montana** (1995), Randall Green with Brad Hutcheson (and contributions from Matthew Taylor, Rafael Grana, Dwight Bishop, Ron Brunckhorst, Brian Hatfield, Jason Taylor), Falcon Press. Earlier, separate statewide guide — verify which of the two Green titles is meant when a source just says "Randall Green's Montana guide," as they are distinct books.
- **Lost Horse Canyon: A Climber's Guide to Montana's Best Cragging and Bouldering** — dedicated guide to the Lost Horse Canyon area in the Bitterroot; author not confirmed in sources gathered. **[unverified author]**

### Research notes

Mountain Project itself was unreachable for direct page fetches in this
research session (network egress to mountainproject.com and thecrag.com
was blocked), so this census was compiled from Mountain Project/theCrag
content as surfaced through search-engine snippets and cached summaries,
cross-checked against SummitPost, local climbers'-coalition sites, guide
publishers, and regional news coverage. Mountain Project's own Montana
coverage is explicitly described by local sources as incomplete, so
absence from Mountain Project does not mean an area doesn't exist, and
several real areas (Bozeman Pass sub-crags, the Paradise Valley Mill
Creek, Memorial Falls rock climbing) could not be independently
corroborated beyond a single source in this pass — those are flagged
**[unverified]** in place rather than presented as confirmed. A second
pass with direct Mountain Project/theCrag access would be needed to
pin down exact route counts and grade spreads area-by-area, and to
confirm or drop the "Other areas found but not independently confirmed"
list.
